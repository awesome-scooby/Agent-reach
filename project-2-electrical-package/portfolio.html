Module 00 · Read this first

Claim integrity — what you may and may not say

Objective

Be able to describe this project honestly in one sentence, and name every activity in it that belongs to someone else — without hesitating.

Engineering hiring in Australia runs on trust. An interviewer who catches you overstating one thing will discount everything else you said, including the parts that were true. The fastest way to look senior as a graduate is to be precise about the edge of your own competence.

Never say

“I designed a 415 V MCC.” · “I delivered this package.” · “This was for a water utility client.”

Say this instead, early and unprompted It's a self-directed reference project — I built a complete MCC package from design basis through FAT and handover so I'd understand how the documents actually connect. It's not delivered work, and there are parts of it I'd escalate rather than sign.

What you can legitimately claim

  • Drawing literacy — reading an SLD, a control schematic, a termination drawing and an I/O list, and cross-checking them against each other.
  • Three-way reconciliation — SLD ↔ schematic ↔ BOM, and how to find what is missing.
  • Technical query discipline — what a TQ is for, how to write one, what happens if you don't raise it.
  • Change management — tracing a late change through every affected document and identifying cost, schedule and retest impact.
  • FAT preparation and coordination, punch-list discipline, MDR structure and handover.
  • Knowing what needs a specialist — which is itself a competency.

The question most graduates miss

“Does the MCC have to keep operating with the switchroom HVAC failed?”

If yes, the design ambient isn't 40 °C — a sealed switchroom with two 90 kW drives and no cooling climbs well past 50 °C, which derates the drives, the breakers and the busbar. If no, then HVAC failure must trip or ramp down the drives, which is a control requirement that has to appear on the schematics. Either answer changes the design. “Nobody asked” is how a board trips on over-temperature the first hot Saturday after handover.

Heat load

You are the only party who knows how much heat two 90 kW drives will dump into that switchroom. If you do not tell the HVAC designer early, in writing, with a number, nobody will ask — and the switchroom will be undercooled. Stage 2

Single line diagram — the power system argument

Objective

Be able to redraw this SLD from memory and defend why each device exists and what must be confirmed before it is finalised.

An SLD is not a picture of a switchboard. It is the power system argument, compressed onto one sheet, showing how energy gets from the source to every load and how a fault is cleared at every point along the way. Three people read it and want different things:

  • The protection engineer — can a fault at any point be cleared, and does the nearest device clear it before the one upstream?
  • The operator / maintainer — how do I isolate this load safely, and what else goes dark when I do?
  • The estimator and panel builder — how many cubicles, how much busbar, how many devices?

The four decisions to be able to defend

1 · Four-pole incomer with an electronic trip unit

Three separate jobs: isolation (a single lockable point to make the board dead), overcurrent protection for the busbar and assembly, and a defined upstream reference for discriminating with the feeders. Four-pole because switching the neutral means the board can be made genuinely dead, rather than leaving a neutral that can carry current from elsewhere. Electronic trip because adjustable long-time, short-time, instantaneous and earth-fault elements are what make discrimination achievable once the coordination study exists. A fixed thermal-magnetic breaker locks in a characteristic you cannot tune later.

2 · Full-size neutral

Know the physics, not just the rule

Third harmonic currents are zero-sequence — 120° apart at the fundamental is 3 × 120° = 360° at the third, so all three phases' third harmonics sit in phase with each other. Fundamental currents cancel in the neutral of a balanced system; triplens do not, they add arithmetically. A reduced-size neutral can then overheat while every phase reads comfortably within rating.

But the drives are not the source of the triplens. A balanced six-pulse rectifier produces characteristic harmonics of order 6k ± 1 — 5th, 7th, 11th, 13th — which are positive- and negative-sequence and do not add in the neutral. The triplens here come from the single-phase electronic load: the switch-mode power supplies, the UPS input, and the lighting and GPO circuits on DB-01. The drives are why the harmonic assessment was raised; the single-phase load is why the neutral is full size. Two different problems, two different sources.

The drafter's first revision had a half-size neutral, copied from a project template. Drawing templates carry other projects' assumptions — and so do half-remembered rules of thumb: the first version of this note blamed the drives for the triplens, which is wrong.

3 · VSD feeder breaker selected from the drive manual, not motor FLC

That breaker protects the cable and the drive input — it is not protecting the motor. The motor is protected by the drive's electronic thermal model plus an independent PTC thermistor relay. Many drives require a specific fuse or breaker type to achieve their declared short-circuit current rating; fitting the wrong device can invalidate the drive's SCCR. Note also that the drive's input current is not the same as the motor's full-load current.

Why the independent thermistor relay, when the drive has a thermal model

A drive's electronic overload is a model. It infers winding temperature from current, time and an assumed thermal time constant. It does not know the motor's cooling fan has failed, or that a TEFC motor running at low speed on a drive gets far less cooling than the model assumes. Embedded PTC thermistors measure the actual winding temperature. On a 90 kW pump motor with a long lead time, the extra relay is cheap insurance.

4 · Two kinds of screen earthing

CableScreen earthedWhy
Analogue instrument (4–20 mA)MCC end onlyElectrostatic shielding. Earthing both ends creates an earth loop; potential difference drives circulating current through the screen, coupling noise into the pair — exactly what you were preventing.
VSD motor cableBoth ends, 360°Low-impedance high-frequency return path for common-mode current, keeping it out of the building steel and nearby instrument cables. A pigtail instead of a 360° gland largely defeats it — a pigtail is an inductor at those frequencies.

How the SLD is checked before it is issued

The PE does not draft the SLD. The PE checks it, against a written list. Thirteen checks; these five find the most:

  1. Every load has a feeder and every feeder has a load — catches leftovers from a previous revision.
  2. Ratings are self-consistent up the chain: feeder device < busbar < incomer.
  3. Every device tag matches the schematics and the BOM.
  4. Every cable interface is a labelled terminal or link with a cable number.
  5. Assumptions annotated on the drawing face, not only in the design basis — the fabricator builds to the drawing and will never open the design basis.

Knowledge check

Summary

  • Every symbol on an SLD is a decision someone made and can be asked to defend.
  • The breaker on a VSD feeder does not protect the motor.
  • Triplen harmonics add in the neutral. Know why, not just that.
  • If a client approves a drawing without being told what it assumes, you have not transferred the risk — you have hidden it. The transmittal note is what makes the approval meaningful.
Stage 3

Schematics — control philosophy into logic

Objective

Reason about failure modes, not just normal operation, and be able to draw the E-stop circuit and explain single-fault detection.

The SLD says what is connected and how a fault is cleared. The schematics say what makes it operate, what stops it, and what happens when something breaks. The rule worth quoting: anything that carries load current appears on the SLD; anything that carries a decision appears on the schematic.

Emergency Stop and Safety CircuitNFD-ELE-SCH-0040 Rev 1

Dual channel, monitored, safety relay driving Safe Torque Off on both drives and dropping the DOL contactor coil circuit.

Say this in an interview

Safe Torque Off removes torque. It does not isolate. The drive output — and therefore the motor terminals — may still be at a hazardous potential after an E-stop is pressed. STO is not an isolation method, it does not replace the lockable local isolator at the motor, and it does not satisfy an isolation procedure for working on the machine. The E-stop is there so you can stop the plant quickly, not so you can put your hands in it.

Why dual channel, and the test that proves it

A single-channel E-stop can be defeated by one welded contact or one shorted wire and nobody would know. Two independent channels, monitored by the safety relay for agreement and timing, mean a single fault is detected and the circuit goes to the safe state rather than failing silently.

FAT test M-93 — the one that gets skipped

Anyone can press an E-stop and watch the plant stop. Open one channel only and confirm the relay detects the discrepancy, goes to the safe state, and will not reset until the fault is cleared. That is what distinguishes a dual-channel safety circuit from two single-channel circuits in parallel.

Monitored manual reset means the relay checks the reset button's own behaviour: it acts on the falling edge (button released), so a reset button that is stuck pressed or shorted will not reset the circuit.

The two design decisions to be able to defend

Local mode enforces every permissive

The drafter's first revision had Local mode bypassing the dry-run and seal-water permissives — drawn that way because “that's how the last one was”. It was reversed, and the reason recorded so the next person doesn't reverse it back.

The principle

Local changes who gives the command, not what protects the machine. A Local mode that drops the dry-run and thermistor trips is how a 90 kW pump gets destroyed by a fitter who reasonably assumed it was protected. If a genuine bypass is required for testing, it must be a deliberate function — key-switched, alarmed, time-limited, and specified by the client.

Dry-run protection is hardwired as well as monitored

Three ways this could be implemented:

  1. PLC reads the level transmitter and stops the pump. Fails if the transmitter drifts, the PLC is in program-stop, or someone forces the tag during commissioning.
  2. An independent float read by the PLC. Better — independent sensor — but still depends on the PLC.
  3. An independent float hardwired into the drive's external-fault input, and also read by the PLC for alarming. Protects the asset even with the PLC stopped, and still tells SCADA what happened.

Option 3 is what is drawn. Cost: one extra float, one pair, one drive input. Protection that matters shouldn't depend solely on software that someone can force.

Control Power Distribution — 240 V AC and 24 V DCNFD-ELE-SCH-0030 Rev 1

Redundant power supplies, decoupled through an O-ring module, then divided into six separately protected groups.

Why grouped DC protection, not one device

If everything sits behind a single 20 A device, one shorted field cable pulls down the whole control system — including the PLC that was going to tell SCADA what happened. Grouping means a shorted field cable trips one group, the PLC stays up, and the alarm reaches the operator. The safety circuit has its own group so an ordinary control fault cannot disable it.

Note also: each PSU is sized for the full load, not half. Two 10 A supplies for a 15 A load is not redundancy.

Knowledge check

Summary

  • STO removes torque; it does not isolate. This single distinction is the highest-value thing in the package.
  • Dual channel is only dual channel if the single-fault detection is tested.
  • Local changes who commands, not what protects.
  • Fail-safe is achieved by wiring critical signals normally closed, not by trusting code.
Stage 4

Terminations — the most-used document after handover

Objective

Understand terminal numbering and segregation philosophy, and be able to run the four-way traceability check that proves a termination schedule is right.

Nobody opens the design basis again after handover. Everybody opens the termination schedule — the workshop wireman, the site electrician, the commissioning technician, and the maintainer at 3 a.m. in five years.

Two reasons, and the second matters more: safety (nobody working on a 24 V circuit should have their hand next to a 415 V terminal) and error prevention — if the only ELV terminals are on a different rail, it is physically harder to land a control wire in a power terminal. Good layout removes error modes; it does not just document them.

Small decisions with large consequences
  • Yellow terminals for the safety circuit, and nothing else. A distinct colour makes it obvious to the wireman, the commissioning tech and the auditor that this rail is not to be casually reworked.
  • Disconnect terminals on every analogue loop. Open the loop, inject a calibrated 4–20 mA, verify the whole chain to SCADA — without unwiring anything. A few dollars per terminal, hours saved at every future calibration.
  • Spares grouped, not distributed. One spare after every ten looks tidy and is nearly useless — you can never fit a new multicore's cores together.
  • Never re-use a terminal number. A deleted circuit's terminal becomes a spare and keeps its number. Re-using it means an old drawing and a new drawing disagree, and someone will eventually work from the old one.
  • Four spare yellow terminals so a future E-stop can be looped in properly, rather than someone improvising a connection in a junction box.

The traceability chain — what “checked” actually means

For every I/O point this chain must close:

field device tag → cable → core → terminal → internal wire → module + channel → PLC address → SCADA tag

If any link is missing or inconsistent, the point is not signed off. Build the required list from the schematics, not from the termination drawing — otherwise you are only checking the drafter against themselves.

Knowledge check

Summary

  • The termination schedule is the most-used document after handover. Get it right.
  • Instrument screens: one end. VSD motor cable screens: both ends, 360°. Different problems, different solutions.
  • Build the check list from the schematics, not from the drawing you're checking.
  • Four extra spare cores cost almost nothing. A second cable pull through an operating plant costs a variation and an access permit.
Stage 5

BOM and three-way reconciliation

Objective

Run the reconciliation in three directions and know which class of error each one catches — then handle a substitution correctly.

A BOM is not a shopping list. It is the point where the drawings become money and time. It is also the first place where a drawing error becomes expensive: a missing auxiliary contact block on a drawing costs nothing; the same omission in the BOM stops a wireman.

The three passes — and why one direction is not enough

PassWhat you doWhat it catches that the others can't
SLD → BOMWalk every device on the power drawing. Confirm a BOM line exists with the right rating and the right accessories — handle, aux contacts, trip unit, end cap.Missing purchases. A schematic-only device would not appear here.
Schematic → BOMWalk every sheet. Tally the contacts: how many does the schematic use on each device, how many does the part number actually supply? Check coil voltages against part numbers.Contact shortfall and coil mismatch. Power devices with no control function would not appear.
BOM → drawingsWalk every BOM line and find where it appears on a drawing.Duplicates, orphans, leftovers from a copied BOM. Neither forward pass can find something that shouldn't be there.
The most common BOM defect in the industry

Auxiliary contact shortfall. Contacts are free on a drawing and cost money in a catalogue. The designer uses what the logic needs; nobody tallies it against the device. The contactor arrives with two contacts and the schematic uses four. The wireman stops, an add-on block is sourced at short notice, and add-on blocks are exactly the kind of small item that is out of stock when you need it.

The countermeasure is the contact tally in Pass 2. It is boring, mechanical work that takes an hour at Rev B and costs days in the workshop.

Worked discrepancy — substitution without engineering approval

What happened. The specified drive came back at 16 weeks against a 12-week programme. A supplier offered an alternative manufacturer's drive, same kW and voltage, ex-stock, ~8 % cheaper. The buyer — under genuine and legitimate schedule pressure — prepared a PO. It came to the PE because the procurement procedure requires engineering sign-off on any deviation. Which is exactly why that gate exists.

“Same kW and voltage” is not equivalence. Six things were checked:

CheckFinding
Physical dimensions and mountingDifferent frame and mounting centres. GA, mounting plate and internal cable routing all change — and the GA was already released.
Cooling and airflowDifferent clearances and airflow direction. Cubicle thermal design needs reassessment — and thermal verification is the manufacturer's responsibility.
Safe Torque Off implementationDifferent STO terminal arrangement. The safety circuit drawing would need revision. Any change to a safety-related circuit is reviewed, not absorbed.
EtherNet/IP capabilityNative on the specified drive; on the alternative it needs an option card that was not in the quoted price. The comparison was not comparing the same thing.
Client brand standardTQ-001 still open. Water utilities commonly mandate drive brands across their network.
Whole-of-life costSpares holding, technician familiarity, configuration tooling, fleet commonality. An 8 % saving on two drives is negligible against a maintenance team carrying an orphan brand for twenty years.
Who decides what — the actual point of this example

Procurement identified the lead-time problem and sourced an alternative. Correct behaviour — procurement's job is to surface options, not assess technical equivalence.
The designer assessed the differences and confirmed which sheets would change.
The Project Engineer stopped the PO, compiled the comparison, and recognised this was not their decision to make — it affects the client's asset for its whole life and touches an open standards question. A formal deviation request was raised with a recommendation to reject.
The client decided. They rejected it — and in doing so finally supplied their electrical design standard, which closed TQ-001 and three other open assumptions.

Outcome: no substitution, no cost variation, and four weeks of float consumed — mitigated to about one week by re-sequencing the build so the drive cubicles were wired last. The recovery came from re-sequencing, not from compromising the design. That is the move that distinguishes a project engineer from an expediter.

Knowledge check

Summary

  • Three directions, because each catches a different class of error. One direction misses the most expensive ones.
  • Technical equivalence is not kW and voltage. Purchase price is not cost.
  • Knowing the limit of your own authority, and making the escalation easy to act on, is the skill being demonstrated.
  • Any drawing revision triggers a BOM re-reconciliation.
Stage 6

Procurement and engineering control

Objective

Describe the PE role accurately — who does what — and write a technical query that actually gets answered.

The thing most graduates get wrong

If you describe yourself as the person who selected every breaker, one of two things happens: they don't believe you, or they do and conclude you don't understand how an engineering organisation works.

The accurate description I own the engineering inputs, the interfaces and the decisions. The designer produces the design. Procurement buys it. The workshop builds it. The client approves what is theirs to approve. My job is that all of those line up, that nothing falls between them, and that when something changes, everyone who needs to know finds out.

How to write a TQ that gets answered

Most TQs get ignored because they are written as questions. Write them as decisions:

WeakStrong
“Please advise the fault level.”“We have assumed 25 kA for 1 s. Unless advised otherwise by [date], we will place breaker orders on this basis and any subsequent change will be a variation. Please confirm or provide the actual value.”
“What PLC do you want?”“We propose CompactLogix 5380. This determines module selection, your spares holding and your integrator's resource. Confirmation required by [date] to hold the programme.”

Three things every TQ needs: a stated assumption, a required-by date, and the consequence of not answering. A TQ without a consequence is a request; with one it is a decision point. And send them individually, to the right person — a ten-item TQ to a client PM gets forwarded to three people and answered by none.

Revision control — the rules that prevent real errors

  1. Nothing is built from a letter revision. Letters are preliminary/review/approval. Numbers are Issued for Construction.
  2. Every issue goes out on a transmittal. No transmittal, no issue.
  3. Every revision carries a cloud and a description. “General revision” is not a description.
  4. Superseded drawings are physically removed from the workshop. Not marked, not filed on top — removed.
  5. Every drawing revision triggers a BOM re-reconciliation and an I/O list check.
  6. The integrator gets the I/O list at the same revision as the board.
The transferable rule

You can draft ahead of vendor data, but you must not release ahead of it. The drive GA arrived in Week 10 showing a clearance requirement the cubicle layout didn't allow. Because the general arrangement had been held from fabrication release, the cost was a drawing revision. Had it been released, it would have been a fabricated cubicle requiring modification or replacement.

Knowledge check

Summary

  • A PE doesn't design every component — they make sure the right person does, it's checked, it's bought correctly, and the decision is recorded.
  • A TQ needs an assumption, a date and a consequence.
  • Read what the supplier actually quoted, not what you asked for. Line by line.
  • Draft ahead of vendor data. Never release ahead of it.
Stage 7

Fabrication and inspection

Objective

Know when to inspect, what to look for, and which defects are cheap now and expensive later.

The PE is not building and not supervising the wireman — a good wireman doesn't need supervising and will resent it. Three jobs: confirm the board being built is the board that was designed; catch the things that are cheap now and expensive later; and be available to answer questions fast, so the workshop never has to improvise.

Being reachable is an engineering control

A wireman who can't get an answer in an hour will make a sensible decision and carry on. It will usually be reasonable. It will not be documented. Six months later nobody knows why that link is there.

3~50 % wiring completeWiring practice, routing, ferruling, duct fill, terminal disciplineHighest value inspection

Inspection 3 is the one that pays. If the ferruling convention or duct routing is wrong, finding it at 50 % means correcting half a board and getting the other half right. Finding it at 100 % means correcting a whole board — or, more likely in practice, accepting it, which sets a precedent.

Checks that are genuinely safety items, not workmanship nits

Earth bonds through paint

Powder coat is an insulator. An earth bolted through paint is not an earth. Paint removed or serrated washers at every bonding point — and verified by measurement at FAT, not by eye. Two bonds were found made through powder coat at the 50 % inspection; every bonding point was then re-checked.

Swarf in the board

Metal swarf from drilling a gland plate, sitting on a busbar shroud, is a phase-to-phase fault waiting for the first vibration. Boards get vacuumed before energisation for a reason. This is not housekeeping.

The ten-second check nobody remembers

Read the revision in the wireman's hand

Walk the workshop and look at the drawing on the bench. Read the revision number. It takes ten seconds and it is one of the highest-value checks in the whole process. A board being wired from a superseded revision fails FAT in ways that take days to unpick.

Other findings worth knowing

  • PLC mounted directly above a drive — found at Inspection 2 and relocated. Heat rises; a PLC above a drive is a bad idea. Cheap then, a rewire later.
  • Ferrules unreadable in situ — a ferrule you must remove a wire to read is useless. Corrected at 50 % with half the board still to wire.
  • Manufacturer's drive clearances — ignoring them causes over-temperature trips on hot days, months after handover, when nobody remembers why.
  • Maintainability — can the PSU come out without unwiring the terminals? Can that MPCB be adjusted with the board against a wall? These are what a client's maintenance rep notices at FAT, and they generate the most heated punch-list arguments.
“Can I just…” is the most dangerous question in a workshop

Usually a sensible suggestion. Sometimes it changes a safety circuit, a segregation arrangement or a rating. Assess it properly, then either approve it formally or decline it with a reason. Every workshop deviation gets documented, no matter how small — a board full of undocumented “sensible decisions” cannot be maintained and its as-built is worthless.

Knowledge check

Summary

  • Inspect at four points. The 50 % wiring inspection is the one that pays.
  • An earth bolted through powder coat is not an earth.
  • Check the drawing revision on the bench. Ten seconds, enormous value.
  • Capture mark-ups as they happen. The as-built is a record, not a reconstruction.
Stage 8

Factory Acceptance Test

Objective

Understand what a FAT is for, how to prepare so it doesn't go wrong, and — most importantly — what to do when a test cannot be completed.

A FAT exists so that problems are found in a workshop, where they are cheap, rather than on site, where they are not. A defect found at FAT costs roughly a tenth of the same defect found at site, and a hundredth of the same defect found after handover. That is the entire business case, and the answer to “why is FAT worth three days?”

The most practical advice in the whole package

Never let the client witness the first energisation. Run the whole procedure internally first. Fix what you find. Then invite the client. A FAT is a demonstration, not an experiment.

And open the FAT by declaring what is not ready and why. A client told at 8 a.m. will accept it. The same client who discovers it themselves at 3 p.m. will question everything else.

The insulation resistance trap

Disconnect or bypass all electronic equipment before insulation testing — drives, PLC modules, power supplies, UPS, surge protective devices, power meter, electronic trip units. Applying a 500 V test to a circuit with an SPD connected will operate the SPD. Applying it to drive control terminals will damage them.

The disconnection list goes in the procedure, written before the day — not remembered on the day. And reconnection is a signed step, because a device left disconnected is a classic post-FAT defect.

Arguing about categories after a defect is found is a bad conversation.

Worked defects

FD-001 · Thermistor relay contact wired NO instead of NC — Category A

This is the serious kind because it tests as working under normal conditions and fails only when you need it. With the drive's external-fault input polarity set to suit, the pump would run normally and the motor over-temperature trip would never operate. A 90 kW motor would run to destruction with no protection from its own thermistors.

Why it was caught: because the point-to-point check is performed by someone other than the person who wired it. A self-check would very likely have repeated the same misreading. That independence requirement is not bureaucracy; it is the control that caught this.

FD-002 · Contactor supplied with a 240 V AC coil instead of 24 V DC — Category A

Physically near-identical; the difference is one suffix in the part number. Category A not just because the feeder wouldn't operate, but because someone might “solve” it by connecting it to the 240 V services supply — putting 240 V AC through ELV terminals, in the ELV duct, out to a field station designed for ELV.

Two lessons. Goods-inward inspection must check part numbers, not descriptions — “Contactor 9 A” on a docket matches both parts. And when you find one picking error, check the whole class: one further incorrect device was found, which would otherwise have gone to site.

FD-003 · Screen earth bar bonded at multiple points — Category B

One mounting screw fitted through to the mounting plate. No symptom in a workshop; on site it creates earth loops, and the symptom is noisy, drifting analogue readings — one of the hardest classes of fault to diagnose after commissioning. Found only because the procedure deliberately tests for it. Without test E-35 this board would have shipped, and the noise would have been blamed on the field cabling for months.

FD-004 · Alarm descriptions transposed at SCADA — Category B

The protection worked; the information was wrong. An operator told the seal water has failed will check the seal water, find it healthy, reset, and restart a pump with a hot motor. A misleading alarm is worse than no alarm — it directs effort in the wrong direction and erodes trust in every other alarm on the screen. Root cause: the integrator working from an earlier I/O list revision. The entire alarm section was re-run, not just the corrected points; two more errors were found.

FD-005 · Protection settings cannot be verified — a DEVIATION, not a defect

The defect that best demonstrates engineering integrity

The coordination study still wasn't available at FAT, so the adjustable settings couldn't be verified against any approved basis. The board is fully functional, but discrimination between the incomer and the feeders is unverified — a feeder fault might trip the incomer and black out the whole station instead of just the faulted feeder.

It would have been easy to record “settings applied” and move on. That would transfer an unverified protection arrangement into service with a signed FAT record implying it had been verified.

What was actually done: recorded as a deviation (not a pass, not a defect — a defect is something we got wrong; a deviation is something that can't be completed for reasons outside our scope); conservative provisional settings applied and recorded; a warning label fitted inside the board; formal written notification that settings must be applied and verified before site energisation; and a SAT hold point created.

The correct answer to “we can't test this” is never “mark it as passed”.

FD-006 · Label discrepancies — two Category B, one Category C

It's tempting to file everything labelled “label” as cosmetic. Two of three were not. A transposed terminal marker actively causes future wiring errors by someone who correctly followed the schedule. A missing “multiple supplies” warning label is a safety defect — someone will isolate the board, believe it is dead, and open it. Category is determined by consequence, not by the type of item.

Root cause of the missing label was an omission from the label schedule, not from the board — so fixing the board alone would have left the next board to repeat it.

Knowledge check

Summary

  • Never let the client witness the first energisation.
  • Declare incomplete items in the opening, not when they're discovered.
  • When you find one picking error, check the whole class.
  • When a test can't be completed: record it as a deviation with a hold point. Never as a pass.
Stage 9

Design change — a late motor uprate, traced

Objective

Trace one change through every document without losing anything — and understand why the cost of a change is set by decisions taken before it arrives.

Week 12, after the design freeze and after IFC drawings were released: the client's hydraulic consultant re-ran the network model with updated demand and revised pipe roughness for the ageing rising main. The duty point moved outside the 75 kW pumps' capability. The vendor confirmed 90 kW motors.

A 15 kW uprate is trivial engineering. The difficulty is entirely in when it arrived.

The trace

TQ-011 — the finding a graduate would miss

It is easy to check that our incomer and busbar still cope. It takes a step back to ask whether the 500 kVA transformer upstream still has capacity — because it also feeds site loads that are not on our drawings and not in our scope.

If it doesn't, the consequence is a transformer replacement: six figures, long lead, and an outage. Asking that question in Week 13 rather than at commissioning is worth more than everything else in this document.

What did NOT change — and why saying so matters

Control philosophy, schematic control logic, I/O list, safety circuit architecture, terminal numbering, incomer and busbar, network design, field control stations.

A good impact assessment states what is unaffected as explicitly as what is affected. It prevents unnecessary rework, limits the retest scope, and gives the client confidence the assessment was actually done rather than guessed. An assessment listing only changes leaves everyone wondering what was checked.

Cost and schedule

On the cost figures

No dollar figures are quoted in this package, deliberately. On a real project every line comes from a vendor quote or a rate. The structure of the answer is the deliverable: “I can't give you a figure without the quotes, and I wouldn't trust one that I could. What I can tell you is where the cost sits — the drives, the cubicle modification, the engineering hours for the revisions, and the cable variation on the installer's side. The two biggest potential costs, cancelling the drive order and reworking finished cubicles, were both avoided.”

Net schedule impact: ~2–3 weeks instead of 8–10. Five reasons, and every one was a decision made before the change was known:

  1. The drive order had not been placed — no cancellation, no restocking dispute, no scrap.
  2. The build had been re-sequenced so the drive cubicles were last — no wiring rework.
  3. The gland plates were undrilled — no plate rework.
  4. Glands and lugs were held pending confirmed cable sizes — minimal wrong stock.
  5. The general arrangement was held from release until vendor data arrived — the cubicle was modifiable, not scrap.
The answer to “tell me about a decision you weren't sure about” There was a decision that looked wrong for about six weeks. We had a sixteen-week lead time on the drives against a twelve-week programme, and the obvious fix was to just order them early. I held the order because the motor data wasn't confirmed and the design wasn't frozen, and I recovered the time by re-sequencing the build instead. For six weeks that looked like being overly cautious and it cost me some goodwill with the planner. Then in week twelve the client uprated the motors, and those drives would have been scrap. I'd like to say I predicted it — I didn't. What I actually did was refuse to commit to a long-lead, configured item against unconfirmed inputs, which is a rule rather than a prediction. The rule is what saved it.

Knowledge check

Summary

  • The cost of a late change is set by decisions taken before it arrives.
  • State what is unchanged as explicitly as what changes.
  • Verify, don't assume — frame sizes from the vendor GA, STO terminals from the manual.
  • Look upstream of your own scope. TQ-011 is the highest-value question in the whole exercise.
Stage 10

As-built and handover

Objective

Understand what an MDR is for, and why the carried-forward register is one of the most professionally important pages in the package.

The Manufacturer's Data Record is the asset's documentation for the rest of its life. This board will be in service for twenty to thirty years. Everyone who built it will have moved on. Three tests of a good MDR:

  • Maintenance — can a technician who has never seen this board fault-find it at 2 a.m. using only what is in here?
  • Modification — can an engineer in ten years safely add a feeder, knowing the spare capacity, the fault level basis and the protection arrangement?
  • Accountability — can anyone reconstruct why a decision was made?
The failure that destroys everything else

When the as-built doesn't match the board, every future maintainer eventually learns not to trust the drawings — and from then on the entire documentation set is worthless, because nobody knows which parts are still true. A transcribed as-built that nobody physically verified is worse than no as-built at all, because it looks authoritative and is fiction.

Why this page matters so much

Without it, a board arrives on site with a signed FAT certificate and everyone reasonably assumes it is finished. With it, the hold points are explicit and the risk sits with the party who actually owns it. CF-01 and CF-05 are the two that could cause real harm — one leaves protection unverified, the other could overload a transformer belonging to someone else. Both are genuinely outside our scope. Both are ours to have raised, and ours to have transferred visibly.

The handover meeting

Not a document transfer — a briefing. What the board does, walked physically cubicle by cubicle; where the spare capacity is; what is provisional and why; what the FAT did not test; the hold points before energisation; where the parameter backups are; the deviation and change history; who to call.

Hand over to the right people

The project manager will be gone in six months. The maintenance technician will be there in ten years, and is the person who will either trust this documentation or learn to ignore it.

Knowledge check

Summary

  • Verify the as-built against the physical board. Don't transcribe mark-ups blindly.
  • Chase certificates before final payment.
  • The carried-forward register transfers open risk visibly, with named owners.
  • Say explicitly what the FAT did not prove.
Interview preparation

The three explanations and the hard questions

Objective

Be able to deliver the 60-second version without notes, and answer the five questions that expose whether you actually understand this.

Rule

You may not use any of this until you can explain the underlying document without reading it. A polished answer you don't understand is worse than a rough answer you do — the follow-up question exposes it immediately, and then everything else you said becomes suspect.

The 60-second explanation

Time it — 55 to 70 seconds The project is a full electrical package for a water utility pump station — a 415-volt motor control centre with two 90-kilowatt variable-speed transfer pumps, some smaller feeders, a PLC with remote I/O, and a fibre link back to SCADA.

I should be upfront: it's a self-directed reference project, not delivered work. I built it because I wanted to understand how a package actually develops rather than just how to read a drawing. So I took it right through — design basis, single line, schematics, terminations, BOM, procurement, fabrication inspection, FAT, and handover.

The two things I got most out of it were the reconciliation work — cross-checking the SLD against the schematics against the BOM, three directions, and finding the things that don't line up — and tracing a late design change through every single document.

And it made me clear on where my limits are. There are things in there I deliberately didn't do: the fault level study, the protection coordination, the functional safety Performance Level. Those are inputs or specialist work. I flagged them as assumptions and escalated them, which is what I'd do on a real job.

Do not skip the last paragraph to save time. It converts a potential gotcha into evidence of judgement, and it is the most valuable part.

#QuestionWhat it tests

What it doesn't show is how I behave when a client is unreasonable, when the programme is gone, or when I have to tell someone senior that something they want isn't going to work. I haven't been tested on that and I'm not going to pretend I have.

What I'd say is that I've built the technical foundation so that when I am in those situations, the engineering isn't the thing I'm struggling with. And I'd want to be somewhere I can sit alongside someone who has done it, rather than being handed a package and left to find out.

When you don't know something

You will get asked something you can't answer. How you handle it is being assessed more than the answer.

  1. Say you don't know. Plainly. No hedging.
  2. Say what you do know that is adjacent.
  3. Say how you'd find out.
Never

Never invent a number. Never say “I think it's about…” for something with a real answer. An interviewer who catches one fabricated figure will re-evaluate everything you said — and they will be right to.

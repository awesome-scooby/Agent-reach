🚨 The chart everyone forgets

FACTGold hit a record near US$5,600 in January 2026, then fell more than 20% — its worst quarter since 2013 — before rebounding to around US$4,400–4,500 in August. Anyone who bought the January record is still well underwater eight months later. Gold is not a low-volatility asset. It is a low-correlation asset, which is a different and much less comforting thing.TheStreet, BingX and Intellectia market commentary, Aug 2026

What is driving the price right now

  • Real interest rates. Gold pays no income, so it competes with the after-inflation yield on bonds. When US inflation cooled to 3.4% in July and traders trimmed the odds of a September Fed hike, gold jumped. This is the dominant driver.CNBC, US CPI July 2026 (released 12 Aug 2026)
  • Central banks buying. They are now the dominant marginal buyer. China added 33 tonnes in Q2 2026, its strongest quarter since Q4 2023. Goldman Sachs expects roughly 60 tonnes per month of official-sector buying through 2026 as reserve managers diversify away from the dollar.World Gold Council / Goldman Sachs, via BingX and goldsilver.com, 2026
  • Geopolitics. Middle East developments are explicitly named by commentators as a swing factor for both inflation and the next two US CPI prints.CNBC, 11 Aug 2026
  • The Australian dollar working against you. AUD +10.8% over the year means AUD gold has risen materially less than USD gold. An unhedged Australian holder gets the metal's move minus the currency's move.

💰 Is gold expensive, fair or cheap right now?

READUnanswerable in the way you'd want. Gold has no earnings, no rent and no coupon, so there is no ratio that says "cheap". You can only compare it to its own history and to the alternatives — and by both of those measures it is expensive: near records, after +150% over five years, at a time when risk-free cash pays 5.55%.

CALLAnalyst targets exist (J.P. Morgan forecasts an average around US$6,000/oz by Q4 2026; the institutional range quoted is US$5,400–6,300). Treat these as opinions with a poor track record, not as data. They were also being published shortly before the 20% Q1 fall.

🐂 Bull case
  • Structural central-bank buying is price-insensitive and persistent — a genuine new source of demand.
  • If the Fed cuts rather than hikes, real yields fall and gold's main headwind becomes a tailwind.
  • Genuine portfolio insurance: it tends not to move with shares, which is worth something even at a zero yield.
  • Australian-dollar weakness would amplify returns for a local holder.
🐻 Bear case
  • Three US Fed members dissented in July wanting a hike. Higher-for-longer real rates is gold's worst environment.
  • It just proved it can fall 20% in a quarter. That risk has not gone away.
  • Zero income. In a 5.55% cash world, holding gold costs you 5.55% a year in forgone return.
  • A strengthening AUD keeps eating the local return.
  • Crowded positioning after a strong run is its own risk.

🔁 What would change this view

Toward green: a clear Fed pivot to cutting; a 15–20% pullback that restores a sane entry; evidence central-bank buying is accelerating.
Toward red: a September Fed hike; central banks slowing purchases; AUD strengthening past ~0.75; gold rallying another 15%+ on momentum alone with no change in the underlying drivers.

Digital assets

🪙 Stablecoins & crypto

A tool, not an investment Confidence High

⛔ Read this before anything else in this section

A stablecoin is not a bank deposit. It is not covered by the Australian Government's Financial Claims Scheme, which guarantees up to $250,000 per person per authorised deposit-taking institution. If a stablecoin issuer fails, is frozen, is sanctioned, or its reserves fall short, there is no government backstop. You are an unsecured creditor of a private company.

A term deposit paying 5.55% with a government guarantee and a stablecoin "yield" of 5% are not the same product wearing different clothes. The second one is a credit, custody, smart-contract and regulatory bet that happens to quote a similar number.

Stablecoins matured a lot in 2026 — real US law, real reserve rules, real audits. That reduced some risks and did nothing about others.

What actually changed: the GENIUS Act

  • FACTNow enacted US law. Implementation rules were due 18 July 2026.
  • Issuers must back every token 1:1 with high-quality liquid assets and hold 100% reserves.
  • Regular audits are mandatory.
  • Payment stablecoin issuers are financial institutions under the Bank Secrecy Act — full anti-money-laundering programs, transaction monitoring and suspicious activity reporting, similar to a chartered bank.

Sources: dualmedia.com, valueaddvc.com, eco.com GENIUS Act explainers, 2026.

📊 A detail that flips the "which is bigger" question

DATAUSDT leads on supply ($186.4B vs ~$75B). USDC leads on annual transaction volume — $18.3 trillion versus USDT's $13.3 trillion in 2025. So the "number one stablecoin" depends entirely on whether you measure the money parked or the money moving. USDC is used more; USDT is held more.Bitrue / reap.global stablecoin data, 2026

⚠️ Risks that regulation did not remove
  • De-peg risk. Full reserves reduce it. They do not eliminate a run.
  • Custody risk. Exchange failures have destroyed more retail money than de-pegs have.
  • Counterparty risk on "yield". Any stablecoin paying you interest is lending your money to someone. Ask who, and what happens if they don't repay.
  • Freezing. Major issuers can and do freeze addresses on request.
  • Australian tax. Crypto-to-crypto transactions are generally CGT events for Australian residents. Confirm your position with the ATO or a registered tax agent.
  • Shariah view. Interest-bearing stablecoin yield closely resembles riba. If compliance matters to you, treat any advertised "yield" as needing scholarly review before you touch it.
🎯 Where a stablecoin genuinely helps

Moving money between crypto venues, or settling internationally faster than a bank wire. Those are real uses.

CALLAs a store of your house deposit, a government-guaranteed Australian term deposit at 5.55% beats a US-dollar stablecoin on every axis that matters: yield, guarantee, currency match, tax simplicity and complexity. There is no gap here that a stablecoin fills for you.

The label on the tin is a starting point, not a verdict.

✅ Working in a buyer's favour

  • Prices falling on every timeframe — month, quarter, year and year-to-date all negative.
  • Clearance rate 57.4%, well under the ~65% historically associated with balanced conditions. Below 65% means sellers, not buyers, are the ones under pressure at auction.
  • Stock is building and homes are sitting longer. Vendors are accepting larger discounts than a year ago.
  • Yields repairing from both ends — 4.0% gross, now above Sydney and above the combined-capitals average, with rents up 5.1%.
  • Cheapest major capital on price-to-income at 7.1× versus Sydney's ~10×.

⛔ Working against a buyer

  • The cash rate is 4.35% and rose three times this year. Cheaper house, dearer money — the monthly repayment may not improve at all.
  • Servicing a new mortgage on the median dwelling nationally takes 45.9% of gross household income, versus a long-run average of 34.3% and a 30% stress threshold.
  • Roughly $180,000–$200,000 household income is generally cited as needed to comfortably carry a median-priced Melbourne house.
  • Vacancy under 1.8% means renting while you wait keeps getting more expensive.
  • A falling market can keep falling. Nothing here says it has stopped.

💵 The arithmetic nobody enjoys

A 20% deposit on the median house ($936,528) is $187,306. On the median dwelling ($797,354) it is $159,471. These are pure arithmetic on the sourced medians above.

Current variable mortgage rates: not verified — the 4.35% figure is the RBA cash rate, and what a bank actually charges you sits meaningfully above it.

🧭 Supply, demand and the longer-term picture
  • Population. Victoria has passed 7 million, growing ~1.8% a year, overwhelmingly through net overseas migration.   • Construction. Building approvals for Greater Melbourne were running 18% below the 10-year average as of Q1 2026. Approvals have been erratic — a 85.1% surge in February as a backlog of apartment applications cleared, then falls in March and June.ABS building approvals, 2026
  • Rental market. Vacancy below 1.8% with rents up 5.1%. A market cannot have a genuine oversupply problem and a 1.8% vacancy rate at the same time.

⏳ If you wait 6–12 months, what could improve?

  • Prices keep drifting down. At the current run rate, another 3–6% is plausible — that is roughly $28,000–$56,000 off the median house.
  • The RBA stops hiking, or starts cutting. This is the big one. It would improve your borrowing capacity far more than a price fall does.
  • Yields keep improving as rents rise and prices fall, making the numbers stack up better whether you live in it or rent it out.
  • You save more deposit at 5.55% in a term deposit while you wait — roughly $8,300 a year on a $150,000 balance, before tax.
  • Vendor motivation increases the longer stock sits unsold.

⏳ What could get worse?

  • Another rate hike. Inflation at 3.9% keeps this live. Your borrowing capacity falls, so a cheaper house becomes less affordable, not more.
  • The market turns before you do. Vacancy under 1.8%, population +1.8% a year and approvals 18% below average is a recipe for a fast recovery once rates ease. Everyone waiting for the bottom arrives at the same time.
  • Rents keep rising 5%+ — waiting has a running cost that compounds.
  • Your own circumstances. Job security matters more than any number on this page.

CALLThe genuinely useful move in a 🟡 window is not "buy" or "wait". It is get fully ready — finance pre-approved, deposit parked at 5.55%, suburbs researched, inspections done — so that when a condition flips you can act in days rather than months. Readiness costs nothing and is the only part of this you fully control.

📚 What is this thing, actually?

Plain-English explainers. Nothing here assumes you already know the jargon. Open one, close it, come back later.

Detail hidden by Focus mode. Turn Focus mode off in the top bar to see it.
🥇 Gold

What is it? A metal. That's genuinely all. It doesn't run a business, employ anyone, or pay you anything.

How does it make money? It doesn't. You only profit if someone later pays more than you did. There is no income, no dividend, no rent.

What moves the price? Mainly real interest rates — the return on safe assets after inflation. When safe assets pay well, holding a metal that pays nothing is expensive, so gold falls. When rates drop or inflation rises, gold rises. Also: central banks buying, a weaker US dollar, and fear.

Risks? It can fall 20% in a quarter — it did in early 2026. Storage or fund fees. And for Australians, a rising AUD eats the return.

Beginner should look at: the fee (0.15% vs 0.59% is a real difference), and whether it's hedged. Ask yourself what job gold does in your portfolio — if the answer is "it's been going up", that's not a job.

📈 Shares and the ASX 200

What is it? A share is part-ownership of a business. The ASX 200 is an index tracking the 200 largest Australian listed companies — a scorecard, not a thing you buy directly.

How does it make money? Two ways: dividends (your slice of profits, paid in cash — often with franking credits that reduce your tax) and capital growth (the share price rising as the business grows).

What moves the price? Company profits, and what investors will pay for those profits — the P/E ratio. A P/E of 20 means you're paying $20 for every $1 of annual profit. Higher interest rates push P/Es down, because safe alternatives get more attractive.

Risks? Businesses fail. Dividends get cut. The Australian market is unusually concentrated in banks and miners, so "diversified Australian shares" is less diversified than it sounds.

Beginner should look at: the P/E versus its own history, the dividend yield versus a term deposit, and what the index actually holds.

🧺 ETFs

What is it? A single ASX-listed thing that holds hundreds of investments. Buy one unit of VAS and you own a sliver of 300 Australian companies.

How does it make money? It passes through whatever its holdings produce — dividends as distributions, and price growth as unit-price growth. It takes a management fee first.

What moves the price? Whatever it holds. An ETF has no independent opinion; it's a wrapper.

Risks? All the risks of what's inside, plus fees, plus currency risk on unhedged offshore funds. A cheap ETF holding a terrible index is still a terrible investment.

Beginner should look at: the fee, the top holdings, whether it's hedged, and fund size (bigger usually means easier to trade). Fees are the only part of your return you can control with certainty.

🏠 Residential property

What is it? Land and a building. Unlike everything else on this board, it's usually bought with borrowed money — which is what makes it powerful and dangerous at the same time.

How does it make money? Rent (income) and capital growth (the value rising). Leverage amplifies both: with a 20% deposit, a 10% price rise is a 50% return on your cash — and a 10% fall is a 50% loss.

What moves the price? Chiefly how much people can borrow, which is set by interest rates and incomes. Then population growth, new supply, and confidence. This is why rate decisions matter more to house prices than almost anything else.

Risks? Illiquidity — you can't sell a bedroom. Huge transaction costs (stamp duty, agents, legals). Concentration: one asset, one suburb, most of your net worth. And interest-rate risk on the debt.

Beginner should look at: gross versus net yield. A 4.0% gross yield becomes roughly 2.5–3% after rates, insurance, management, maintenance and vacancy. Also: total cost to buy and sell, which is often 5%+ of the price each way.

🏛️ Bonds

What is it? A loan you make to a government or company. They pay you interest and return your money on a set date.

How does it make money? Interest payments (the "coupon"), plus a capital gain if you sell when yields have fallen.

What moves the price? The single most counter-intuitive rule in investing: bond prices move opposite to yields. If you hold a bond paying 5% and new bonds start paying 6%, nobody wants yours at full price — so its value falls. This is why bonds lost money while rates rose, and why they're attractive now that yields are high.

Risks? Rising rates cut the value of what you hold. Inflation erodes fixed payments. Company bonds can default; Australian government bonds effectively don't.

Beginner should look at: whether you'll hold to maturity (if yes, price swings don't matter — you get your 5.04%), and the yield versus inflation.

🪙 Stablecoins

What is it? A digital token designed to hold a steady value, almost always US$1, backed by reserves the issuer holds.

How does it make money? For you, normally it doesn't — $1 stays $1. The issuer makes money investing your dollar in US Treasuries. Any "yield" offered to you means someone is lending your money onward.

What moves the price? Nothing, by design. What moves is trust. If people doubt the reserves, the peg can break — that's the entire risk in one sentence.

Risks? No government guarantee. Custody risk. Freezing. US dollar exposure if you're an Australian saver. Crypto tax complexity.

Beginner should look at: who audits the reserves and what they hold. And be brutally honest about whether you need one at all — for saving Australian dollars in Australia, you don't.

📊 The jargon, decoded
  • Yield — annual income as a % of price. $20,000 rent on a $500,000 place = 4%.
  • Gross vs net — before costs vs after costs. Property ads quote gross. Your bank account sees net.
  • P/E ratio — price divided by annual earnings. Roughly, the years of profit you're paying for.
  • Real return — return minus inflation. 5.55% minus 3.9% = 1.65% real. This is the number that matters.
  • Basis point — one hundredth of a percent. A "25bp hike" is 0.25%.
  • Hedged — currency movements stripped out. Unhedged means AUD moves affect your return.
  • Franking credit — a tax credit attached to Australian dividends, because the company already paid tax on those profits.
  • Clearance rate — % of auctioned homes that sold. Above ~65% favours sellers; below favours buyers.
  • Drawdown — the fall from a peak to a trough. Gold's early-2026 drawdown was over 20%.
  • Sukuk — the Islamic finance instrument closest to a bond: ownership of an income-producing asset rather than a loan at interest.

What could go wrong

⚠️ Biggest risks right now

🔴 Another rate rise

Inflation at 3.9% is above the 2–3% target, the RBA "remains concerned", and it has already hiked three times in 2026. In the US, three FOMC members voted for a hike in July.

Hits: your borrowing capacity, house prices, share valuations, bond values, gold. Almost everything on this board at once. Helps: term deposits.

🔴 Melbourne keeps falling

−1.2% in a single month, −4.9% year to date, 5.5% below peak. Nothing in the data says this has stopped.

The trap: buying at what looks like the bottom and watching another 5% go. The counter-trap: waiting for a bottom you'll only recognise a year after it happened. This is why the buy-signal section says "get ready", not "buy" or "wait".

🟠 Shares are priced for things going well

ASX at ~20× earnings (top of its 5-year range) and 1.23× fair value; S&P near a record. Little cushion if earnings disappoint or rates stay high.

Specific worry: banks are the ASX's biggest sector and they sold off in August on housing concerns. Falling house prices and expensive bank shares are the same risk wearing two hats.

🟠 Gold after a fast run

+6.5% in 16 days, and a >20% quarterly fall already this year. No income, no valuation anchor, and a strengthening AUD subtracting from local returns.

The specific risk: the September Fed meeting. It rallied on lower hike odds; it can give it all back on higher ones.

🟠 The Australian dollar

+10.80% over 12 months, +2.45% in a month, at 0.7169. Every unhedged offshore holding — VGS, IVV, NDQ, GOLD, PMGOLD — is being quietly reduced by this.

Often invisible: you see a US record high in the news and a flat balance in your account, and blame the fund. It's the currency.

🟠 The risk inside your own head

The three assets that went up most in the last year (Nasdaq +26%, gold +22%, Perth +20.5%) are the three this board is least enthusiastic about. That is not a coincidence — it's what buying after the move looks like.

The tell: if you find yourself wanting to act because of a number that already happened, that's the moment to close the tab and come back tomorrow. Nothing on this board needs a decision today.

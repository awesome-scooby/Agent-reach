How this plan is built

Three design rules, taken from your own answers

You told me two things that most health plans ignore, and they are the reason this one is shaped strangely. First: routines die for you when they stop being novel. Second: being told what to do makes you want to stop doing it — even when you were the one who set the rule. That is not a character flaw and it is not a discipline problem. It is a fairly ordinary way an ADHD brain handles low-stimulation obligation, and it is predictable enough to design around.

1. Nothing here is an instruction.

Every daily item is a menu you choose from, never a list you must complete. You pick two. Which two is genuinely yours. The research term is autonomy support; the practical version is that a choice you made survives roughly three times longer than a rule you were handed.

2. The slot stays, the content rotates.

Habits fail when the activity is fixed. So the time-of-day anchor is permanent and the thing you actually do inside it changes every fortnight. Boredom is treated as scheduled maintenance, not as failure.

3. The floor is embarrassingly low, and I'm the check-in.

You said external accountability works and that things need to be stupidly easy. So: a five-minute walk counts as a complete day, and once a week I ask you three questions. There is no streak on this page to break, on purpose.

The reframe that has to stick

Prevention framed as avoiding a heart attack is a fear campaign, and fear is a stimulant with a very short half-life — it works for about a week and then your brain habituates and stops paying. Prevention framed as arriving at sixty with clean arteries and a spine that still tolerates a chair is a construction project. Construction projects survive boredom. That is why this document is named what it is named.

The one medical thing

Everything below this line is educated guessing until you have current numbers. Your last lipid panel is over a year old, and it followed a period of genuinely high cholesterol. That is the one gap worth closing this month.

Why this matters more than any habit on this page

Cholesterol that normalises and then drifts back is extremely common, because the thing that fixed it — a diet phase, a medication, a period of more activity — usually lapses without anyone noticing. You cannot feel your LDL. There is no symptom to alert you. A single blood draw replaces a year of guesswork, and it is the only item here that has a defined end point rather than an ongoing demand.

The question to actually ask, out loud

“My cholesterol was high, then normalised. What should my non-HDL or ApoB target be, and how often should I retest given that history?” That one sentence turns a routine appointment into a plan, and it puts the target in your hands rather than leaving it as ‘it was fine last time’.

The structural insight

Three problems, one cause

Your tailbone pain, your dry eyes, and your largest open cardiovascular exposure are not three problems. They are three invoices from the same behaviour: ten-plus hours a day, still, in front of a screen.

This is genuinely good news, and it is the most important paragraph on this page. You do not need three plans, three routines, or three sets of willpower. Interrupting one behaviour pays into all three accounts simultaneously:

  • Your coccyx is loaded by sustained sitting pressure and by the slouched, backward-tilted pelvis that appears after about forty minutes in a chair. The fix is not a better cushion. It is a shorter sitting bout.
  • Your eyes dry out because sustained screen focus cuts your blink rate substantially and, worse, produces incomplete blinks that never fully resurface the eye. Looking away and blinking properly is the treatment.
  • Your cardiovascular system responds to uninterrupted stillness with worse glucose handling and worse lipid clearance. Notably, breaking up sitting appears to matter partly on its own — a gym session in the evening does not fully undo ten unbroken hours.

So the anchor habit of this entire plan is not exercise, or diet, or anything that requires a decision. It is a three-minute interruption, roughly every forty-five minutes. It costs you close to zero additional time, because it replaces a break you were already taking — you were just taking it on your phone, seated, staring at a screen at the same distance.

The interruption itself

Three things, in whatever order. The whole thing is shorter than the average scroll break.

  • Change your posture. You already have a standing desk — the switch is the break. Sit thirty, stand twenty to thirty, alternate. Standing all day creates its own problems; alternating is the goal, not standing.
  • Twenty seconds looking at something more than six metres away, and ten deliberate, fully-closed blinks. Squeeze them shut. The full closure is the part that matters — incomplete blinking is the actual mechanism behind screen-related dry eye.
  • Move to a different room. Water, kettle, window, anything. The relocation is what stops you sitting back down in eight seconds.

Pick your trigger — one of three, your choice

  • A timer. Works if you find timers tolerable. Many ADHD brains do not.
  • An existing event. Every time a meeting ends, every time you finish a task, every time your water runs out. No new thing to remember — it rides on something already happening.
  • The symptom itself. When your eyes feel gritty or your tailbone starts complaining, that is the alarm. It's already firing several times a day. You just haven't been treating it as a signal.

The five levers

Ordered by how much they matter for you specifically. These are collapsed on purpose — nobody with ADHD reads a wall of health text, and pretending otherwise is how documents like this get closed and never reopened.

Largest gap

1 · Sleep — your most neglected lever

Late nights that are hard to stop is not laziness, and it is not a willpower deficit. It has a name in the literature — revenge bedtime procrastination — and it is disproportionately common with ADHD, for a reason that is worth stating plainly: after a day of doing what other people needed, the late evening is often the only stretch of time that feels genuinely yours. Going to bed feels like surrendering the last autonomous hours of the day. Any plan that treats this as a discipline problem will fail, because it is not one.

Why it belongs in a heart plan at all: consistently short sleep is associated with higher blood pressure, worse insulin sensitivity and disrupted appetite regulation. It is also the reason every other item on this page feels impossible the next day. It's the multiplier.

What actually works, none of which is “go to bed earlier”

  • Anchor the wake time, not the bedtime. This is the single highest-leverage change on this page. Your wake time is what actually shifts your body clock; bedtime follows it downstream. Pick one wake time and hold it within about an hour, weekends included. Trying to force an earlier bedtime directly almost never works — you just lie there.
  • Daylight within an hour of waking. Ten minutes outdoors, cloudy is fine, no sunglasses. It is the strongest signal your circadian system responds to and it is free. Bundle it with a coffee or the first walk so it isn't a separate task.
  • Give the evening an ending, not a ban. Don't remove the scroll — it's meeting a real need. Give the need a defined container instead: a window that is explicitly yours, with an ending signal that isn't a person or an app telling you off. Lights changing, an audiobook starting, a playlist that ends.
  • Put the phone across the room. The friction is the whole intervention. A ten-dollar alarm clock removes the phone's last legitimate reason to be within arm's reach at night.
  • Move in fifteen-minute steps. Fifteen minutes earlier per week, not two hours at once. Two hours at once fails by night three and takes the rest of the plan down with it.

One thing worth ruling out with your GP

If you snore heavily, wake unrefreshed regardless of hours slept, or anyone has noticed you stop breathing in your sleep — ask about obstructive sleep apnoea. Unlike most sleep complaints, it is an independent cardiovascular risk factor in its own right, it's treatable, and it is very commonly missed for a decade or more.

Daily anchor

2 · Breaking up sitting — and the tailbone

Your doctor has already assessed the coccyx pain, so this is management rather than diagnosis. The central point is unintuitive: the fix is time, not equipment. No single sitting bout longer than about forty-five minutes will do more for your tailbone than any cushion ever will.

Sitting mechanics that matter

  • Cushion: a firm wedge with a coccyx cut-out at the back beats a donut ring. Firm beats soft — counterintuitively, soft surfaces are worse.
  • Sofas and car seats are the enemy, more than your office chair. You sink, your pelvis rolls backwards, and your weight transfers off the sit bones directly onto the coccyx. If your evening scroll happens slumped on a couch, that posture is doing more damage than your workday.
  • Sit on the sit bones. Slight forward pelvic tilt, feet flat on the floor, weight through the two bony points you can feel under you. Slouching is the loading mechanism.
  • Alternate, don't just stand. Roughly thirty minutes seated, twenty to thirty standing. All-day standing swaps one problem for another.

Five minutes of strength, two or three times a week

Sitting shortens the hip flexors, which tilts the pelvis and increases the load on the coccyx, while the glutes progressively stop firing. Three movements address the whole chain:

  • Glute bridges — two sets of twelve. Restores the muscle that should be carrying you.
  • Half-kneeling hip flexor stretch — thirty seconds each side. Directly undoes the shortening.
  • Dead bugs — two sets of eight each side. Deep core, no spinal loading, safe with tailbone pain.

Go back to the doctor if

Pain radiates down a leg, you get numbness or tingling, there's any change in bowel or bladder function, the pain wakes you at night independently of sitting, or it follows a fall. Those are outside routine coccydynia management.

Same anchor

3 · The eyes — mostly free fixes

Also already assessed, so again: management. Most of what works here costs nothing and takes no time, which makes it unusually good value for a brain that resists effortful obligations.

  • Drop your monitor below eye level. Top of the screen at or slightly under eye height, so your gaze angles downward. Looking down narrows the eyelid opening, exposes less ocular surface, and slows evaporation. It is the single largest free improvement available to you and it takes ninety seconds to do once.
  • Train the blink, not just the break. Ten deliberate, fully-closed blinks per break, squeezed shut. Incomplete blinking — where the lids never quite meet — is the actual mechanism, so a fast partial blink doesn't count.
  • Check what air is hitting your face. Air conditioning vents, fans, car vents, heaters. This is the most commonly missed cause by a wide margin, and it's solved by pointing something in a different direction.
  • Warm compress, five to ten minutes, if your optometrist mentioned meibomian gland dysfunction or blocked oil glands. Heat first, then gentle lid massage. Worth asking them directly whether that's your diagnosis, because it changes the treatment entirely.
  • Preservative-free drops if you're using artificial tears more than about four times a day. Preservatives such as benzalkonium chloride irritate the ocular surface with frequent use — people often make themselves worse with the cheap bottle.
  • The late-night phone is the worst case for both problems at once: a bright screen, close range, in a dark room, with a blink rate near its floor — and it's also what's wrecking your sleep. Fixing the evening pays double.

Don't waste money on this one

Omega-3 supplements are widely recommended for dry eye, but the largest randomised trial on the question found no meaningful benefit over placebo. Save the money. Oily fish as food still earns its place in this plan — just for your heart, not your eyes.

15–20 min

4 · Movement — two things, not five

You have fifteen to twenty minutes. That is genuinely enough, provided it isn't spread across five different ambitions. Two things:

Walking volume

The mortality benefit curve for daily steps rises steeply from around 2,500 and flattens out somewhere near 7,000. Ten thousand is a marketing number from a 1960s pedometer campaign, not a clinical threshold. Your target is simply to move from wherever you currently are toward roughly seven thousand, by whatever route annoys you least.

  • Bundle it with something you actually want. Pick one podcast, audiobook or show and make it walk-only — you don't get it sitting down. This is a well-evidenced technique and it works especially well when the barrier is boredom rather than time.
  • Walk after dinner specifically. Ten to fifteen minutes after your largest meal blunts the post-meal glucose rise more than the same walk taken at another time of day. Same effort, two benefits, no extra minutes.

Strength, twice a week, ten minutes

Legs, glutes, back. Bodyweight is completely fine. This is doing double duty: muscle is where glucose goes, which improves insulin sensitivity, and it's the same work that takes load off your coccyx. If you only ever do the three movements from the tailbone section, that counts.

The rotation is the point

The console above swaps the flavour every fortnight, and there's a button to swap early. Use it. When you notice boredom, that is the system working as designed — it's a maintenance signal, not evidence that you've failed again.

Add, don't subtract

5 · Food — explicitly not a diet

You eat about half home-cooked, a quarter out, a quarter air-fryer chicken. That is a workable baseline and I'm not going to ask you to overhaul it, because restrictive eating plans fail on roughly the same timeline as fear-based motivation and take the rest of the plan with them.

The air fryer is fine. Plain chicken breast or tenderloins are lean protein and there is nothing wrong with them. The only thing worth changing is crumbed versus plain — the coating is where the sodium and refined starch live, and sodium matters given the family blood pressure picture. Same appliance, same convenience, different packet.

The highest-yield change: add fibre, don't remove food

Soluble fibre genuinely lowers LDL cholesterol — it binds bile acids in the gut and forces your liver to pull cholesterol from your blood to replace them. It is a real mechanism, not a wellness claim. Most people eat about fifteen grams of fibre a day; the target is closer to thirty.

The cheapest possible version: a tin of chickpeas, lentils or beans thrown into whatever is already cooking, two or three times a week. It requires no new recipe, no new skill, and no decision at the point of eating. Oats and psyllium are the other two heavy hitters.

A real sequence — the order matters

The four-week ramp

One new thing per week. Not four at once. Starting everything simultaneously is the single most common way plans like this die in week two, and it's the mistake almost everyone makes because week one feels so good.

Assume this happens — it's in the design

When it falls apart

It will. Not as a possibility to guard against — as a scheduled event. Every plan you have started before collapsed at some point, and the collapse is never actually what ended it. What ended it was the story you told yourself afterwards. So here is the protocol, written down in advance while you're not in it:

  • A missed day is data, not a verdict. There is no streak on this page. Nothing resets. The thirty-day counter only ever counts upward, specifically so that a bad Tuesday cannot be turned into evidence about your character.
  • The floor day is a real, complete day. One break, one set of blinks, a five-minute walk. That is a full entry. It is not a lesser version and it doesn't come with an asterisk.
  • Boredom is the maintenance light, not the failure light. When something stops being interesting, that's the fortnight rotation asking to happen early. Press the swap button. You're allowed to be bored of your own plan.
  • If you drop the whole thing for three weeks: restart at the week one row. Book the test, lower the monitor, nothing else. Not as punishment — because that's the entry point that works, and it worked the first time.
  • You are permitted to renegotiate any of this with me. It's a draft, not a prescription. If an item is stupid, or doesn't fit your life, say so and it comes out. A plan you had a hand in editing is one you're far more likely to still be running in March.
Your external accountability

The weekly check-in

You said someone checking in on you is what actually works. So once a week, I ask three questions. It takes about sixty seconds and there is no wrong answer to any of them.

  1. How many days did you move at all? Any amount. Five minutes counts.
  2. What time did you get to sleep on the worst night? Not the average — the worst one. The average hides the problem.
  3. What bored you? This is the one that matters most. It drives the fortnight rotation, and it's the question no health plan ever asks.
Non-negotiable

Red flags

Emergency — call an ambulance, don't drive yourself, don't wait to see if it passes

  • Chest pain, pressure, tightness or heaviness — particularly if it comes on with exertion and eases with rest
  • Pain spreading to the jaw, neck, shoulder, back or either arm
  • Shortness of breath out of proportion to what you're doing
  • Sudden cold sweat, nausea, or a feeling of impending doom alongside any of the above
  • Fainting, or a racing heartbeat with light-headedness

Heart attack symptoms in women, and in people with diabetes, are more often breathlessness, nausea, extreme fatigue or back and jaw pain rather than classic chest pain. Worth knowing regardless of who you are.

Same-week appointment, not an emergency

  • Eyes: sudden vision change, genuine eye pain rather than dryness, new light sensitivity, or discharge
  • Tailbone: leg radiation, numbness, bowel or bladder changes, night pain unrelated to sitting, or pain following a fall
  • Sleep: heavy snoring with unrefreshing sleep, or witnessed pauses in breathing

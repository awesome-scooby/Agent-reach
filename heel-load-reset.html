Straight answers to your specific questions

Is 2–3 km/day, 5 days/week reasonable? Yes — as a destination. It was not reasonable as a jump, which is what you did. The volume isn't the problem; the rate of change was.

Are rest days sufficient? No. You have effectively none. That's the second-biggest fixable thing after the ramp rate.

Should you reduce distance or speed? Distance yes, speed mostly no. Cut distance to roughly 60% for two weeks. Keep pace at a comfortable 4.5–5.5 km/h — walking very slowly lengthens ground-contact time and doesn't meaningfully reduce heel load.

Does pain persisting after 2–3 days off mean something is badly wrong? Not necessarily. Irritated plantar-heel tissue commonly takes 1–3 weeks to settle, not 2–3 days. But it is the reason this plan starts conservative, and the reason the heel-squeeze test below matters before you start.

02 · What is probably going on

Likely, unknowable, and worth ruling out

I'm not diagnosing you and I can't — that needs hands on your foot. What I can do is tell you which explanations your specific details support and which they argue against, because your presentation has one detail that cuts against the obvious answer.

The detail that changes things: your pain is worst in the evening

Textbook plantar fasciitis is worst on the first steps in the morning and after sitting, and eases as you move. Yours builds through the day and peaks at night. That's a cumulative-load pattern, not a classic fasciitis signature. It doesn't rule fasciitis out — early-stage overload can present this way before the morning pattern develops — but it means I'd weight the possibilities differently than a generic "you have plantar fasciitis" answer would.

Plantar heel load intolerance / early plantar fascia overload

Fits well

Bilateral, aching-and-tight, load-proportional, triggered by a documented load doubling, in a 33-year-old with no strength base. This is the most probable bucket. The evening pattern fits an early or load-driven stage rather than an established one.

Heel fat pad irritation

Fits well

Strongly supported by your own observation that it hurts more when you don't wear socks. That's an interface-pressure signal: a few millimetres of cushioning changing your symptoms points to the heel pad taking direct compressive load. Also fits "worse in the evening" and "both sides."

Insertional Achilles / posterior heel tendinopathy

Possible — depends on exactly where

You said "back of feet … heel," which is ambiguous. If you mean the rear of the foot as opposed to the forefoot, this is less likely. If you mean the back of the heel bone where the Achilles attaches, this moves up sharply — and it changes your treadmill incline setting. Test C below settles it in 30 seconds.

Calcaneal bone stress reaction

Rule out before starting

Less likely bilaterally, but a 94% load increase in three weeks is exactly the mechanism, and bone stress does not settle with a few days off. Test D below is the screen. If it's positive, don't start the walking plan — get imaging.

Inflammatory enthesitis (spondyloarthropathy spectrum)

Flag, not a prediction

Bilateral heel pain in a young male is a recognised presentation of inflammatory enthesitis, and it's the one thing here that's a GP and blood test question rather than a physio one. Your evening-worst pattern argues against it (inflammatory pain is classically morning-worst with prolonged stiffness and improves with movement). Mention it to a GP only if you also have morning stiffness lasting over 30 minutes, low back or buttock stiffness, or other joints involved. I'm naming it because leaving it out would be dishonest, not because I think it's likely.

Cannot be determined without an examination

  • —Which specific tissue is generating the pain. Palpation, resisted testing and sometimes ultrasound settle this; a chat cannot.
  • —Your actual calf flexibility. Limited ankle dorsiflexion is one of the more consistently reported associations with plantar heel pain and is measured, not guessed.
  • —How your foot behaves under load. "Normal arch" standing still tells you very little about what happens mid-stance at 5 km/h.
  • —Whether your current insole is helping or actively causing a pressure point. See the shoe audit in section 04.
  • —Whether there's a bone stress component. Only imaging answers that definitively.
03 · Two minutes, tonight

Four tests that narrow where

These localise the problem. They do not diagnose it. Do all four on both feet, sitting down, and note which reproduces your pain most clearly — then tell your podiatrist or physio the letter.

TEST A

Medial heel press

Press your thumb firmly into the inside-front corner of the heel bone, where the arch meets the heel.

Sharp and pinpoint → plantar fascia attachment region
TEST B

Central pad press

Press into the middle of the heel pad, straight down where you'd bear weight.

Deep, dull, bruise-like → heel fat pad region
TEST C

Achilles pinch + 10 heel raises

Pinch the back of the heel where the tendon attaches, then do 10 slow heel raises holding a wall.

Tender at the back, worse with raises or stairs → Achilles insertion region
TEST D · THE ONE THAT MATTERS

Calcaneal squeeze

Squeeze the heel bone firmly from both sides at once, like a vice, not from underneath.

Sharp pain → possible bone stress. Book a podiatrist or physio this week and do NOT start the walking plan.

Get assessed promptly — don't wait six weeks — if any of these apply

  • Test D reproduces sharp pain, or the pain is worsening despite reduced walking.
  • Pain at rest or pain that wakes you at night, unrelated to what you did that day.
  • Swelling, redness, warmth over the heel, or any fever.
  • Numbness, pins and needles, or burning into the sole or toes — that's a nerve question (tarsal tunnel, Baxter's nerve, or referred from the lower back), and the strengthening plan is not the right answer for it.
  • Morning stiffness lasting more than 30 minutes, especially alongside low back or buttock stiffness, or other joints.
  • A sudden pop or snap, or you can't bear weight.
  • No meaningful change after six weeks of doing this plan properly.

Who to see: a podiatrist or physiotherapist first — they diagnose the tissue and check your footwear and loading. A GP if any of the systemic or nerve flags above apply, or if you need an imaging referral. In Australia, if this becomes persistent, ask your GP about a GP Management Plan — it can attract Medicare rebates for up to five allied-health visits per calendar year.

04 · Footwear

Yes, this is probably a major factor

Audit your Skechers tonight — two specific checks

1. Count the insoles. Pull out the insert you added. Is there a factory insole underneath it? If you stacked the aftermarket insert on top, you've reduced the shoe's internal depth, raised your heel up out of the heel counter where it's least secure, and quite possibly put an arch bump in the wrong place. Remove the factory one and re-test. This is a genuinely common cause of new heel pain in people who "added support."

2. Press the heel midsole with your thumb. At roughly a year of 5-days-a-week wear, you're likely 500–900 km into a foam that compresses faster than most. If it feels dense and doesn't spring back, the cushioning is gone — and it went gone right around the time you increased your distance.

Two more contributors sitting in plain sight

Road camber. Footpaths and roads are built with a 1–2% cross-fall for drainage. Walking the same route on the same side, day after day, chronically everts one foot and inverts the other. You report your left is worse. That asymmetry deserves a look — and it's one thing a treadmill fixes outright.

Barefoot hours at home. You walk 2–3 km deliberately, then spend eight or ten hours barefoot on tile or floorboards. That's the larger unmanaged load, and it's the best explanation on the table for why your pain peaks in the evening. Fixing it costs about $45.

What goes on your feet, by situation

1 · Outdoor walking

3–5 hrs/week
Cushioned running shoeNot "walking shoes"

Running shoes get far better midsole foam per dollar, get updated yearly, and are what the research on footwear and load is actually done on. Always with socks. Choose flat routes and alternate the direction you walk your usual loop so the camber isn't always tilting the same foot.

2 · Treadmill

Your primary surface for weeks 1–4
Same running shoesNot barefootNot socks only

Wear your joggers. This one isn't a close call. A treadmill belt is a thin layer over a hard deck — it is more forgiving than concrete but nowhere near soft. Socks on a moving belt means no rearfoot control, friction blisters, and a real slip risk. And your own symptom report settles it: if losing a sock makes your heel hurt more, removing the shoe as well is the opposite of what your foot is asking for.

3 · Around the house

8–10 hrs/day — your biggest load
Supportive slide or indoor pairNot barefoot on tileNot flat scuffs

This is the highest-leverage change in this whole document and it takes no effort. Options, cheapest first: a supportive arch-contoured thong (Archies, ~$45, Australian, made for exactly this), a recovery slide (OOFOS ~$90–110), a slipper with an actual midsole (Vionic ~$100–130), or simply keep your previous pair of runners by the door as dedicated indoor shoes for $0. Flat scuff slippers with no midsole do nothing — they're barefoot with fabric on top.

4 · Longer walks (3 km+)

From week 5
Newest / most cushioned pairRotate two pairs

Save your freshest pair for the long one. Once you can afford it, running two pairs in rotation is associated with lower injury rates than wearing one pair into the ground — the foam gets time to decompress and the small differences between shoes vary your loading slightly. Not urgent, but it's the reason to keep your old pair rather than bin it.

Barefoot walking

Revisit at week 6
Not now — later, and gradually

Barefoot and minimalist walking can build intrinsic foot strength, and I'm not against it in principle. But it's wrong for you right now for three concrete reasons: you have zero current strength base, your symptoms are explicitly worse with less cushioning, and rapid minimalist transitions are associated with increased bone stress injury — which is the one thing here worth being careful about given your load spike. Re-entry protocol from week 6, only if you've been green: 5 minutes barefoot on carpet or grass, add 2–3 minutes a week, never on concrete, never on the treadmill.

Insoles

Worth a trial — but check yours first
Prefab, yesCustom, not yet

The evidence is modest but real: off-the-shelf foot orthoses give a small short-term reduction in plantar heel pain over about 3 months, and custom orthoses have not been shown to beat prefabricated ones for most people. So don't spend $400–600 on customs as a first move. Fix the stacking issue first, try a $40–70 prefab, and give it three weeks. If Test C was your positive (back of the heel), a simple 6–10 mm heel raise in both shoes is a cheap, evidence-supported trial that a generic arch support won't replicate.

What to look for in a shoe — checked in the shop, in 60 seconds

Heel stack, meaningful but not maximal
Roughly 22–32 mm under the heel. Enough foam to blunt the impact peak; not so much that the platform gets unstable.
Heel-to-toe drop of 8–12 mm
While your heel is symptomatic, a higher drop reduces the demand on both the plantar fascia and the Achilles. Avoid zero-drop for now.
Firm heel counter
Squeeze the cup at the back between thumb and finger. It should resist. If it folds flat, your heel isn't being held.
Torsional stiffness
Hold toe and heel and twist like wringing a towel. It should fight you. A shoe that wrings out easily gives the midfoot nothing.
Bends only at the ball of the foot
Push the toe up. The crease should form under the toe joints, not through the middle of the arch.
Removable factory insole
So an insert goes in place of it, never stacked on top. This is the mistake you may already have made.
Toe box you can splay in
Thumb-width past the longest toe, and enough width that your toes spread rather than press together.
Fit late in the day
Feet swell. Try them at 5pm, in the socks you'll actually walk in. And replace at 600–800 km.

05 · The six-week rebuild

Strength is the fix. Stretching is the relief.

If you only take one thing from this section: the loaded calf raise is the exercise that changes your outcome. The trial evidence on plantar heel pain favours high-load progressive strengthening over stretching alone at three months. Stretching feels better sooner; strengthening is what stops this recurring the next time you increase your walking. Do both — but never skip the strength days to do more stretching.

Incline depends on your test result

If Test A or B was your positive (under the heel): use 1–2%. A slight incline reduces the braking force at heel strike and shifts load forward off the heel. Most people with plantar heel pain tolerate it better than flat.

If Test C was your positive (back of the heel, Achilles): keep it at 0–1%. Incline increases ankle dorsiflexion and Achilles demand, and it will aggravate you. Ignore the incline column above and stay flat.

Never use a decline. And don't hold the handrails — it changes your gait and unloads the very thing you're trying to train.

Treadmill vs concrete: treadmill wins, for now

Not because belts are magically soft — they're firmer than most people assume. It wins for four specific reasons: no camber (removes the asymmetric loading that may be behind your left foot being worse), fixed speed (you can't unconsciously drift faster as you warm up), a stop button (you're never 1.5 km from home when it starts hurting), and exact dosing (1.5 km means 1.5 km, not "about 1.5 km").

Long-term it's neutral — outdoor walking is fine and more enjoyable. Use the treadmill as the controlled environment for weeks 1–2, then reintroduce outdoors deliberately.

06 · Pain rules

Judge every walk by tomorrow morning

The 24-hour rule — the single most useful habit in this document

How a walk feels during and immediately after is mostly noise. Adrenaline, warmth and blood flow all mask what's happening. The signal is your first 20 steps out of bed the next morning.

Score it 0–10 the same way every day: same time, before coffee, before the stretches, first 20 steps. Then compare three numbers — today, yesterday, and your five-day average. A walk that felt fine but leaves tomorrow morning at 7 was too much. A walk that ached at 4 but leaves tomorrow at 2 was fine. Trust the morning number over your in-the-moment impression every single time.

The same test applies to exercises. A new strength exercise that leaves you sore in the muscle belly of the calf is working. One that leaves the heel itself worse the next morning is too much load — halve the reps, don't drop the exercise.

Green — continue

  • 3/10 or less during the walk
  • Settles within 60 minutes of finishing
  • Next morning's first 20 steps score the same or better
  • Both feet behaving similarly
→ Carry on. Progress on schedule once all four increase criteria are met.

Yellow — reduce and hold

  • 4–5/10 during or after
  • Takes 2–24 hours to settle
  • Next morning stiffer, but eases within 10 minutes of walking
  • One foot clearly worse than the other
→ Halve the walk for two days, then return to your current week's distance. Do not advance a week. Keep the exercises — they are not what's aggravating you.

Red — stop and get assessed

  • Above 5/10, or you're limping or altering your gait
  • Morning score higher two mornings in a row
  • Pain at rest, or waking you at night
  • Swelling, redness, or heat over the heel
  • Numbness, tingling, or burning into the sole or toes
  • Test D (heel squeeze) sharply positive
  • A sudden pop or snap, or you can't bear weight
→ Stop walking. Book a podiatrist or physio. Keep the gentle stretches; drop the loaded work until you've been seen.

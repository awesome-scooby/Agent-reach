0. The honest summary: read this first

You are not a "let's optimise diet and gadgets" couple. You are a "get a proper infertility workup this month" couple.

About 84% of couples conceive within 12 months of regular unprotected sex, and about 92% within 2 years [34]. A 28-year-old woman trying for 2–3 years without a single pregnancy means there is very likely a specific, findable cause. Supplements, food changes, apps and wearables will not fix a blocked tube, endometriosis, an ovulation problem or a low sperm count. Those factors are what you need to find.

Your five most important facts, ranked by how likely each is to matter:

  1. No semen analysis in 2–3 years of trying. A male factor contributes in roughly 40–50% of infertile couples [35]. The test costs about $0–$110 after Medicare. It is the cheapest high-yield test in all of fertility medicine. Book it this week.
  2. Heavy, painful periods. Together with infertility, these are classic red flags for endometriosis or adenomyosis. Fibroids and polyps are other possibilities. Endometriosis is found in a large share of women investigated for infertility [26][39]. This needs a proper pelvic ultrasound and probably a gynaecology or fertility specialist opinion. Heavy bleeding also commonly causes iron deficiency.
  3. Sex once a week "planned around ovulation". If the timing is even slightly off (an LH surge missed, tested at the wrong time of day, or sex after ovulation instead of before), a weekly pattern can miss the fertile window in many cycles. The fix is free: sex every 1–2 days through the fertile window, and every 2–3 days the rest of the month [21].
  4. Ovulation has not been confirmed. A positive LH strip shows the surge, not that an egg was released. A correctly timed mid-luteal progesterone blood test is the cheap confirmation.
  5. Tubal patency is unknown. Blocked tubes cause no symptoms. If the other tests are normal, a tube test is next. An oil-based contrast tube flush has randomised-trial evidence that it increases pregnancy rates in the following six months [22].

About wanting a baby boy: no natural method changes the sex of your baby, and choosing sex through IVF for non-medical reasons is prohibited in Western Australia and under NHMRC guidelines [19]. The details are in Section 11. The chance of a boy is about 51% whatever you do. Please don't spend money or cycles on "boy" timing methods. Timing sex late in the window to "favour boys" (the Shettles method) can actually lower your chance of pregnancy at all.

About your "normal" tests: "some tests were normal" is not enough information. Ask your GP for printed copies. Standard blood tests often do not assess the things most likely to matter here: tubes, endometriosis, confirmed ovulation, and sperm. Section 8 lists what is still needed.

Missing data that matters: actual weights (BMI). At about 168 cm, BMI 25 is about 71 kg and BMI 30 is about 85 kg. BMI over 30 in either partner measurably reduces fertility, and a BMI over 35 excludes you from the public KEMH fertility clinic [1].

Your simplest high-probability schedule

Rule 1 (base layer, whole month): sex every 2–3 days, regardless of any test. This alone covers almost every fertile window without tracking [21].

Rule 2 (fertile boost): from about day 10 of the cycle (for 28–32-day cycles), switch to every day or every second day until 2 days after the positive LH test.

Rule 3: on the day the LH strip turns positive and on the following day, have sex both days.

Rule 4: don't abstain for more than 5 days at any point in the cycle, even outside the fertile window.

How to use LH strips properly (where many people go wrong):

  • Start testing about 17 days before your expected period. For a 28-day cycle that is day 11, for 30 days it is day 13, and for 32 days it is day 15. Start 2 days earlier if your cycles vary.
  • Test twice a day (about 10 am to midday, and 6–8 pm). Surges can be short, and once-daily testing misses some.
  • Reduce fluids for about 2 hours before testing. Don't rely on first-morning urine for most LH strips.
  • A faint line is negative. Positive means the test line is as dark as or darker than the control line.
  • If you never get a clear positive, or you get several positives spread across the cycle, tell the doctor. That can suggest anovulation or PCOS.

6. Food and fertility

Honest framing: almost all diet–fertility evidence is observational. Healthier eaters also differ in many other ways. Better diets are consistently associated with better semen quality and ovulatory function [40][41]. No food has been proven to raise pregnancy rates by itself. So this guide says "associated with" and never "fertility-boosting".

Parallel referrals: KEMH (public, free, long wait) [1] and Adora Fertility Murdoch (bulk-billing model) [6] or Genea's bulk-billed Fertility GP [4] → 6. Tubal test via the specialist → 7. Treatment matched to the findings. Go to IVF only if tubes are blocked, there is severe male factor or significant endometriosis, or cheaper treatments fail. If IVF is needed, a bulk-billing clinic costs about $2,430 per cycle compared with $6,600–$7,200 at full-fee clinics [7][8][11].

15. Private health insurance: do you need it?

  • Not for investigations. GP visits, blood tests, semen analysis, ultrasound and specialist consults are out-of-hospital Medicare services. Private insurance cannot legally pay for out-of-hospital Medicare gaps.
  • For IVF, insurance only helps with the in-hospital part (the egg-collection day-surgery fee and theatre costs). This needs Gold-tier hospital cover that includes "assisted reproductive services", with a 12-month waiting period [50]. A couple's Gold hospital policy in WA typically costs several thousand dollars a year (approx.). That is often more than it saves across 1–2 IVF cycles, especially at a bulk-billing clinic.
  • Pregnancy and birth: public maternity care in Perth (Fiona Stanley, Rockingham General, KEMH) is free and high quality. Private obstetric care is a comfort choice, not a safety requirement, and also needs a 12-month wait.
  • Lifetime Health Cover loading: this applies from 1 July after you turn 31 if you don't hold hospital cover. He is 33, so he may already attract a loading (2% per year over 30) when he buys cover. She is 28, so there is no loading yet. Check your own situation.
  • Possible KEMH issue: a third-party source says KEMH public fertility requires no private insurance [10]. Confirm with KEMH first.
  • Verdict: do not buy insurance for fertility now. Revisit only if the specialist says IVF is likely and you've done the maths with a clinic quote, or if you want private obstetric care. If you'd pay the Medicare Levy Surcharge anyway because of your income, basic hospital cover may make sense for tax reasons. That is a separate calculation.

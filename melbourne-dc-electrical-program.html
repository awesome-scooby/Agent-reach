Your position — read this first

Before any curriculum: you are not starting from zero, and the plan changes completely because of where you actually sit.

The thing you're undervaluing

You are a project engineer at a switchroom and switchboard manufacturer. On every Melbourne data-centre build happening right now, the switchboards and the transformers are the critical path. Lead times are the single most common cause of a program blowing out. You already sit inside the part of the supply chain that contractors and clients are most afraid of and least able to interrogate.

What you already own

  • AS/NZS 61439 — form segregation, type testing, temperature rise, Icw/Ipk withstand, busbar bracing, IP ratings. Data-centre clients are the most demanding 61439 customers in the country.
  • FAT — factory acceptance testing is literally Level 2 commissioning. Most graduate engineers applying for these roles have never witnessed one.
  • Drawing fluency — GA drawings, schematics, wiring diagrams, termination and cable schedules for boards.
  • Delivery mechanics — submittals, technical queries, material approvals, long-lead procurement, schedule pressure, angry customers.
  • Switchgear selection reality — ACB vs MCCB, frame sizes, trip units, cascading, what's actually available versus what the spec asked for.

What's missing — the whole gap, honestly

  • Everything upstream of your board — utility supply, HV/MV switchgear, transformers, protection grading.
  • Everything downstream — UPS, batteries, STS, PDU, RPP, busway, rack power, dual-corded loads.
  • System-level thinking — N+1 vs 2N, concurrent maintainability, fault tolerance, single points of failure.
  • Site-side design — AS/NZS 3000 and 3008 as applied to installations, not factory-built assemblies.
  • L3–L5 commissioning — SAT, integrated systems testing, load banks, black-building, failure simulation.
  • Design calculations — maximum demand, cable sizing, volt drop, fault level, generator and UPS sizing.
Strategy in one line

Do not present yourself as a graduate engineer who wants to learn data centres. Present yourself as the switchgear specialist who understands why your board is late and what it will do under fault — and who is now building the system-level picture around it. That story is worth $150k+. "Keen to get into data centres" is worth $110k.

ADHD guardrails

  • One output per block. A drawn diagram, a solved number, a recorded answer. If there's no artefact, the block didn't happen.

06

Lesson 01 — the whole power path

Start here. Everything else in data-centre electrical hangs off this one diagram. Read the analogy, study the drawing, then answer the questions in chat and I'll mark them.

Analogy first

A data centre is a building where the power is never allowed to stop, not even for a quarter of a second. Everything in the diagram below exists to answer one question: if this part dies right now, what carries the load? Utility dies → generator. Generator takes 10–15 seconds to start → battery covers the gap. Whole path dies → there's a second identical path. That's it. That's the entire industry.

The eight stages, in plain language

Utility supply22 kV from the network. Two separate feeds, ideally from different zone substations.
HV switchboard / RMUSwitches and protects the HV. Where the site boundary usually sits.
Transformer22 kV down to 415 V. Dyn11 — delta primary, star secondary, 30° shift. Star point gives you the neutral and the earth reference.
LV main switchboardYour world. ACBs, busbar, metering, protection. The generator connects here.
UPS + batteryCovers the 10–15 seconds between utility loss and generator on load. Nothing else can.
PDUSteps down / distributes to the floor. Often has its own transformer and sub-metering.
Busway / RPPOverhead busway or a remote power panel feeding rows of racks.
RackDual-corded server. Two PSUs, one on A, one on B. Each path carries the full load alone.

The thing everyone gets wrong

In a true 2N design, each path can only ever be loaded to 50%. If path A is at 70% and B fails, A trips and you've lost the room. Every capacity conversation in a data centre is really a conversation about this number.

Your switchboard knowledge connects directly: the ACB frame size and the busbar rating on MSB A must carry the whole facility load, not half of it — even though it normally sees half.

Memory trick

"Under Terrible Times, Get Us Power Before Reboot" — Utility, Transformer, Tie/MSB, Gen, UPS, PDU, Busway, Rack. Clunky, which is exactly why it sticks.

Interview question this lesson answers

"Walk me through the power path in a data centre from the street to the server, and tell me what happens at each stage when the utility fails."

This is asked in almost every first-round DC electrical interview. Being able to answer it fluently, unprompted, in 90 seconds, is worth more than any certificate.

Your turn — answer these in chat

Don't look anything up. Wrong answers are more useful to me than right ones — they tell me where to aim.

  • Q1. The utility fails at 09:00:00. Describe, second by second, what happens between 09:00:00 and 09:00:20 in the diagram above.
  • Q2. Why can't the generator alone protect the IT load? Be specific about the mechanism.
  • Q3. A facility has a 1,000 kW IT load in a 2N design. What's the minimum continuous rating of MSB A's incoming ACB, and why?
  • Q4. Name one component in the diagram that, if it failed, would take down the whole facility. If you think there isn't one, say so and justify it.
  • Q5. From your day job: which item in that diagram have you personally built or tested? How would you describe it in an interview?
Practical problem

A 1.2 MW IT load, 2N, power factor 0.95, UPS efficiency 96%. Size the UPS on each path and state the minimum transformer rating you'd specify for path A. Show your working — I'll mark the method, not just the number.

07

How to read an SLD

Experienced engineers don't read a single line diagram left-to-right. They run a fixed sequence, every time, on every drawing. Learn the sequence and you'll look experienced within a fortnight.

The eye path — in this order, always

Find the sourcesTop of the sheet. How many incomers? Utility, generator, UPS. Count them before anything else.
Find the voltage transitionsEvery transformer. Note the ratio and the vector group. Voltage tells you which standards apply.
Find the bus sections and tiesWhere can the board be split? A bus tie is the single most information-dense symbol on the drawing — it tells you the operating philosophy.
Trace one path to one loadPick the most critical load. Follow it back to source, naming every device you cross.
Now trace its alternate pathIf you can't find a second path, it isn't redundant. Say so.
Look for the common pointWhere do A and B touch? That point is the design's real weakness. Often it's a single earth bar, a shared control supply or one bus tie.
Read the protection lastCT ratios, relay functions, settings. Only meaningful once you know the topology.

Apply it to FIG 06.1

Run the sequence on the diagram above right now, out loud:

1 → four sources (2 utility, 2 gen)
2 → two transformers, 22kV/415V Dyn11
3 → no bus tie shown — fully separated
4 → rack ← busway A ← PDU A ← UPS A ← MSB A ← TX A ← HV A
5 → same again on B
6 → common point = the rack itself
7 → protection not shown on this sheet

The question that makes you sound senior

Whenever you're handed an SLD in an interview or a design review, ask: "What's the single common point between A and B, and is it acceptable?" It reframes you instantly from someone reading a drawing to someone reviewing a design.

Drawing types you'll be trained on, in order

  • Single line diagram — weeks 1–4
  • Load schedule + cable schedule — weeks 3–5
  • Switchboard GA and panel layout — you already read these
  • Schematic and wiring diagrams — weeks 5–7
  • Protection and relay diagrams — weeks 9–11
  • Control diagrams and cause & effect matrix — weeks 12–14
  • Earthing layouts and site electrical layouts — weeks 15–17

Redundancy topologies

The highest-yield interview topic in the entire field. Candidates who confuse N+1 with 2N do not get offers.

TermMeansSurvivesDoes not surviveTypical use
NExactly enough capacityNothingAny single failureNon-critical / office
N+1One spare module on a common busOne module failure; module maintenanceBus fault, switchboard fault, upstream faultUPS modules, generators, CRAC units
2NTwo complete independent systemsAny single failure anywhere in one pathTwo simultaneous failures across both pathsHyperscale critical power
2N+1Two systems, each internally N+1A whole path plus a module on the otherVery little — highest cost tierHighest-tier financial / defence
Concurrently
maintainable		Any component can be taken out for planned work with no load impactPlanned maintenance on any single itemNot the same as fault tolerance — an unplanned failure during maintenance may still biteUptime Tier III
Fault
tolerant		Any single unplanned failure is survived, including during maintenanceUnplanned failure at any timeMultiple simultaneous independent faultsUptime Tier IV
The distinction that separates candidates

Concurrent maintainability is about planned work. Fault tolerance is about unplanned failure. A Tier III facility lets you service any component without dropping load — but if something fails while that component is out, you may lose the room. Tier IV survives both. If you say only this in an interview, you're already ahead of most applicants.

09

Commissioning — where your FAT experience pays

Data-centre commissioning runs in five levels. You have already done Level 2 professionally. Most candidates for these roles have not.

Tests you must be able to discuss

  • Insulation resistance — 500 V / 1000 V megger, what a failing result actually means
  • Continuity and earth — earth fault loop impedance, why it governs disconnection time
  • Cable testing — VLF, tan delta on HV cable
  • Transformer — ratio, winding resistance, vector group, oil
  • Protection relay — secondary injection, pickup and timing verification
  • CT/VT — ratio, polarity, burden, saturation
  • Generator — load acceptance, step load, paralleling, fuel run
  • UPS — transfer, static bypass, battery discharge to autonomy
  • ATS/STS — transfer time, in-phase transfer, failure to transfer

The scenarios that define IST

  • Black building — kill all utility, prove the site rides through on battery then generator, then returns on utility restoration
  • Failure simulation — drop one UPS, one generator, one path, and confirm no load impact
  • Load bank testing — steady state, step load, and thermal soak at design capacity
  • Redundancy proving — remove any single component, load stays up
  • Fuel endurance — full-load generator run to prove tank sizing
Why this matters commercially

IST is the last gate before handover and the most expensive week of the whole project. Engineers who can run it are scarce, and paid accordingly.

11

Software — what to learn, what to skip

You already have AutoCAD, Excel and Bluebeam. That's genuinely most of what a project engineer needs. Resist the urge to collect software.

MUST — do these

  • Excel, to calculation-sheet level — build your own maximum demand, cable sizing and load schedule sheets. This is the learning, not a tool for it.
  • Bluebeam Revu — you have it. Push into revision comparison, takeoffs and markup workflows. Every design review runs on this.
  • MS Project or P6, reading level — you must be able to read a program, find the critical path, and understand float. Reading is enough at Stage 1.

USEFUL — when a role calls for it

  • ELEK Cable Pro Web — AS/NZS 3008 cable sizing, maximum demand, fault loop, protection coordination, with standards references shown. Australian-specific, browser-based, free tier.
  • Navisworks — clash detection and model review. Learn only if you land somewhere BIM-heavy.
  • Power BI — only if you end up in reporting-heavy client-side delivery.

OPTIONAL — later, or never

  • ETAP / DIgSILENT PowerFactory — serious power system modelling. Consultancy design tools. Only worth it if you deliberately pivot to design; not needed for project engineering.
  • Revit — useful in consultancies, largely irrelevant to a contractor-side project engineer.

DON'T — no return for you

  • Learning Revit and ETAP "to be safe." Hundreds of hours, no salary impact on your target path.
  • Any certification course before you can read an SLD. Sequence matters — competence first, credentials later.
  • Programming or scripting. Not what these roles pay for.

09 — Draft an RFI
Draft a Request for Information for this issue on a data centre electrical package.

THE ISSUE: [describe]
DRAWINGS/SPEC AFFECTED: [reference]
WHY IT MATTERS: [describe]

Write it as a professional Australian construction RFI:
- Subject line
- Background — factual, one short paragraph, no blame
- The specific question — singular and unambiguous
- Reference documents
- Our proposed solution (always offer one — it speeds up the response)
- Cost impact: yes/no/TBC
- Program impact: state the required-by date and the consequence of a late response
- Requested response date

Tone: factual, collaborative, but protective of our position. Make the program consequence explicit without being aggressive. Keep it under one page.

11 — Explain protection coordination
Explain the protection coordination in this system. [Attach SLD / protection schedule / curves.]

Walk me through it visually:
1. THE HIERARCHY — draw the protection tree from upstream to downstream as an indented list
2. EACH DEVICE — type, rating, settings, what it's protecting against
3. DISCRIMINATION — for each upstream/downstream pair, does it discriminate? On current, time, or energy? Show the margin.
4. THE FAILURE — where does discrimination break down, and what would trip unnecessarily?
5. DATA CENTRE IMPLICATIONS — a nuisance trip upstream in a critical power system is an outage. Where's the risk here?
6. A sketch description of what the time-current curves should look like

Explain the concept before the terminology. I'm learning this from scratch.

14 — Review a load schedule
Review this load schedule for a data centre. [Attach.]

Check and report on:
1. ARITHMETIC — do the connected loads, diversity factors and maximum demand actually add up? Show any discrepancy.
2. DIVERSITY — are the applied diversity factors appropriate? In a data centre, IT load diversity is near 1.0 — flag anything that assumes otherwise.
3. POWER FACTOR — is it stated and consistent? kW vs kVA errors are the most common fault in these.
4. SPARE CAPACITY — how much headroom, and is it enough for the stated growth plan?
5. REDUNDANCY CHECK — if this is 2N, is each path rated for the FULL load rather than half?
6. MISSING LOADS — what would you expect in a data centre schedule that isn't listed? (Mechanical, lighting, small power, BMS, fire, security, external.)
7. UPSTREAM IMPACT — what transformer, generator and UPS ratings does this schedule imply?

Present as a table of findings with severity.

The six projects

You attempt each one first. I review it, mark it, and only then show you the model answer. Attempting badly and being corrected beats reading a worked solution — every time.

Project 1 · Small LV distribution system WEEKS 3–4

Scenario. A small commercial workshop. 415 V three-phase supply from a 100 A main switch. Loads: 3 × 7.5 kW three-phase motors, 12 kW of single-phase small power, 6 kW lighting, one 15 kW welding outlet.

Deliverables. Load schedule with diversity applied; maximum demand calculation using an AS/NZS 3000 method; a hand-drawn SLD showing the main switch, MCCB/MCB selection and every submain; cable sizing for each submain with all four AS/NZS 3008 checks shown; volt drop verification.

Questions to answer. Which of the four cable checks governed each circuit, and why? What diversity did you apply to the motors and can you justify it? What happens to your maximum demand if the welder is used continuously?

Review criteria. Method correctness over answer correctness. I mark whether you knew which check governs, not whether you got 25 mm² or 35 mm².

Project 2 · Commercial building electrical system WEEKS 6–7

Scenario. Six-storey office, 1,200 m² per floor. Main switchboard in a basement substation fed by a 1,000 kVA transformer. Distribution boards per floor, riser busduct, mechanical services switchboard, essential services.

Deliverables. Full SLD from transformer to floor DBs; busduct sizing; MSB schedule with ACB and MCCB selection including breaking capacities; earthing arrangement (MEN) sketched; essential services separation per NCC; fault level estimate at the MSB.

New skills. Your first fault level calculation. Your first transformer impedance calculation. Your first real ACB selection with an Icu/Ics justification — which you'll find easier than most, given your background.

Project 3 · Generator and ATS system WEEKS 10–11

Scenario. Add standby generation to Project 2's building. Essential load 420 kW. Client wants generator support for essential services plus one lift and the server room.

Deliverables. Generator sizing including step load acceptance and starting kVA for the largest motor; fuel storage sizing for 24 hours at full load; ATS selection and location on the SLD; generator neutral earthing arrangement per AS/NZS 3010; changeover sequence described as a cause & effect matrix; generator switchboard concept.

The trap. Most people size the generator on running kW alone and get caught by motor starting and by non-linear load. If your answer is "420 kW plus 10%", I will fail it and explain why.

Project 4 · UPS and battery system WEEKS 13–14

Scenario. 300 kW of critical IT load requiring 10 minutes autonomy, N+1 UPS configuration.

Deliverables. UPS sizing in kVA and kW accounting for power factor and efficiency; module count for N+1; battery sizing calculation including end-of-life and temperature derating; input and output cable and breaker sizing; static bypass arrangement explained; a battery room ventilation and safety note; how the UPS interacts with the generator during a transfer.

Questions. Why must the generator be sized larger than the UPS output rating? What does the UPS do to the fault level seen downstream? Why does a static bypass need a synchronised source?

Project 5 · Data centre A/B distribution WEEKS 17–18

Scenario. One data hall, 40 racks averaging 12 kW, dual-corded, 2N distribution from UPS output to rack.

Deliverables. A/B SLD from UPS output down to rack PDU; busway vs RPP selection with justification; busway sizing and tap-off arrangement; PDU sizing and count; rack-level power budget; capacity management plan showing per-path loading; a written statement of what fails and what survives for six named failure scenarios.

Review focus. The 50% loading rule. If any path in your design exceeds 50% under normal operation, the design fails — and I'll ask you to explain the consequence before you fix it.

Project 6 · Full data centre electrical architecture WEEKS 21–23

Scenario. Greenfield 5 MW IT load facility in Melbourne's west. 22 kV dual utility supply. Tier III equivalent — concurrently maintainable. You are the electrical project engineer producing the concept design and the delivery approach.

Deliverables. Complete SLD, utility to rack. Full load schedule and maximum demand. Transformer, generator and UPS sizing with calculations. Fault level study at each bus. Protection philosophy and discrimination strategy. Earthing concept. Equipment schedule with long-lead items identified. A commissioning plan L1–L5 with an IST scenario list. A procurement and program strategy naming the critical path. A one-page design basis statement.

This is your portfolio piece. Done properly, it is the thing you bring to interviews. It demonstrates every capability the Stage 1 role requires, and no other candidate at your level will have one.

Interview bank

Ask me to run any of these as a live drill — one question at a time, I score, correct, and escalate. Practise out loud, not in your head.

Technical — expect these

  • Walk me through the power path from the street to a server.
  • What's the difference between N+1 and 2N? Which would you specify and why?
  • Explain concurrent maintainability versus fault tolerance.
  • Why does a data centre need both a UPS and a generator?
  • What's the maximum you'd load each path of a 2N system, and why?
  • How do you size a generator for a data centre? What catches people out?
  • What are the four checks a cable must pass under AS/NZS 3008?
  • What is a Dyn11 transformer and why is the vector group relevant?
  • How does fault level change downstream of a UPS?
  • Explain discrimination. What happens in a critical facility when it fails?
  • What's a Form 4b switchboard and when would you specify one? (Your question — own it.)
  • What does Icw mean and why does it matter for a DC switchboard?

Project & scenario — the ones that decide it

  • The switchboard manufacturer says delivery has slipped six weeks and it will delay commissioning. What do you do? (You have a genuine advantage here — use it.)
  • During IST, dropping UPS A causes a partial load loss. What's your process?
  • The client wants a design change late in construction. Walk me through how you handle it.
  • An installed cable doesn't match the cable schedule. What now?
  • How do you manage an electrical subcontractor who is behind program?
  • Tell me about a technical problem you solved under time pressure.
  • How do you handle being asked a technical question you don't know the answer to?
  • Why are you moving from manufacturing to site delivery?
Your answer to "why the move?"

Have this rehearsed word-perfect. Something close to: "I've spent my career on the equipment that's on the critical path of every data centre build. I know why boards are late, what the type-test evidence actually proves, and what happens at FAT. I want to be on the delivery side, where I can use that to de-risk the whole electrical package rather than one piece of it." That reframes a lateral move as a strategic one.

15

Getting hired

Competence gets you through the interview. Positioning gets you the interview.

CV — the rewrite

  • Retitle yourself. "Electrical Project Engineer — Critical Power Switchgear" beats "Project Engineer".
  • Lead with data centre relevance. If your boards went into data centres, that belongs in the first three lines. If they didn't, lead with critical infrastructure, hospitals, industrial.
  • Quantify everything. Board ratings in amps, project values, number of boards, fault ratings, forms of separation, delivery timeframes.
  • Name the standards. AS/NZS 61439 on your CV is a keyword that recruiters and hiring managers filter on.
  • Put FAT in bold. Then say how many you've witnessed or run.
  • Add the portfolio project once Project 6 is done. "Concept design for a 5 MW Tier III facility" is a legitimate line.

LinkedIn, recruiters, network

  • Headline: "Electrical Project Engineer | Critical Power & LV Switchgear | Data Centre Infrastructure". Recruiters search these exact terms.
  • Post once a fortnight about something you learned in this program — a diagram you drew, a concept explained simply. Low effort, compounding visibility.
  • Pick 3–4 recruiters who actually place DC roles in Melbourne. Tell each one your target title and salary band explicitly. Vague candidates get vague roles.
  • Talk to the contractors you already supply. You have existing relationships with the exact companies you want to work for. This is the single most underused asset you have.
  • Never name your current salary first. Say: "I'm targeting $150–165k plus super for a project engineering role — is that in range?"

The apartment problem

Why "cheapest, closest to the CBD" is usually the wrong buy

  • Capital growth. Land appreciates; buildings depreciate. In an apartment you own a small share of the land, so growth has historically lagged houses badly — in inner Melbourne, apartment values have underperformed detached housing for well over a decade.
  • Supply. Inner-city apartments can be built upward indefinitely. Scarcity is what drives price, and there is none.
  • Owners corporation fees of $3,000–7,000 a year, forever, plus special levies for defects — a live risk in Australian apartment stock.
  • Lending. Many lenders restrict apartments under 50 square metres or in high-density postcodes, which limits both your borrowing and your future buyer's.
  • You are planning children. A one- or two-bedroom apartment has a short useful life for you, and selling within five years usually loses money after duty and costs.

What to buy instead, given your actual situation

  • A house or townhouse on its own title in a middle-ring suburb, 12–20 km out, near a train line. Slower commute, materially better long-run growth, and it works with children.
  • In Melbourne that is a genuinely large set of suburbs at $650k–850k. In Perth the same money buys closer in.
  • Buy for a ten-year hold. With duty, fees and selling costs, anything under about five years is likely to be worse than renting.
  • Do not buy before the city decision is settled. Buying in Perth and moving to Melbourne in two years is the most expensive sequence available — you'd pay duty twice and sell into your own deadline.
  • If your Melbourne move is real, rent there first for six months. It costs a few thousand dollars and prevents a several-hundred-thousand-dollar mistake.

What the waiting period actually is

  • From 1 January 2019, holders of temporary partner visas — subclasses 309 and 820 — are subject to a 104-week newly arrived resident's waiting period for Parental Leave Pay.
  • The clock starts on the later of the date you applied for the visa or the date you arrived in Australia to live.
  • Every day in Australia as an Australian resident counts, including time on a bridging visa. Time spent overseas does not count.
  • Being granted the permanent subclass 100 does not reset the clock — it runs from your original start date. So if you are partway through, you simply finish serving it.
  • You must also meet the work test (330 hours across 10 of the 13 months before the birth) and the income test. Published individual income limits differ by claim year — figures of $168,865 and $180,007 both appear in current sources. VERIFY with Services Australia
What to do with this. Set the "months resident" slider on the left to your real figure. Three traps that are not in the salary comparison

1. The house you sell while non-resident

Since December 2019, foreign residents get no main residence exemption at all, subject to narrow life-events exceptions. The 50% CGT discount is also unavailable for the foreign-residency period after May 2012, and the gain is taxed at non-resident rates — which start at 30% from the first dollar.

On your numbers: a Melbourne house bought for $650k and sold a decade later while you are in Dubai could carry a tax bill of roughly — that would have been zero had you sold as a resident.

The fix is simple and free: sell before you cease Australian tax residency, or keep it and sell after you return. Never sell in between.

2. Ten years of no superannuation

No employer contributions, no concessional cap use, and no compounding on money that never arrives. At your combined incomes that is roughly — a year of contributions forgone, and the compounding loss by age 60 is far larger than the contributions themselves.

You can voluntarily contribute to Australian super while overseas, and if you are serious about going you should — but the deduction is worth little when you have no Australian taxable income to deduct it against.

3. The re-entry cost

Returning at 42 after ten years away means no Australian referral network, no current local reputation, and recency-of-practice expectations to satisfy. Budget 12–18 months to rebuild a caseload — call it — of forgone income on top of everything above.

Keep your Ahpra registration current the entire time. Letting it lapse and re-applying from overseas is slow, expensive and occasionally messy.

GST, and working from home

GST
  • Psychological services to the patient are GST-free health supplies. No GST on therapy fees, and no forced registration at $75k of clinical turnover.
  • Medico-legal and insurer reports, corporate training, supervision to other practitioners and course sales are generally taxable. Register once taxable turnover passes $75,000.
  • The trap: unregistered, you cannot recover GST on room rent, software or equipment. A "$120 plus GST" room actually costs you $132.
Home telehealth room
  • Claim running costs at the ATO fixed rate of 70c per hour worked from home, or use the actual-cost method with records. Depreciate desk, chair and computer separately.
  • No council approval needed where no client attends and there is no signage.
  • Requirements that are real: a lockable room, no audio bleed, secure record storage, a compliant video platform, and a crisis plan for clients in other states.
  • Do not claim occupancy costs (a share of rates, interest, insurance) unless advised to — it can cost you part of the main residence CGT exemption when you sell, which is far larger than the deduction. That interacts badly with a possible Gulf move.

Cash-flow discipline

Rule 1

A separate tax account

Transfer 32% of every private dollar the day it lands. Your first full private year arrives with no PAYG withheld and the bill lands months later.

Rule 2

Expect the double-up year

The year the ATO puts you on PAYG instalments, you may pay last year's tax and this year's instalments together. Budget for it.

Rule 3

Carry-forward super

With a total super balance under $500,000 you can use unused concessional cap from the previous five years. Early in a career that is a large unused allowance — and it is the right tool for a single high-income year later on.

What a practice is worth when you sell it

How buyers price it
  • Australian allied-health practices typically transact on a multiple of normalised earnings before interest and tax — commonly around 1.5–3× for small practices, higher for larger ones with management depth. EST — thin public data
  • "Normalised" means after paying a market salary for the work you do. A practice whose profit is just your own clinical hours has almost no sale value — the buyer would have to replace you.
  • What raises the multiple: contractors on signed agreements, documented systems, a referral base that is not personally yours, a lease that transfers, and clean financials.
  • What destroys it: clients loyal to you personally, no manager, undocumented processes, and a name that is your own name.
Your indicative exit value

The practical implication: if you ever want to sell, build the practice from day one under a business name rather than your own, and get a manager before the fifth clinician. Both decisions are nearly free at the start and nearly impossible to reverse at the end. Small business CGT concessions may substantially reduce tax on a sale — get advice years before, not weeks before.

Your fee — bulk bill or $200?

Neither, quite. Bulk billing pays you the rebate and nothing more — $98.95. After a 30% split that is $69, below the cost of delivering the hour once you count notes, no-shows and overheads. A fully bulk-billed caseload is a subsidy you personally fund, and it anchors your clients and referrers at a price you can never move away from.

Do instead: standard fee $200–220 from day one, two to four genuinely reduced-fee places, and raise the standard fee $10–20 every July. The APS suggested fee for 2026–27 is $330. Almost nobody charges it in a general practice setting, but it shows how much room sits above where you were considering starting.

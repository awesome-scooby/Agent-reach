Burnout maths, priced

You currently see 5–6 clients a day. Sustained, that is 25–30 face-to-face hours a week plus notes. The honest research and the workforce data both point the same way: the practitioners who last three decades sit at 18–22 client hours a week and fill the rest with higher-priced non-clinical work.

Dropping from 6 to 5 sessions a day costs you — a year at your current fee. Replacing that one hour with assessment or medico-legal work at $300+/hr makes it back and reduces emotional load. This is the trade the model rewards, and the one most people never make.

The three domains worth arguing with you about

You said no

NDIS

You want to skip it. On the 2026–27 numbers that is now a costly preference. From 1 July 2026 the NDIS price limit for psychology rose to $252.99/hr — the only therapy discipline to get a rise — and the pricing structure was split into six separate line items so that reports, non-face-to-face work and travel are billed separately rather than absorbed.

That makes it the highest-paying routine work available to a general-registered psychologist with no extra qualification. The real costs are administrative: plan management chasing, longer payment cycles, complex presentations, and documentation. Verdict: don't build a caseload on it; do take a capped 15–25% of your hours. Reassess if the price limit falls.

Underrated

Supervision

Once you have the required post-general experience and Board-approved supervisor training, supervising provisional psychologists pays roughly $150–250/hr (estimate — no public schedule), carries a fraction of the clinical load, is booked in blocks, and is the most recession-proof line in the table because the supply of supervisors is the workforce bottleneck.

It is also the on-ramp to the two highest-leverage things on the ladder: employing juniors, and training. Verdict: plan for it around year 4–5, not year 15.

Overrated

Courses and digital products

Every "scale your psychology practice" pitch ends here. The honest base rate: most clinician-built courses sell to a few dozen people, gross under $20k, and consume 200–400 unpaid hours. They work for people who already have an audience or an institutional channel — a university, a professional body, an employer network.

Verdict: not a 2027–2032 project. Revisit only when you have either (a) a distinctive clinical specialty other clinicians want training in, or (b) a corporate client who will pay you to build it. Selling training to organisations beats selling courses to individuals by a wide margin.

The company myth, plainly. "Set up a Pty Ltd and pay 25% tax" is the most common piece of bad advice given to new private-practice psychologists. It fails on personal services income. A solo psychologist's fee income is a reward for personal effort; unless you satisfy one of the personal services business tests, the income is attributed to you and taxed at your marginal rate whatever the letterhead says. The company earns its keep when you are earning from other people's hours or from products — not from your own clinical hours.

GST — the one where psychology is unusually well treated

GST-free
  • Psychological services supplied to the patient by a recognised professional are GST-free health supplies. No GST on your therapy fees.
  • This means you are not forced to register at $75k of clinical turnover, and you save your clients 10%.
Taxable — watch the $75k line
  • Medico-legal and insurer reports commissioned by a third party, corporate training and consulting, supervision supplied to other practitioners, and course sales are generally taxable.
  • Once taxable turnover passes $75,000 you must register and charge GST on those services.
  • The trap: if you are not registered, the GST on your room rent, software and equipment is a real cost you cannot recover. Add roughly 10% to any quoted rent that is "plus GST" — the model on the left assumes your rent figure is GST-inclusive to you.

Working from home — the two versions people confuse

Version 1 — telehealth from a home office
Recommended
  • You see clients by video from a room in your home. No client ever attends.
  • Claim running expenses: the ATO fixed rate of 70c per hour worked from home covers electricity, gas, internet, phone and stationery, or use the actual-cost method with records.
  • Depreciate the desk, chair and computer separately from the fixed rate.
  • No council approval needed in most cases, because no clients visit and there is no signage or traffic.
  • Requirements that are real: a lockable room, a door that closes, no household audio bleed, secure storage for records, a compliant video platform, and a documented safety plan for clients in crisis in another state.
Version 2 — clients attending your house
Think hard
  • Needs a home occupation / home business permit from your local government under the local planning scheme. Rules on client numbers, hours, parking and signage vary by council.
  • Your home and contents insurer must be told; professional indemnity and public liability must cover the premises.
  • Claiming occupancy costs (a share of rates, mortgage interest, insurance) requires the area to be a genuine place of business — and doing so can cost you part of the main-residence capital gains exemption when you sell. That can dwarf the deduction.
  • Clinically: it collapses the boundary between home and practice at exactly the life stage — a newborn in the house — where you least want that.
  • Verdict: viable as a cheap start, but do it for a defined period with eyes open, not as a permanent model.
Where this actually fits your plan. A home telehealth room is the perfect 2028 asset: it lets you restart earning after the birth in one- and two-hour blocks with zero fixed cost and no commute, at a time when a rented room sitting empty would be bleeding you. Set it up before the birth, while you have time and income.

Cash-flow discipline — the failure mode that actually gets people

Rule 1

A separate tax account

Transfer 32% of every private dollar into a separate account the day it lands. Never spend from it. Your first full year of private income arrives with no PAYG withheld and the bill lands months later.

Rule 2

Expect the double-up year

The year the ATO puts you on PAYG instalments you may pay last year's tax and this year's instalments in the same twelve months. Budget for it or it will feel like a disaster.

Rule 3

Insurance is not optional

Professional indemnity is mandatory for registration. Add income protection — premiums are deductible — before you reduce salaried days. As a contractor, nobody pays you when you are sick.

The caseload question nobody warns you about

What happens to private clients
  • A therapy caseload does not survive a six-month absence. Clients need continuity; they will find someone else and stay with them.
  • Referrers behave the same way. A GP who sends you three referrals and hears you are away for six months routes the next thirty elsewhere — and habits are sticky.
  • Under a room-rent model, rent keeps running while you are away unless the contract says otherwise. Under a split, an empty calendar costs you nothing.
What to do about it
  • Stay on the split through 2027. Zero fixed cost during leave is worth more than the few thousand a rent model saves you that year.
  • Negotiate a pause-and-return clause now — up to 12 months, same days, same room. Ask while they are courting you.
  • Warm handover, not disappearance. Tell clients eight weeks out. Refer within the practice so the referrer relationship stays with the building, and with you.
  • Restart on telehealth from home first. Two to four hours a day, no commute, no room cost, nap-schedule compatible. This is the single most practical return-to-work structure in psychology.

Where the ladder actually breaks

Level 5 is a trap

Own rooms while still solo adds $25k–60k of fixed annual cost and buys you nothing a rented room doesn't, unless you are about to put other clinicians in them. Plenty of psychologists sign a five-year commercial lease, work harder, and earn less. Skip 5 unless it is a deliberate run-up to 6.

Level 6 margins are thinner than people think

The arithmetic: a contractor bills $200k, keeps 65%, so $70k gross margin to you. Take out their room, software, admin share, marketing and your management time and $25k–40k is a realistic net per clinician. Three contractors is roughly $90k of extra income for a second, unpaid, job. It only pays at scale — six or more — or with employed clinicians on salary where the margin is larger and the risk higher.

Level 8 is the underrated one

Corporate mental-health work — training, critical incident response, wellbeing program design, EAP subcontracting — carries day rates several times your clinical hourly, needs almost no capital, and scales by adding associates rather than rooms. Your current EAP employment is the free apprenticeship for it. Most clinicians never notice they're being trained in the exact thing they could later sell.

Phase 9 — geography

Perth, Melbourne, or neither in particular

You've framed this partly as a property play — move to Melbourne's north or west expecting prices to soften until rate cuts late next year. That thesis deserves a direct answer, because it is the part of your plan where the reasoning has the most holes.

The problem with "wait for the rate cuts". Rate cuts are the trigger for the recovery, not a window that stays open after it. When the market prices in cuts, buyer borrowing capacity rises and competition returns before the cuts land — so "buy just before the cuts arrive" is a plan to buy into a rally you have announced in advance. If you genuinely believe Melbourne's north and west are soft, the logically consistent move is to buy while it is soft and everyone else is waiting, not to wait for the signal that ends the softness. Currently the data supports your read on direction — Melbourne dwelling values fell about 1.2% in July 2026 and 3.4% over that quarter (CoreLogic-derived, reported Aug 2026) — but "prices are falling" and "I can time the bottom" are different claims, and only the second one is a plan.
Three more holes worth naming
  • The corridors you named are structurally supply-rich. Melton was Australia's fastest-growing council two years running and Wyndham and Melton are each projected to add well over 150,000 people to 2036. Population growth sounds bullish; combined with unlimited greenfield land release it usually is not. Land supply that expands to meet demand caps capital growth — which is exactly why these corridors have lagged Melbourne's median for a decade. You would be buying affordability, not growth.
  • Your income is the bigger asset than the house. Moving cities in 2028–29 resets your referral network to zero. That is a real cost of roughly one to two years of private-practice income — six figures — against a speculative property gain. Never move a practice for a property trade. Move for a life reason, and buy where you land.
  • The binding constraint is the deposit, not the timing. With savings in the $10k–30k band, "when to buy" is not yet your decision. "How fast can we assemble a deposit and what is our joint borrowing capacity with a baby in 2027" is.
The genuinely useful finding
  • Since 1 October 2025 the Australian Government 5% Deposit Scheme has no income caps and no annual place limits. Eligible first home buyers can buy with a 5% deposit and no lenders mortgage insurance.
  • Property price caps: $950,000 Melbourne and Geelong, $850,000 Perth.
  • Victoria: first home buyers pay no stamp duty up to $600,000, with a sliding concession to $750,000.
  • WA: no duty up to $500,000 with concession to $700,000 metro; the May 2026 state budget proposed lifting these to $600,000 and $800,000, subject to parliament — confirm with RevenueWA before relying on it.
  • What this means: a $600k purchase in Melbourne's west is roughly $30k deposit, no LMI, no stamp duty. That is inside your reach far sooner than you assumed — which makes the real question serviceability during a 2027 parental leave, not market timing.
But: a 95% loan on a market you expect to fall is negative equity by design. If you use the scheme, treat it as a home you will hold for a decade, not a trade — and get pre-approval before, not during, parental leave, because lenders assess on current and evidenced future income.

Your fee — the answer to "bulk bill or $200?"

Neither, quite. Bulk billing under Better Access pays you the rebate and nothing more — $98.95 a session. After a 30% split that is $69, which is below the cost of delivering the hour once you count notes, no-shows and overheads. A full bulk-billed caseload is not a business; it is a subsidy you personally fund. It also anchors your clients — and your referrers — at a price you can never move away from.

Do this instead: set your standard fee at $200–220 from day one, keep two to four genuinely reduced-fee places for people who need them, and raise the standard fee by $10–20 every July. The APS suggested fee for 2026–27 is $330; almost nobody charges it in general practice settings, but it tells you the ceiling is far above where you were considering starting. Starting low and raising later is much harder than starting mid and holding.

The single recommendation

Stay salaried through the 2027 birth. Add one private day on the 70/30 from general registration. Spend this month establishing whether clinical endorsement is open to you — and if it is, start the registrar program the week you are generally registered.

Everything else on this page is a consequence of that sentence. The 70/30-versus-rent question is worth roughly — a year at your projected volume. The endorsement question is worth roughly — a year, every year, for the next thirty-five. You have been giving them equal attention. They are not equal.

The second-order point: your stated goal is financial independence as soon as possible, and the fastest route there for a 27-year-old psychologist is not maximising 2027 income. It is protecting your earning capacity through the family-formation years, buying permanent pricing power while you are young enough to amortise it, and starting to invest the surplus in 2027 rather than 2032. On the modelled path, the difference between starting to invest now and starting at 35 is larger than the difference between any two career strategies on the chart.

Scope

This is a strategic and financial analysis, not personal financial product advice, tax advice or legal advice.

01

Day one: the six things that must exist before anything else

None of this is training. It's the frame that makes training legal and safe. Do it in the first week or the whole plan sits on sand.

⚠
The one to not skip

He is used to a world where working on a live 48 V bus is normal, trained-for practice. Until someone explicitly tells him that the default answer here is isolate, his instincts are calibrated for the wrong energy. Say it out loud in week 1, and say it again the first time you see him near an open board.

02

The exposure ladder

He can be genuinely useful long before he can be licensed. Move him up a rung when he's shown you the one below — not on a calendar.

Rungs 1–3

What you provide

  • Drawings, schedules and standards
  • Someone willing to narrate what they're doing while they do it
  • A finished board and its pack, and permission to mark up a copy

Move him up when: his mark-ups start finding real discrepancies instead of formatting nits.

Rungs 4–5

What you provide

  • A test schedule or ITP to witness against
  • An existing test plan to copy the shape of
  • Ten minutes explaining the pass criteria before the test, not after

Move him up when: he rejects an incomplete test sheet without being asked to.

Rung 6

What you provide

  • Explicit, named authority for a defined scope
  • A licensed person who reports into that scope
  • Clarity on what he still escalates rather than decides

The trap: letting him direct work he hasn't yet checked or witnessed. Skipping rungs is how supervision becomes theatre.

03

What to hand him, week by week

Each block matches a week of the Decoder's 10-week plan. Give him the documents at the start of the week, not when he asks — the reading only works if the real thing is on the desk beside it.

W1

Orientation and scope

modules 00–01
  • The written scope of practice and the named supervising licence holder
  • Org chart — who designs, who checks, who approves, who signs test sheets
  • The house drawing standard / legend sheet / CAD standard
  • Isolation, LOTO and permit procedures
  • One complete drawing pack for a board he can walk up to
WhyThe complete pack is the spine of the next nine weeks. Everything he learns gets hung on it.
W2

Three-phase power

module 02
  • A transformer nameplate — photo or datasheet, with kVA, vector group and Z%
  • Two or three motor nameplates, or a motor schedule with kW / FLC / pf / poles
  • A fault level or short-circuit study from a past job
  • A PFC bank spec or kVAr calculation, if you have one
WhyHe can check every rule of thumb in module 02 against your real plant in an afternoon.
W3

The drawing pack

module 03
  • The same pack again, plus a superseded revision of it
  • A drawing register and one transmittal
  • An example sheet with rev clouds and deltas on it
  • One IFA and one IFC stamped sheet, side by side
WhyComparing two revisions of one drawing teaches revision control faster than any procedure document.
W4

Symbols

module 04
  • Legend sheets from two or three different clients — they never agree
  • A schematic with symbols your shop uses that aren't in the general set
  • Any in-house symbol block library from your CAD system
WhySeeing the same device drawn three ways by three clients is the lesson. It removes the fear that he's "missing the real symbol set".
W5

Colours, MEN and the gear

modules 05–06
  • Your wiring colour standard, written down
  • A complete BOM from a finished board
  • Catalogues: Schneider ComPact NSX, Acti9, TeSys; NHP Terasaki TemBreak
  • Scrap or demo gear he can hold — an MCCB, a contactor, an overload, a CT
WhyMatching a BOM line to a catalogue page to a physical device is what turns part numbers into objects.
W6

Reading an SLD

module 07
  • Three SLDs of increasing size — a small DB, a mid-size MSB, and one with a generator, PFC or HV on it
  • The discrimination study for one of them
  • The maximum demand calculation for one of them
WhyThree SLDs at different scales show him the grammar is identical and only the vocabulary grows.
W7

The DOL schematic

module 08
  • A plain DOL starter schematic
  • A star-delta schematic, ideally from the same job
  • One schematic with a real interlock or safety circuit in it
  • The functional description / control philosophy for that plant
WhyThe functional description is the sentence the ladder is a drawing of. Reading them together is the moment control circuits click.
W8

Schematic to real wires

module 09
  • The wiring schedule and the cable schedule for the same board
  • The label schedule
  • Your ferruling and labelling standard — source-destination, in writing
  • Photos of a good loom, and if you have one, a bad one
WhyThis is the week the source-destination convention has to be nailed down. Coming from potential-numbered drawings, two different ferrules on one wire looks like an error until someone says otherwise.
W9

The numbers and motor control

modules 10–11
  • Cable sizing calculations from a real job, with the AS/NZS 3008 rows used
  • Volt drop calcs for a long run
  • A motor schedule with starter type per motor
  • A VSD parameter sheet from a commissioned drive
WhyRe-deriving one of your own numbers and landing on the same answer is the confidence moment for an engineer.
W10

Control, instruments and the board

modules 12–13
  • An I/O schedule, and a loop drawing if you have one
  • A GA, a gear plate layout and a shipping split drawing
  • Photos of a Form 2, a Form 3b and a Form 4b board, internals open
  • A photo of a compliance / rating plate
WhyForms are almost impossible to learn from a definition and obvious from three photos.

Then, timed to the first real job of each kind

F1

Before his first design review

module 14
  • A completed ITP with hold points signed
  • Routine verification test sheets from a shipped board
  • A FAT report
  • Whatever documents your verified-system claim rests on — the original manufacturer's design verification data, or your own
  • An RFI log and a transmittal history from one job
WhyThis is the paperwork that makes AS/NZS 61439 concrete. It's also where an outsider's eye finds gaps fastest.
F2

Before his first HV switchroom

module 15
  • An HV SLD with the RMU, transformer and protection on it
  • A protection settings schedule
  • A blank or redacted switching program and permit to work form
  • The site's arc flash study summary and label examples
WhyHe needs to understand what the HV crew are doing well before he's ever near it — and to know exactly where the line he doesn't cross is.
F3

Before his first site visit

module 16
  • A switchroom GA and the matching board GA
  • A full cable schedule
  • A lifting plan and delivery method statement
  • A fire-stopping / penetration certificate
WhyOverlaying the two GAs before the visit turns the site walk into a verification exercise instead of a tour.
F4

Before his first commissioning

modules 17–18
  • Completed commissioning test sheets — IR, earth continuity, RCD, phase rotation, functional
  • A punch list from a live job
  • A set of as-built mark-ups and the handover pack index
  • The calibration register and instrument list
WhyReading completed test sheets before witnessing one means he arrives knowing what "good" looks like on paper.
04

What to strip out before you hand anything over

Almost every document above belongs to a client. Two minutes of redaction now is cheaper than a conversation with their legal team later.

Remove

Take these out

  • Client name, logo and site address — including in the title block and the file name
  • Anything commercial: pricing, margins, rates, purchase order values, subcontractor quotes, variation costs
  • Personal data: named individuals' contact details, signatures, licence numbers
  • Security-relevant detail on utility, defence, telecommunications, water, health or other critical infrastructure sites — SLDs and switchroom layouts are exactly what you don't circulate
  • Live access credentials — PLC passwords, VSD parameter passwords, SCADA logins that sometimes ride along in commissioning notes
Keep

Leave these in

  • Drawing numbers, sheet numbers, revision letters and dates — the revision story is half the lesson
  • All technical content: ratings, cable sizes, tags, wire numbers, settings
  • Drawn / checked / approved initials — they teach him who to ask
  • The standard references and the Form / IP notes in the border

Don't over-redact. A drawing with the ratings blanked out teaches nothing.

Check first

Before you copy anything

  • Does the contract or NDA for that job allow internal training use? Most do; some explicitly don't.
  • Does your IP policy allow drawings on personal laptops or phones? Assume no unless it says yes.
  • Is there an internal reference or demo job you own outright? If so, use that first and save the client work for later weeks.
Best option

Build one teaching pack

If redaction is going to be a recurring argument, spend half a day once: take a completed, non-sensitive job, redact it properly, and save it as TRAINING-PACK-01.

It pays for itself the second time you hire someone, and it doubles as the example you show clients when they ask what your documentation looks like.

ℹ
A quiet upside

Redacting a pack forces someone to open every sheet of it. More than one shop has found a missing schedule, a superseded sheet still in circulation, or an unanswered RFI while preparing one for a new starter.

05

The 20-minute weekly check-in

Same four questions every week, plus one that's specific to what he just covered. Twenty minutes, standing up, in front of a board if you can.

Every week

The four standing questions

  1. What's one thing you couldn't explain this week? — the most useful question on this page. If the answer is "nothing", he isn't reading properly or isn't comfortable saying so.
  2. Show me something on a real drawing you can now read. — make him point at it. Recognition is not the same as reading.
  3. What did the Friday task turn up? — the Decoder's weekly at-work task. If he skipped it, the week didn't happen.
  4. Did you see anything that looked wrong or unsafe? — a new pair of eyes catches things. Make it explicitly safe to answer, or he'll stop looking.

06

Signals — is it landing?

Going well

What progress actually looks like

  • His questions get more specific — from "what's this symbol" to "why is this a D curve and that one a C".
  • He starts finding real discrepancies: a BOM line that doesn't match the SLD, a label schedule that disagrees with the drawing, an RFI answer that never made it back onto a sheet.
  • He reaches for the drawing before the person, then asks a sharper question.
  • People on the floor start explaining things to him unprompted. That's the real signal.
  • He raises an RFI rather than guessing.
Watch out

Red flags, and what they usually mean

  • "It's all making sense" with no questions → he's reading passively. Ask him to point at something on a real drawing.
  • Skipping the Friday task two weeks running → the plan has become homework. Do the next one with him.
  • Reaching for tools — even helpfully, even trivially → the telecom instinct hasn't been recalibrated. Stop and repeat the scope conversation, once, plainly.
  • Confident about ratings he hasn't checked → engineers from adjacent fields can pattern-match too fast. Ask him where the number came from.
  • Quiet for three weeks → usually means he's embarrassed about something basic. The four standing questions exist for exactly this.
★
What to give him as soon as he's ready

The highest-value early job for a drawings-literate engineer who can't yet touch anything: own the drawing-pack consistency check — BOM against SLD, label schedule against drawing, revisions consistent across every sheet, RFI answers actually incorporated, ITP signed at each hold point. It's within scope, it's real work, and most shops are quietly short of someone doing it.

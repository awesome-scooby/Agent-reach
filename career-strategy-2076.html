Diagnosis: where you actually stand

Finding 01

Authority, not credentials

Accredited degree, a Master's, seven years, $5–20M of owned scope. Every PM job ad screens for cost ownership and people. You have neither. Adding a fourth qualification is adding a lock to a door that was never locked.

An OEM sells a product, so its project roles are order-fulfilment roles. Estimated ceiling $150–175k. You will never touch the contract, the margin or a site. Two more years is fine. Five is career-defining.

Finding 03

You are underpaid, and it is a symptom

$110–140k for this profile in Perth is roughly $20–40k under market (estimate). Underpayment at your capability level is almost always a self-advocacy problem, not a market problem.

WAY 02

Assumption test: the electrician question

You want the trade ticket as job-security insurance. As insurance it is priced absurdly, and it does not cover the risk you actually face.

The mandatory question for any 1,000–3,000 hour commitment is not "is this useful?" — almost everything is useful. It is "what else could these hours have bought?" Here is that comparison, honestly.

Verdict — Option D

Do not do the apprenticeship. Take Option D: buy the practical competency without the ticket.

Roughly 4 years and 6,000–8,000 hours, with $150–250k of forgone income, to hedge the scenario "electrical engineering roles dry up for an Australian citizen with an accredited degree during the largest grid and electrification build-out in the nation's history." That is close to the least likely macro outcome available. And the policy does not even pay out: if engineering collapsed, you would be a 36-year-old first-year sparky competing on wage with 21-year-olds. Meanwhile the course consumes precisely the years 31–35 in which the PM transition has to happen — so it increases the risk it was bought to cover.

What Option D actually looks like (~200 hours, not 6,000)

  • A restricted electrical licence / EWP or equivalent competency unit if your state offers one relevant to test and inspection.
  • AS/NZS 3000 and AS/NZS 3008 read properly, not skimmed — plus AS 61439 for assemblies, which you should already own.
  • Two weeks deliberately spent on the factory floor and at site commissioning, wiring and terminating alongside the techs. Ask your manager; it costs the business nothing.
  • HV switching / HV operator training and an LV/HV rescue and safe-work ticket. High credibility per hour, genuinely useful, and cheap.
  • Protection relay fundamentals (SEL / SIPROTEC / Micom hands-on). This is the single highest-credibility technical skill available to you, and it is scarce.

That package costs perhaps $4–8k and 200 hours, and it closes the credibility gap almost as well as a trade ticket does — because what earns respect on site is knowing the standard and the kit, not holding the licence.

The property assumption

You raised the Melbourne move as a housing decision. It is a career decision wearing a housing costume, and it deserves the same test.

Verdict — Option C, then B

Your relative-value read on Melbourne is defensible; your vehicle is wrong. You do not have to live in an asset to own it. Option A's one-off ~$45k of first-home benefits is erased by the career differential in year one. And borrowing capacity is a function of income: buying at $125k locks you into the $550k tier — the exact tier your own thesis does not cover. Buying at 33–34 on $200–230k opens the $850k–1M tier, which is the part of Melbourne that is genuinely mispriced. Defer 18–24 months, execute the employer move, then buy as an investment from a materially stronger balance sheet. Verify all concession figures with a broker and the Victorian SRO — those rules change.

WAY 08

Certifications and education

You already hold a Master's in Engineering Management. That is roughly 70% of what an MBA would give you. You do not need another degree — you need three specific certificates and a licence to sign things.

The short answer

Do: CPEng/NER (2027–28), AS 4000/4902 contract training (now), a cost-control or PMI-SP-adjacent scheduling capability (2027), AIPM CPPP/CPPM as the Australian-recognised PM credential, and eventually FIDIC if the Gulf plan proceeds. Skip: a second Master's, an MBA before 40, PRINCE2 in an Australian resources context, and any short course whose main output is a LinkedIn badge.

WAY 09

Controller vs. Manager vs. Director

The most important comparison in this document, because the two questions have different answers.

Target next

Project Engineer → Senior Project Engineer, on the owner or head-contractor side, with explicit cost ownership.

Not Project Controller, and not yet PM. Here is why: Project Controls is a superb skill and a mediocre destination — it is the most automatable role in this table, it caps around $220–260k, and controllers rarely convert to Project Director because they never carry delivery accountability. Meanwhile jumping straight to PM from an OEM with no cost or contract exposure usually means taking a small, low-visibility PM role at a small company — a title without career capital. The senior PE seat on the owner side gets you budget, contract exposure and a major project on the CV, and it converts to PM in 18–30 months at a much higher band. Learn controls; do not become controls.

Ultimate destination

Project Director — then owner of a specialist delivery or advisory business, on your own timing.

Project Director is the highest-paid role reachable from your starting position through employment alone, it is the most AI-resilient seat in the table (accountability cannot be automated), and it is the only one that generates the client relationships a business can later be built on. Engineering Manager is a comfortable trap: high status, lower pay, and a narrowing path. Commercial Manager pays similarly to PD and is more AI-resilient still, but it is a harder switch from where you sit — so treat commercial capability as something you acquire en route to PD rather than a destination you chase.

WAY 16

Future-proofing to 2076

Which of your skills appreciate, which get commoditised, and which get automated outright. Position yourself where accountability, physical assets and relationships intersect — that is the part machines cannot take.

Where to stand

The durable position is: accountable for physical electrical infrastructure that carries a contract and a deadline, in a sector with a multi-decade capital pipeline. Machines will do the takeoffs, the schedules, the progress reports, the document control and most of the estimating. They will not sign a variation, take the risk, front an angry client, or be liable when the substation does not energise. Every hour you invest in the accountable half appreciates; every hour in the reporting half depreciates. Your current job is roughly 70% in the depreciating half. That, not automation itself, is the risk.

WAY 17

The barbell

A highly employable core, plus a small allocation to high-variance upside. Not a business today — a business when the conditions below are actually met.

The mistake most engineers make is starting a business at 34 with no client relationships, no capital and no reputation, and calling it entrepreneurship. It is not; it is buying yourself a low-paid job with extra risk. The correct sequence is to accumulate the three assets a business runs on — relationships, reputation and capital — inside someone else's balance sheet, and only then to leave.

The trigger conditions

Go independent when all four are true, not before: (1) three or more clients would hire you personally by name, without your employer's brand; (2) you have delivered a project above $50M and can point to it; (3) you hold 12–18 months of living expenses in liquid savings; and (4) inbound work is arriving that you are currently turning down. On the current plan those converge around 2038–2044, age 43–49. Every year you start before that, you pay for in equity and stress; every year you delay past it, you pay for in foregone upside.

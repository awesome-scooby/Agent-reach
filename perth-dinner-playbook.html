The quiet host's playbook

You don't need to be the loudest person in your own kitchen. You need to be the one who listened properly, remembered the details, and made everyone feel welcome. That's a real advantage — this plan is built around it.

Tonight mode strips this down to the one-page cheat sheet. Use it on your phone.

What I assumed — correct anything that's wrong

  • 6 guests, likely 3 couples, met through your wife. Adults only or kids playing separately.
  • ~4 hours, evening, at your home. Food and drinks; maybe a game or the backyard later.
  • You've met few or none of them properly before.
  • You're an Electrical / Project Engineer; you want to learn from people — plus career leads, side-hustle ideas, property/investing thinking, and local Perth know-how.
  • You and your wife will likely see these people again — this is episode one of a series, not a one-off. That changes everything below: you are playing a long game, so nothing has to happen tonight.

01

Your objectives tonight

Five goals, ranked. Numbers 1–3 are the evening. Numbers 4–5 are bonuses that only happen because 1–3 worked. Notice what is not on this list: job leads, business deals, investment tips. Those are the harvest, and you don't harvest on planting day.

1

Learn one real thing from every single guest

Not small talk — one specific thing about their world you could not have found on Google. How a mine shutdown actually gets scheduled. What a nurse sees at 3am. Why their business nearly folded in 2020. This is your stated goal, and it's a good one: it makes you a great listener, which makes people like you, which produces everything else.

Success looks likeTomorrow morning you can name six things you didn't know yesterday.
2

Make each person feel genuinely liked

Trust is the engine. Nothing on this page works without it — not the friendships, not the career leads, not the second invitation. People decide whether they like you in the first two minutes and confirm it in the last two. Warm door, warm goodbye, full glasses, remembered names.

Success looks likeAt least three people say "we should do this again" without you raising it first.
3

Get one proper one-to-one with every guest

Ten minutes each, away from the group. This is where a quiet person wins — you're average in a loud group of seven and excellent one-on-one, so engineer the one-on-ones. Hosting gives you free reasons to do it: drinks, the barbecue, showing someone the backyard, walking someone to their car.

Success looks likeYou know all six people's jobs, how they got there, and one thing each is currently dealing with.
4

Leave exactly two doors open

Not favours. Doors — a small, specific, easy next thing with two people. "I'll send you that podcast." "Come look at the shed when you're next around." "We should do the Margaret River thing." A door costs the other person nothing, which is why they walk through it.

Success looks likeTwo follow-up messages you can send within 48 hours that don't feel like networking.
5

Practise the ratio: listen 60, talk 40

You're quiet, so the risk isn't talking too much — it's the opposite. Pure listening feels like an interview and quietly makes people uneasy. The 40% is a target to hit, not a limit. Every three questions you ask, offer one thing about yourself unasked.

Success looks likeYou told at least four short stories about yourself, unprompted, and none lasted over a minute.

Guest intelligence map

Fill these in before they arrive — even guesses. Your wife can fill most of it in five minutes; ask her tonight. What you write here saves ("saved on this device"), so you can update it after the evening while it's fresh.

Who to invest your time in
  • Anyone in mining, resources, construction, utilities or government. Closest to your world as an Electrical/Project Engineer — the intel is directly usable and you'll have real things to say back, which makes you interesting rather than interviewing.
  • Anyone who runs their own business, even a small one. Highest density of everything you asked for — money, opportunity, industry knowledge, how Perth actually works — and business owners genuinely enjoy being asked about it. This is your best single conversation of the night.
  • The quietest guest in the room. Lowest competition, highest friendship return, and the person most like you. Almost nobody will talk to them properly. You will, and they'll remember it.
  • The women, not just the men. The invitation came through your wife, and the next invitation will too. If the wives enjoy the night, this becomes a regular thing — which is worth more than any single conversation. Don't retreat into a men-only huddle.
Where quiet hosts lose the night
  • Over-investing in the loudest guest. They're easy to talk to because they need nothing from you. They're also the person you'll learn the least from, and they'll be fine without you.
  • Hiding in the kitchen. The classic quiet-host move — real jobs that look like hosting but are actually avoidance. Prep everything before they arrive so the kitchen isn't available as a bolt-hole.
  • Sitting in the same seat for three hours. If you don't move, you'll talk to two people all night. Change position every time food or drinks change.
  • Talking mostly to the one person whose job is nearest yours. Comfortable, and a waste — you already know that world.

03

The evening, hour by hour

Built for roughly 6:30pm to 10:30pm — slide the times to fit. The single most important line in this whole section is at 9:00pm: the wind-down is when people get honest. Most hosts are tidying up by then. Don't be.

T−45
45 min · before anyone arrives

Set the room so you don't have to work it

DoFinish all food prep. Anything still cooking at 7pm is a hiding place. Drinks visible and self-serve — an esky or an open fridge shelf means guests help themselves and each other, which starts conversations without you.
DoKill the overhead lights, use lamps. Music on, at the level where people lean in slightly — quiet enough that nobody has to raise their voice. Mozzie coils out if the back door's open.
DoCreate two seating zones, not one — a table and a couch/patio cluster. Two zones is what makes one-on-one conversation possible later. One big circle forces group-only talk all night, which is your worst format.
Ask wifeFive minutes, out loud: names, who's married to who, jobs, kids' names, anything sensitive to avoid (a redundancy, an illness, a family thing). Write it in your phone. This is the highest-value five minutes of your whole night.
SelfOne line to yourself: My job tonight is to be interested, not interesting. That's the whole strategy. It takes the pressure off completely.
AvoidRehearsing clever things to say. It never survives contact and it makes you stiff for the first hour.
6:30
2 min per group · at the door

Arrival & welcome

SayHey — come in, come in. I'm [name]. So good to finally meet you, [wife] talks about you both. Then immediately: What can I get you — beer, wine, something soft?
Key moveSay their name back within five seconds of hearing it. Dave — good to meet you, Dave. Then use it once more before you leave them. This one habit will make you seem far more confident than you feel.
Key moveTake something off them — the wine, the dish, their jacket. Hands-free guests relax faster. Then physically walk them to another guest and introduce with a hook (see the scripts).
AskOnly easy things at the door: Did you find us alright? · Where'd you come from — is that far?
AvoidStarting a real conversation at the door. You'll get interrupted by the next knock and it'll feel like a brush-off. Park it: I want to hear about that properly — grab me in a bit.
TransitionCome through, I'll grab you a drink. Movement is always the easiest transition. Nobody notices you leaving if you're both walking.
6:45
30–40 min

Drinks & mingle — the shallow end

Your jobNot depth. Circulation. Two to four minutes with everyone, so that by 7:15 you've exchanged real words with all six people and nobody is standing alone. Depth comes at 8:30 — trying for it now is the classic mistake.
AskWarm-ups only: how they know your wife · what suburb they're in and why · kids and ages · been anywhere good lately · how long they've been in Perth.
SayGive something back every time. We're in [suburb] — we picked it for the schools and now I do the freeway every morning and question everything. Small self-deprecation is the fastest trust-builder there is.
Key moveThe 30-second rule: nobody stands alone for more than 30 seconds. Sweep the room every few minutes. Rescuing someone from standing alone earns you more goodwill than any clever thing you could say.
AvoidLeading with so what do you do? in the first minute. Get to it, just not first — it makes the evening feel like a conference. Let it come up naturally, or ask it third.
TransitionLet me top you up · I'd better check the barbecue · Come and meet Sam, you two will get on. All three are unarguable.
7:20
60–75 min · at the table

Food & group conversation

Your jobTraffic control, not performance. A host isn't the best talker — they're the one who makes sure everyone gets a turn. Watch for the person who's been quiet for ten minutes and hand them the floor.
SayThe single most useful sentence a host owns: Priya, you'd know about this — didn't you say you'd done exactly that? Passing the ball costs you nothing and makes you the reason the night flows.
SayAsk the table one story question so the night has a spine: Okay — worst job anyone's ever had. I'll go first, and mine's bad. Going first makes it safe for everyone else. Group storytelling is where quiet people get liked without having to hold the floor.
AskCareers surface here naturally. Use the day-to-day question rather than the job title: What does that actually look like day to day? I never know what those job titles really mean.
AvoidMoney, politics, religion, property-prices-as-a-moral-issue, and correcting anyone's technical facts in front of the group. Being right in public costs more than it pays — always.
TransitionClearing plates is your legitimate excuse to break the table up. Leave those — grab a seat outside, it's nice out there. Breaking one big group into two small ones is the move that unlocks the next hour.
8:30
40–50 min · the important hour

The one-to-one window

Your jobThis is the whole evening for you. Everything before it was setup. Aim for three or four proper one-on-ones of about ten minutes. This is where a quiet person is genuinely better than a loud one — you don't have to compete for airtime, and you can actually go deep.
How to get oneUse a task as cover. Come give me a hand with the drinks · Come see the backyard, I want to show you something · I'm going out for some air, come with. Nobody refuses, and nobody notices it was deliberate.
AskNow you go two levels deeper. How did you end up in that? Was it the plan? → Would you do it again? → What's the part people don't realise? This trio, in this order, gets you a person's whole working life in about eight minutes.
Ask (once)Your opportunity question, dropped in casually, never twice: What's the thing everyone in your industry complains about and just puts up with? That's where businesses come from — and it's a polite question, because you're asking about their world, not your idea.
SayTrade, don't extract. After two of their answers, give one of yours — short. If you only take, they'll feel it by minute six even if they can't name why.
AvoidPitching yourself, hinting that you're looking for work, asking for an introduction, or offering to solve the problem they just described. All of it can wait, and all of it works better in two weeks.
TransitionEnd on a hook, not a fade: I want to hear the rest of that — I'm going to go check on everyone, but don't let me forget. Leaving with a promise is what makes them seek you out later.
9:15
30–45 min

The fun bit — game, backyard, music

Your jobLet go of the plan. Everything strategic is finished. This hour is where people decide whether they actually like you, and that's decided by whether you can enjoy yourself, not by anything you say.
Key movePick a game that makes people talk, not compete. Cards, a quiz, or a simple "two truths" round works far better than anything with teams and winners. Competitive games can sour a room of near-strangers fast.
SayBe the first to look silly at whatever it is. A host who's willing to lose badly and laugh gives everyone else permission to relax.
AvoidForcing an activity nobody's asked for. If the conversation is flowing, the conversation is the activity. Never interrupt a good room with a scheduled game.
10:00
20–30 min · the honest hour

The wind-down — most hosts waste this

Your jobDo not start cleaning. This is when the good conversations happen — the group has thinned, the guard is down, and people say the true version of what they said at 7pm. The best thing you'll learn all night gets said between 10 and 10:30.
AskLate-night questions only work now: What's something you know now that you wish someone had told you at 25? · What was the hardest year? · Would you do it all again?
SayPlant the door: We should do this again — do you two play cards? · Next one's at yours, I'm not doing all this washing up twice. Light, joking, and it makes a second evening feel already agreed.
AvoidYawning, checking the time, stacking the dishwasher. Guests read all three instantly and you'll lose the best half-hour of the night.
10:30
2 min each · at the door

The goodbye — the bit they'll actually remember

Why it mattersPeople remember beginnings and endings, and endings more. Two well-spent minutes here outweigh an hour in the middle. Walk them out. Every time.
SayUse the name and one specific detail from the night: Dave — really good to meet you. And seriously, send me that thing about the Pilbara job, I want to read it. Specific beats warm. Specific is warm.
NumbersAttach the swap to a reason, never to a relationship: What's your number — I'll send you that link. Nobody has ever refused that. Text them the thing before you go to bed, so your name is already in their phone.
AvoidVague goodbyes (yeah we should catch up sometime). Everyone says it, nobody means it, and it makes the real version harder to say later.
11:00
10 min · before bed, non-negotiable

The question bank

Don't memorise these. Read them once now, and know that the button below exists if your head goes blank at 8:40pm. The starred ones are the highest-return questions on the page.

Tap when you're stuck. It pulls at random from every question below.

A Warm-ups that don't feel like an interview

First 40 minutes only. The goal is comfort, not information.

  • How do you two know [wife's name]?Perfect opener. Safe, flattering to your wife, and it tells you the whole social map of the room in one answer.
  • Did you find us alright? Where'd you come from — is that a hike?Pure warm-up. Never fails, expects nothing.
  • How long have you been in Perth? What brought you over?In Perth this is a genuinely rich question — half the room has a story attached to it.
  • What suburb are you in? What made you pick there?The "why" turns a boring fact into a story about their life stage, budget, family, commute.
  • Have you been anywhere good lately? We're trying to work out where to go next.Travel is the most reliable warm topic in Australia. Asking for a recommendation makes them the expert, which people love.
  • Have you got kids? What ages?Standard and safe at a couples' dinner. Follow with "what are they into?" rather than school questions, which can be loaded.
  • Are you a summer person or a winter person? Perth punishes you either way.Light, opinionated, zero stakes. Good for reviving a conversation that's flatlining.
  • What have you got on this weekend?Better than "how was your week" — it points forward, and it surfaces hobbies without asking about hobbies.

B Career & occupation

The trick is never the first question — it's the second one. "What do you do?" gets a job title. The questions below get a person.

  • What does that actually look like day to day? I never really know what those job titles mean.The single best career question in existence. It's humble, it's honest, and it gets you a real answer instead of a LinkedIn headline.
  • How did you end up in that? Was it the plan, or did it just happen?Almost nobody planned their career. This question gives them permission to tell the true, messier, far more interesting version.
  • What's the part of the job people don't realise?Everyone has an answer ready for this and almost nobody gets asked. Expect the best 90 seconds of the conversation.
  • Is it busy at the moment, or has it gone quiet?The most useful question you'll ask all night. It reads industry health, hiring appetite and workload in one polite sentence — and it sounds like small talk.
  • Who's the best person you've worked with, and what did they do differently?Tells you what "good" means in their field. Also the most flattering possible way to ask what skills actually matter.
  • If you were starting again today, would you go the same route?Opens regrets, alternatives, and where they think the field is heading — without asking any of those directly.
  • What's changed most in the last five years?Their industry's real trend line, from someone inside it. Far better than any article.
  • Is AI actually showing up in your work yet, or is it still mostly noise?The "or is it still noise" half is what makes it good — it gives them room to be sceptical instead of forcing enthusiasm.
  • Where do people in your field usually end up — do they move up, move out, or go out on their own?Career progression and earning ceiling, asked structurally. Nobody feels probed.
  • Is it well paid for what it asks of you?How to raise money without asking a number. Most people answer generously and specifically, because you asked about fairness rather than salary.

C Money, business & opportunity

Rules: one-on-one only, after 8:30pm, once per person, never twice in a night. Ask about their world, never about your idea. The moment this feels like research, you've lost them.

  • What's the thing everyone in your industry complains about and just puts up with?The best business question there is, and it doesn't sound like one. Every real business lives inside an answer to this. You're asking them to vent, which people enjoy.
  • Have you ever thought about doing your own thing?Works on employees and owners alike. Employees tell you their dream; owners tell you the war story. Both are gold.
  • Is yours the kind of industry where people start their own outfit, or is it too capital-heavy?Sounds like curiosity about them. Actually maps barriers to entry.
  • Do you know anyone who's made a side thing actually work? I'm curious what's realistic versus internet nonsense.Asking about a third party removes all pressure — and the scepticism in "internet nonsense" signals you're not about to pitch them.
  • Did you buy or rent when you first got here? Everyone tells me something different about Perth.The natural, non-nosy doorway into property. "Everyone tells me something different" invites an opinion instead of a disclosure.
  • What do you reckon about the Perth market at the moment?Opinion, not personal finances. Safe, and everyone in WA has a take.
  • How did you get into that?Your universal response to any mention of an investment, a business or a side venture. Never "how much" — always "how did you start".
  • If someone gave you six months and no salary, what would you build?A late-night question that's fun rather than financial. Reveals more about a person than an hour of career talk.
  • What's the best money you've ever spent?Money question that isn't about wealth. Usually gets a story about a trip, a tool, a course, or a decision.

D Life lessons

After 9:30pm, one-on-one, and only with someone you've already laughed with. Ask these too early and they land as intense.

  • What's something you know now that you wish someone had told you at 25?The single best late-night question. It works on absolutely everyone and the answer is always worth hearing.
  • What was the hardest year, and what got you through it?Deep, but safe — it's framed around recovery rather than the wound. Only for the wind-down, and only if they've opened up first.
  • What made you move to Perth? Would you ever go back?For anyone who migrated. Almost always the richest story they have, and it's rarely asked with real interest.
  • What's the best decision you've made in the last five years?Positive, easy to answer, and it surfaces what they actually value.
  • What did your parents do? Did that push you towards it or away from it?Two generations of story in one question, and it explains a person's whole approach to work.
  • What's something you were completely sure about at 20 and have totally changed your mind on?Invites honesty and humility, and gives you an easy story to trade back.
  • What's the thing you're most glad you said no to?An unusual angle nobody expects. Often gets the most thoughtful answer of the night.

E Perth & Australian opportunities

Ask these as a newer local wanting to understand the place, not as someone scouting. The framing does all the work.

  • Where's the work actually at in Perth right now, honestly?"Honestly" gives them permission to tell you the real version instead of the news version.
  • Is it still all mining, or is that shifting? I keep hearing both.Invites a real opinion on WA's economy from someone living in it. Great for anyone in resources, construction or government.
  • What's growing here that nobody's really talking about yet?Flatters their judgement and gets you genuinely non-obvious information.
  • If you were new here, who would you make sure you knew?Asks about networks without asking for an introduction. Note the names. Do not ask to meet them tonight.
  • Are you part of any clubs or groups? We're trying to meet more people outside work.Honest, slightly vulnerable, and often produces an actual invitation on the spot. Say "we" — it's a couple's question, not a networking one.
  • What's something you still can't find anyone decent to do, here?Unmet demand, stated out loud by a customer. This is where a service business hides, and it lands as a normal Perth gripe.
  • Best thing you've discovered since living here?Ends any Perth conversation on a warm note, and you'll get somewhere good to take your wife.
  • What's the thing you wish someone had told you when you first got to WA?Practical local intelligence — schools, trades, banks, suburbs — from someone who learned it the hard way.

F Questions that pull out stories

These are your communication practice. Stories are what make an evening feel good, and prompting one is easier than telling one.

  • What's the strangest thing that's ever happened to you at work?Every trade, every industry, every shift-worker has one. Instantly makes a group conversation good.
  • What's the worst job you've ever had?The best table question in the world. Everyone can play, everyone's is funny, and nobody has to be impressive.
  • How did you two meet? I want the real version, not the short one.Couples love this and both of them talk — which doubles your return and makes them feel like the centre of the night.
  • What's the closest you've come to a proper disaster on the job?Perfect for tradies, medics, miners and engineers. You'll have one to trade back, which makes it an exchange rather than an interview.
  • What's a hill you'd die on? Something completely trivial."Completely trivial" is the safety catch — it keeps it fun and steers well clear of politics.
  • Have you got a hidden talent nobody would guess?Reliably surprising, and it gives quiet guests something easy and flattering to contribute.
  • What's the most Perth thing that's ever happened to you?A local in-joke that includes everyone. Works on lifers and newcomers alike.
The four phrases that keep a story going

You need far fewer questions than you think. When someone gives you a short answer, don't reach for a new question — reach for one of these. They do 80% of the work of being a good conversationalist:

“How so?”  ·  “Then what happened?”  ·  “What did you do?”  ·  “That must have been ___.”

The last one is the strongest. Naming the feeling — "that must have been terrifying" — is what turns a factual answer into a real conversation, and it's the thing people remember you for.

05

Your strategy: be the one who remembered

You will not out-talk the loud guest, and you shouldn't try. Being remembered has almost nothing to do with volume — it comes from specificity. The person who says "how did the Karijini trip go?" three weeks later beats the person who told the best joke, every time.

Talk vs listen

Aim for 40% you, 60% them

Most advice tells quiet people to listen more. That's wrong for you — you already do. Your actual risk is under-sharing, which reads as guarded and slowly makes people uncomfortable without them knowing why.

The rule: three questions, one offer. After every third question, volunteer something about yourself that nobody asked for. Keep it to three sentences. That single habit converts "polite interviewer" into "someone I got on with".

Curiosity without interrogation

Go two deep, then hand back

Interrogation isn't caused by hard questions — it's caused by unreciprocated ones. Three in a row with nothing from you and it becomes an interview no matter how warm you are.

The pattern: question → follow-up → follow-up → your own bit → pause. The pause matters. Let a silence sit for two seconds; people fill it with the most interesting thing they've said yet.

Remembering names

Four steps, and it's a solved problem

  1. Say it back in five seconds. "Dave — good to meet you, Dave."
  2. Attach a picture. Dave–drives the ute. Priya–pharmacy. Absurd is better; it sticks harder.
  3. Use it once more before you leave that conversation.
  4. Write all six names in your phone at the first bathroom break. This is legal, invisible, and it works.

And if you lose one: "I'm so sorry — remind me of your name? I'm hopeless with them." Nobody has ever minded. Pretending, and getting caught at 10pm, is much worse.

Finding common ground

Hunt adjacent, not identical

You won't share a job with these people. You don't need to. Work down the ladder until something clicks: place → family stage → work rhythm → hobby → gripe.

"Work rhythm" is the underrated one for you: early starts, being on call, sites, deadlines, being away from family. A nurse on nights and an engineer on shutdowns have far more in common than either expects.

And shared complaints bond faster than shared loves. Freeway traffic, the cost of everything, tradies who don't call back — instant, harmless allies.

Talking about yourself

The three-sentence rule

1. The plain answer. 2. One bit of colour or a joke at your own expense. 3. Hand it straight back.

"I'm an electrical engineer — mostly project side, so I spend a lot of time making sure things get built the way they were drawn. Which mainly means I'm the person on site saying 'that's not what the drawing says.' What about you — are you hands-on or is it all emails now?"

Twelve seconds. Says what you do, makes you human, and returns serve. That's the whole formula, and it works for every question about yourself all night.

Making people comfortable

Comfort is physical before it's verbal

  • Never let a glass sit empty. The most powerful hosting move, and it requires no conversational skill at all.
  • Give people jobs. "Can you grab the salad?" A guest with a task is a guest who has stopped feeling like a guest.
  • Introduce with a hook, never just names. It hands two strangers their first topic.
  • Go first. First to admit the bad job, the mistake, the thing you don't know. It sets the depth for the whole room, and it costs you nothing.
Joining a group

Arrive with a reason, react before you speak

Approach the open side of the circle. Stand there for twenty seconds without saying anything — laugh where they laugh, nod. Then react to what's being said rather than starting something new.

Never open with what are you guys talking about? — it stops the conversation and makes them re-explain. As the host you also have the world's best entry ticket: arrive carrying something. A bottle, a plate, a question about food. Instant, welcome, unquestioned.

Leaving a conversation

Warm reason plus a gift

Never drift off. Close it properly, and leave something behind so they want the next round:

"I'm going to go rescue the barbecue before I ruin dinner — but grab me later, I want to hear how that ended."

Reason + compliment + open loop. As the host you can leave any conversation at any moment, guilt-free. Use that privilege — it's the biggest advantage you have tonight.

When you run out of things to say

It will happen, probably around 7:40pm. It is not an emergency and nobody else notices it as much as you do. Three moves, in order:

1 · Go back

Reach for something they said earlier. You said before that you'd nearly moved to Melbourne — what stopped you? This is the best of the three, because it proves you were listening.

2 · Go wide

Use the room. The food, the music, the weather, the drive over, someone else's story. Have you tried the — actually hang on, have you eaten? Come here.

3 · Go host

Top up a drink, fetch a plate, introduce them to someone. A task resets the conversation with no awkwardness whatsoever. This is your escape hatch, and it's always available.

And the thing to actually believe: a silence between two people who have eaten together is not awkward. It only feels that way from the inside. Let a few of them happen tonight — you'll notice nobody else flinches.

06

Your story bank

I don't know your life, so I can't write your stories — but I know the ten shapes that work, and every one of them is sitting somewhere in your history. Spend fifteen minutes filling these in before Saturday. Type into the boxes; they save on this device.

The rules, before you write anything
  • Under 60 seconds. Time one out loud. Almost everyone's natural version is three minutes, and three minutes is where a room goes quiet in the wrong way.
  • Structure: setup → problem → the moment → hand back. One sentence of setup. The "moment" is the whole point — get there fast.
  • Never be the hero. Be the idiot, the witness, or the one who got lucky. It's the only form of talking about yourself that makes people like you more, not less.
  • End on a question, always. "Have you ever had one of those?" turns your story into their turn, and stops you feeling like you hogged the floor.
  • Don't upgrade the details. These people may hear the story twice over the next two years. The unexaggerated version is the one that survives.

1 · The job that went sideways

ShapeA site, a switchboard, a shutdown or a commissioning that went wrong. The moment you realised, and what you did in the next ten minutes.
HookThe exact second it clicked — and the very calm, very stupid thing you said out loud.
CueWhat's the closest you've come to a disaster at work?

Exact scripts

Say these near enough word for word — they're built to sound like you, not like a script. Read them out loud once before Saturday; the ones you've said aloud are the ones that come out naturally under pressure.

Welcoming someone at the door

"Hey — come in, come in! I'm [name]. Really good to finally meet you both, [wife] talks about you all the time. Here, give me that. What can I get you — beer, wine, something soft?"

Why it works: name, warmth, hands free, drink in hand, in under fifteen seconds. Then walk them straight to another guest — never leave a new arrival standing.

Introducing two guests (your best move all night)

"Dave, this is Sam — Sam, Dave's the one who did that drive up to Karijini I was telling you about. Sam just got back from Japan with the kids, which sounds like a whole thing."

Why it works: never introduce with names alone. Hand them a topic and they'll run with it for twenty minutes — and you're free to move. Do this for every pairing you can.

Starting a conversation from nothing

"So how do you two know [wife's name]? I get told the stories but I never get the full picture."

"Whereabouts are you living? … Oh nice — what made you pick there?"

Why it works: both are about them, both are easy, and neither requires you to be interesting yet. "What made you pick there" is the small upgrade that turns a fact into a story.

Finding out what someone does

"What is it you do? … Right — and what does that actually look like day to day? I never really know what job titles mean."

"How'd you end up in that? Was it the plan, or did you fall into it?"

Why it works: the honest admission that you don't know what the title means is disarming, and it gets you a real description instead of a rehearsed one.

Going one level deeper on their career

"Is it busy at the moment, or has it gone quiet? … And what's the part of it people don't realise?"

"If you were starting again today, would you go the same way?"

Why it works: three questions that read as interest but deliver industry health, insider knowledge and honest reflection. Only ask them once you've already laughed together.

Discovering an opportunity naturally

"What's the thing everyone in your world complains about and just puts up with? Every industry's got one."

"Huh. And how do you deal with it now?" — then: "Fair enough. Anyway — how's the [food/game/kids]?"

Why it works: the exit line is the important half. Being the one who changes the subject is what proves you were curious rather than collecting. If it matters, it will come back to you later.

Talking to someone much more successful than you

"I'll be honest, I don't know much about your world at all. What's the thing people always get wrong about it?"

"What would you be doing if you were at my stage now?"

Why it works: successful people are surrounded by others performing for them. Straightforward curiosity, with nothing being asked for, is rare — and it's the thing they remember. Never compete, never impress, never apologise for yourself either.

Talking to someone in a field you know nothing about

"Right, explain it to me like I'm five — because I genuinely have no idea what that involves."

"What's the hardest part? … And what's the bit you actually enjoy?"

Why it works: people love explaining their work to someone who's honestly interested and not pretending to follow. "Hardest part / best part" works on literally any job on earth.

Joining a group already talking

[Arrive with a bottle or a plate. Stand at the open side. Listen for twenty seconds. Laugh where they laugh.] "Sorry — top up? … No, keep going, this sounds better than what I was doing."

Why it works: you enter as a host doing a host thing, then explicitly hand the floor back. Never ask them to summarise what you missed.

Escaping a conversation politely

"I'm going to go check on the barbecue before I ruin dinner — but grab me later, I want to hear how that ended."

"Let me top you up. Actually, come with me — you should meet Priya, she did the same thing."

Why it works: reason, compliment, open loop. The second version is better still — you leave and you've done them a favour. Neither one can be taken badly.

Swapping numbers without it being a thing

"What's your number — I'll send you that link about the [thing]."

"Are you on WhatsApp? I'll flick you the details for that place we were talking about."

Why it works: attach the swap to a small promise, never to the relationship. Then actually send it that night — your name lands in their phone attached to something useful.

Ending the evening well

"Dave — really glad you came. And send me that thing about the Pilbara job, I want to read it properly."

"We should do this again. Seriously — next one's at yours, I'm not doing this washing up twice."

Why it works: name, one specific detail, one concrete forward step, delivered lightly. Walk every single guest to their car. Two minutes at the door will outweigh an hour in the middle of the night.

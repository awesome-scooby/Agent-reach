01

From telecom to power: what transfers, what doesn't

16 min · read this first

You're not a beginner. You're an engineer who is expert in a different energy regime — and that's an advantage, but only once you know which of your instincts still apply and which of them will hurt you.

The shift

Telecom power is about keeping a load alive. Switchboard work is about containing energy when something fails. Same architecture, completely different stakes.

What transfers straight across

Keep it

Reading drawings

Telecom runs on rack elevations, cable schedules, patch schedules and block diagrams. The habit of working from a controlled drawing with a revision on it is the hardest thing to teach, and you already have it.

Keep it

DC power plants

If you've worked on a −48 V plant you already understand rectifiers, battery autonomy, busbars, distribution fuses, alarm contacts and load sharing. That's most of module 06's vocabulary in a different voltage.

Keep it

Loop testing & continuity

Buzzing out a pair, proving end-to-end, labelling both ends, tracing a fault by halving the run — identical discipline, different conductor.

Keep it

Commissioning mindset

Test plans, witness points, results recorded against a schedule, punch lists, handover packs. Telecom is heavy on this. It maps directly onto module 17.

Keep it

EMC intuition

Screening, earthing at one end, segregating noisy from sensitive, bonding practice. This is exactly what a VSD installation needs and most sparkies learn it the hard way.

Keep it

Standards culture

You're used to working to a written standard rather than a habit. Swap ACMA / AS-CA for AS/NZS 3000, AS/NZS 61439 and AS 2067 and the muscle is the same.

What will catch you out

Recalibrate

Energy, not voltage, is the hazard

415 V sounds like "not that much more than 48 V". It isn't a voltage problem — it's a fault energy problem. A bolted fault on a 1000 kVA board delivers tens of thousands of amps and an arc flash that vaporises copper.

The instinct to recalibrate: in telecom, worst case is usually "the load drops". Here, worst case is a burns unit.

Recalibrate

"I'll just work on it live"

Working live on a 48 V DC bus with insulated tools is normal practice in your old world. In LV power, live work needs a documented justification, a risk assessment, a permit, a second person and specific PPE — and the default answer is no.

New default: isolate, lock, prove dead. Module 17 has the sequence.

Recalibrate

Earth means something else here

In telecom, earth is largely a reference: bonding, screening, keeping noise out of signals. In an installation, the earth conductor is a protective device — it's the return path that turns an insulation failure into a short circuit so the breaker trips.

Same green wire, completely different job. Module 05 draws it.

Recalibrate

AC brings friends

DC has voltage, current, resistance. AC adds phase, reactance, power factor, harmonics and a rotating field. A cable's rating depends on how it's installed; a motor draws six times its rated current for a few seconds every start.

None of it is hard — it's just new furniture. Module 02 lays it out.

Recalibrate

"Circuit" changes meaning

In telecom a circuit is a path for information, defined end to end. In power a circuit is a loop of current with a protective device sized to the cable at its origin.

When someone says "that circuit is 32 amp", they're naming the breaker, not the load.

Recalibrate

Different symbol set

You can read schematics — just not these ones. AS 1102 / IEC 60617 is a different alphabet for the same grammar. Module 04 is a searchable dictionary, not something to memorise.

⚠
Your scope of practice — read this once, then live by it

You are an electrical engineer without an Australian electrical licence. In Australia, electrical work — installing, altering, repairing, terminating, testing and connecting electrical equipment — is licensed work, and the rules are set by each state's regulator (in NSW it's SafeWork/Fair Trading, in Victoria Energy Safe, in Queensland the Electrical Safety Office, and so on).

What you can do: design, calculate, specify, review and check drawings, produce and check schedules, inspect finished work against drawings, witness and record testing, write test plans and procedures, run commissioning from the paperwork side, and direct and supervise the people doing the work.

What you must not do until licensed: terminate or alter wiring, energise or operate switchgear on an installation, carry out live testing, or perform the electrical work itself — even "just this one wire", even under someone else's licence, unless you are a registered apprentice or trainee doing supervised work under a proper arrangement.

Action: get this confirmed in writing by your employer, and check it against your state regulator's own guidance, in week 1. 02

Three-phase power: the parts that actually matter

20 min

Not a theory course. These are the six ideas that appear on every drawing, in every conversation and in every calculation you'll be asked to check.

TL;DR

Three-phase is three voltages 120° apart. 415 V is not a different supply from 240 V — it's the voltage between any two of the three, and 240 V is the voltage from any one of them to neutral. √3 (1.732) is the number that connects them, and it turns up in almost every formula you'll ever use.

Idea 1

Why three and not one

  • Constant power. A single phase delivers power in pulses — twice per cycle it passes through zero. Three phases overlap so total power is steady. Motors don't shudder.
  • A rotating field for free. Three windings fed 120° apart produce a magnetic field that rotates. That's an induction motor: no brushes, no commutator, nothing to wear out.
  • Less copper. Three conductors carry three times the power of one, without three times the metal.
Idea 2

Star vs delta

  • Star (wye) — three windings joined at a common point. You get a neutral, so you can serve 240 V single-phase loads. Line voltage = √3 × phase voltage; line current = phase current.
  • Delta — three windings in a triangle. No neutral. Line voltage = phase voltage; line current = √3 × phase current.
  • Dyn11 on a transformer nameplate means delta on the HV side, star with neutral out on the LV side, with the LV lagging the HV by 30°. The lowercase n is why your board has a neutral at all.
Idea 3

The neutral is not a spare wire

If the three phases are perfectly balanced, the neutral carries nothing. Real buildings are never balanced, so it carries the difference.

Worse: non-linear loads (VSDs, LED drivers, switch-mode supplies, UPS) generate third harmonic currents which, unlike the fundamentals, add up in the neutral instead of cancelling. A heavily loaded office board can have more current in the neutral than in any phase.

That's why you'll see full-size or oversized neutral bars specified, and why "the neutral only needs to be half size" is a dangerous old habit.

Idea 4

Motors, in three numbers

  • Synchronous speed = 120 × frequency ÷ poles. At 50 Hz: 2-pole = 3000 rpm, 4-pole = 1500 rpm, 6-pole = 1000 rpm.
  • Slip — the rotor always runs slightly slower than the field (about 2–5 %), which is what produces torque. A "1500 rpm" motor actually runs at ~1440 rpm.
  • Rule of thumb: at 415 V three-phase, full load current ≈ 2 × kW. An 11 kW motor pulls roughly 21 A. Good enough to sanity-check a drawing in your head; use the nameplate for anything real.
Idea 5

The one formula to actually memorise

P = √3 × VL × IL × cos φ

Three-phase real power = 1.732 × line voltage × line current × power factor. Rearranged for current — which is what you actually want — it becomes the motor FLC calculation in module 10.

Single-phase is the same without the √3: P = V × I × cos φ.

And the relationships: kVA² = kW² + kVAr², pf = kW / kVA.

Idea 6

Harmonics, briefly

Anything with a rectifier in the front end — VSDs, UPS, LED drivers, computer supplies — draws current in sharp pulses instead of a smooth sine. Those pulses decompose into multiples of 50 Hz: the 3rd (150 Hz), 5th, 7th and so on.

What it causes: neutral overheating, transformer derating, nuisance tripping, capacitor banks resonating and destroying themselves, and metering that reads wrong on an averaging meter.

What you do about it: oversized neutrals, K-rated or derated transformers, detuned reactors in front of PFC banks, line reactors or active front ends on big drives. That's why the SLD in module 07 says "200 kVAr detuned".

Fault level: why a 5.75 % transformer gives you 24 kA

A transformer's impedance voltage Z% is the percentage of rated voltage you'd have to apply to the primary to push rated current through a short-circuited secondary. It's stamped on the nameplate.

Flip that around: if you apply 100 % of the voltage into a short, you get roughly 100 / Z% times the rated current.

  • 1000 kVA at 415 V → full load current = 1 000 000 ÷ (1.732 × 415) ≈ 1391 A
  • Z = 5.75 % → fault current ≈ 1391 ÷ 0.0575 ≈ 24.2 kA
  • Add the utility network's contribution and the back-feed from running motors, and the drawing lands on 43.5 kA

Why this matters: that number sets the Icu of every device in the board and the Icw of the busbar. A lower-impedance transformer is "better" for voltage regulation and worse for fault level. If a client upgrades a transformer without re-checking the board, the board may no longer be compliant — and that's exactly the kind of thing an engineer is there to catch.

Do this at work this week

  • Find a motor nameplate. Read kW, FLC, pf, efficiency, poles and rpm. Check FLC ≈ 2 × kW, and check rpm against 120f/p.
  • Find a transformer nameplate and read the kVA, the vector group and the impedance. Work out the full load current in your head.
  • Ask whether any site you supply has a PFC bank, and whether it's detuned. Then ask why.
03

Why there are five drawings of the same cubicle

14 min

The single biggest thing that confuses people: the SLD, the schematic, the wiring diagram and the GA are not different information. They are the same starter drawn at four different zoom levels, each answering a different question.

TL;DR

SLD = what feeds what. Schematic = how it works. Wiring diagram = which wire goes where. GA = where it physically sits. Schedules = the shopping list.

The five documents, in the order you meet them

Drawing 1

Single Line Diagram (SLD)

One line stands for all three phases. It's the map of power: source → main switch → busbar → every outgoing way.

  • Shows: ratings, cable sizes, CT ratios, fault levels, what feeds what
  • Hides: control wiring, terminal numbers, physical layout
  • You use it to: size things, find the isolation point, understand a fault
Drawing 2

Schematic (elementary / control diagram)

How the circuit thinks. Drawn for logic, not for layout — a contactor's coil is on one page and its contacts on three others.

  • Power section (3 lines) + control section (ladder rungs)
  • Every rung numbered; cross-references point to other sheets
  • You use it to: fault-find, understand interlocks, commission
Drawing 3

Wiring / interconnection diagram

How the circuit is actually built. Devices drawn roughly where they sit, every wire with its number and destination.

  • Terminal strips -XT1, -XT2 with numbered terminals
  • Wire numbers = the ferrules you crimp
  • You use it to: wire the board, buzz it out, terminate on site
Drawing 4

General Arrangement (GA)

The board as an object. Front elevation, gear plate layout, dimensions, cubicle splits, door swings, gland plate, plinth.

  • Shows: tier heights, escutcheon cut-outs, busbar chamber, cable zone
  • You use it to: build it, lift it, get it through the switchroom door
Drawing 5

The schedules (the boring gold)

Tables, not pictures — and often where the real answer is.

  • BOM / parts list — every device, part number, quantity
  • Cable schedule — every cable: from, to, size, cores, length, gland
  • Wiring/termination schedule — every wire: from terminal, to terminal, size, colour
  • Label schedule — every traffolyte label, exact wording
  • I/O schedule — every PLC point, address, description
Drawing 0

The cover sheet & drawing index

Read this first, always. It carries the revision, the drawing list, the legend, and the notes that override everything else.

Check the revision letter before you cut a single wire. Building to Rev C when site is on Rev E is the most expensive mistake in the shop.

A cloud around part of a drawing with a triangle containing a letter (△C) means "this bit changed at Rev C". When you pick up a revised drawing, look for the clouds first — that's the entire diff.

06

What each device actually does

20 min

One sentence each: it does X, it lives at Y, it fails like Z. If you can say the sentence without looking, you own the device.

TL;DR

Protection devices split into two jobs: protect the cable (breakers, fuses) and protect the motor (overloads, MPCBs, thermistors). They are not interchangeable, and a board has both because they trip at different speeds.

Protects the cable

ACB — air circuit breaker

Does: switches and protects the whole board, with an electronic trip unit you can program (long time, short time, instantaneous, earth fault — L/S/I/G).

Lives: the incomer or bus tie of a big MSB, usually on a drawout truck so it can be racked out for maintenance.

Fails like: nuisance trips from a wrong L setting; won't close because the truck isn't fully racked in or the spring isn't charged.

Typical here: Schneider MasterPact MTZ · Terasaki TemPower (via NHP)

Protects the cable

MCCB — moulded case

Does: the same job, smaller, in a sealed plastic case. Thermal element for overload, magnetic element for short circuit.

Lives: outgoing ways of an MSB, feeders to DBs and MCCs, big single loads.

Fails like: trips and can't be reset until pushed fully to the OFF/reset position first. Check the trip setting dial before blaming the load.

Typical here: Schneider ComPact NSX · Terasaki TemBreak 2 / PRO (via NHP)

Protects the cable

MCB — miniature breaker

Does: protects final subcircuits. The curve letter is the magnetic threshold: B 3–5×, C 5–10×, D 10–20× rated current.

Lives: DIN rail in DBs and control sections.

Fails like: a C-curve on a big transformer or motor circuit trips on inrush — you need D. A D-curve on a lighting circuit is too slow.

Typical here: Schneider Acti9 iC60 / iC65

Protects people

RCD / RCBO

Does: compares current out against current back. Any difference is going somewhere it shouldn't — probably a person — so it opens in ~30 ms.

Lives: final subcircuits; RCBO = RCD and MCB in one module.

Fails like: nuisance trips from accumulated leakage on long runs or several VSDs sharing one RCD. Type AC won't see DC leakage from a VSD — you need Type A or B there.

Typical here: Schneider Acti9 iID / Vigi add-on modules

Switches

Contactor (KM)

Does: an electrically operated switch. Energise the coil A1–A2 and all its contacts change state.

Lives: every starter. Rated AC-3 for motors, AC-1 for resistive loads — an AC-1 contactor on a motor will weld itself shut.

Fails like: chatters when the control voltage is low; contacts weld closed so the motor won't stop; coil burns out.

Typical here: Schneider TeSys D (LC1D) · Allen-Bradley 100-C (via NHP)

Protects the motor

Thermal overload (TOL)

Does: heats a bimetal strip with the motor current. Sustained overcurrent bends it and flips an auxiliary contact.

Lives: directly under the contactor. Set the dial to the motor nameplate FLC, not to the breaker size.

Fails like: trips repeatedly = a real mechanical problem or a lost phase, not "a faulty overload". Trip class 10/20/30 = how many seconds it tolerates 6× FLC.

Typical here: Schneider TeSys LRD / LR9 · MPCB: TeSys GV2/GV3, AB 140M (via NHP)

Measures

Current transformer (CT)

Does: scales a big primary current down to 1 A or 5 A. Ratio and class are on the drawing: 800/5 cl 0.5 for metering, 5P10 for protection.

Lives: clamped over a busbar or cable, wired to a test terminal block with shorting links.

Fails like: catastrophically, if you open-circuit the secondary while primary current flows.

Metering typically Schneider PowerLogic PM / ION series

Conditions power

VSD / VFD

Does: rectifies AC to DC, then chops the DC back into variable-frequency AC. Speed = frequency, so you get soft starts, ramps and energy savings.

Lives: its own ventilated cubicle or MCC bucket, with a line reactor or EMC filter.

Fails like: overvoltage trips on fast decel (needs a braking resistor), earth-leakage trips from cable capacitance, comms noise from an unglanded screen.

Typical here: Schneider Altivar (ATV320/340/600/900) · Allen-Bradley PowerFlex (via NHP)

Conditions power

Soft starter

Does: ramps voltage up through thyristors to limit starting current and mechanical shock, then a bypass contactor takes over.

Lives: on pumps and conveyors where a VSD isn't needed but a DOL slam is unacceptable.

Fails like: won't start a high-inertia load because the ramp is too gentle; bypass contactor doesn't pull in and the thyristors cook.

Typical here: Schneider Altistart ATS22 / ATS480

Thinks

PLC + interposing relays

Does: reads inputs (24 VDC), runs logic, drives outputs. Interposing relays let a small 24 V output switch a 240 V coil.

Lives: the control section, on its own DIN rail behind a perspex shield, fed from a 24 VDC power supply.

Fails like: outputs "on" in the software but nothing moves — because the interposing relay, its fuse, or the field wiring is the actual break.

Typical here: Schneider Modicon M221/M241 · Allen-Bradley Micro800 / CompactLogix (via NHP)

Keeps power clean

SPD (surge diverter)

Does: a very high resistance until the voltage spikes, then a near short to earth. Sacrificial by design.

Lives: Type 1 at the main incomer, Type 2 at DBs, Type 3 at sensitive equipment. Always on its own short, straight leads.

Fails like: the status window goes from green to red — it's spent, replace the module.

Typical here: Schneider Acti9 iPRD / PRD1 series

Cuts the bill

PFC bank

Does: capacitor steps switched in by a controller to cancel the lagging current motors draw, pushing power factor toward 1.0.

Lives: a dedicated cubicle at the end of the MSB, well ventilated, with detuned reactors if there are VSDs on site.

Fails like: capacitors bulge and vent; contactors weld from switching in-rush; harmonics resonate with an untuned bank and destroy it.

Typical here: Schneider VarSet / detuned VarSet Harmony

Reading an SLD, line by line

18 min

Here is a real main switchboard SLD — the kind that lands on your bench with a job number on it. Ten callouts, ten questions the drawing is answering. Read the drawing first, then the list.

Method

Read every SLD top-down, then left-to-right: source → main switch → busbar → each outgoing way. Never start in the middle. The numbers on an SLD are always ratings, not measurements.

1

Where the power comes from

An 11 kV ring main unit with a load-break switch and HRC fuses. On a ring you can feed from either direction — which is why RMU switching is a documented, permitted operation, not something you just do.

2

The fault level — the number everything is sized to

1000 kVA, Z = 5.75 % gives roughly 1000/(√3 × 0.415) ÷ 0.0575 ≈ 24 kA from the transformer alone; the drawing states 43.5 kA once motor contribution and the utility source are included. Every device and the busbar itself must survive that number. If someone "upgrades" the transformer, this whole board may no longer be legal.

3

The main switch

2000 A drawout ACB. Icu 65 kA beats the 43.5 kA fault level. Ics 100 % means it can do it repeatedly. The L / S / I / G line is the electronic trip unit's four elements — long time (overload), short time (delayed fault), instantaneous, ground fault. Those settings come from the discrimination study, not from taste.

4

Metering

2000/5, cl 0.5S — the CT turns 2000 A into 5 A for the meter, accurate to 0.5 % for revenue-grade metering. Protection CTs are marked differently (5P10): accurate at 10× rated current, which is what a relay needs.

5

The busbar

2000 A continuous, Icw 50 kA for 1 s, Ipk 105 kA. Icw is the heat it survives; Ipk is the mechanical bang it survives. That's what decides how many bar supports go in and how far apart.

6

N bar, E bar, MEN link

The neutral bar is sized for the neutral current (with lots of VSDs and single-phase electronics, harmonics can make it bigger than you expect). The earth bar is bonded to the structure and the electrode. The MEN link is here and nowhere else downstream.

7

An outgoing way, decoded

-Q3 400 A → device tag and rating. 4C+E 185 → the cable. 28 m · Vd 1.1 % → run length and calculated voltage drop (AS/NZS 3000 allows 5 % total from point of supply). Destination box → where it lands. That's the whole grammar of every outgoing way you will ever read.

8

Surge protection

Type 1+2 SPD at the incomer, with a note about lead length — SPD leads must be short and straight, ideally under 500 mm total, or the inductance of your own wiring defeats the device.

9

Spares are information too

A dashed way marked SPARE means the busbar, the space and often the mounting is there for a future 250 A circuit. If the client later asks "can we add a chiller?", this is the drawing that answers it.

10

The assembly note in the border

AS/NZS 61439.2 · Form 3b · IP42. Form defines how much internal separation there is between busbars, devices and terminals. IP defines what can get in. These aren't decoration — they're the contract for how the board is built and what it costs.

Discrimination (selectivity) — why the settings exist

The goal: a fault on the lighting board trips only the lighting board's breaker. The 2000 A main stays in. If the main goes instead, the whole site is dark because a light fitting failed.

  • Current discrimination — the upstream device is rated far higher, so only the downstream one sees enough current to trip.
  • Time discrimination — the upstream device is deliberately delayed (that's the S element, e.g. 0.2 s), giving downstream time to clear first.
  • Energy / zone discrimination — current-limiting devices, or ZSI wiring where downstream breakers tell the upstream one "I've got this, hold off".

A discrimination study is a document with curves in it. When someone changes a breaker setting on site "to stop the nuisance trips", they may have just destroyed that study. Setting changes are a drawing change.

Do this at work this week

  • Take one real SLD and, out loud, read a single outgoing way front to back: tag, rating, cable, length, destination.
  • Find the fault level note on that drawing. Ask where the number came from.
  • Find the Form and IP rating in the border, and go look at the board to see the separation with your own eyes.

Reading a schematic: one DOL starter, rung by rung

20 min · the important one

This is the drawing that unlocks everything else. Once you can walk a control ladder with your finger and predict what happens, every starter in every MCC is a variation on this.

The whole idea

A control ladder is read like a sentence: power comes down the left rail, through every condition on the rung, into the coil, and out the right rail. Anything in series is an AND. Anything in parallel is an OR. That's it — that's the entire language.

Walk it with your finger

1

-QF1 — the breaker

Protects the cable from a short circuit. Curve D because a motor's inrush would nuisance-trip a C curve. This is also the isolation point for the starter.

2

-KM1 — the three power poles

Three contacts, drawn open, joined by a dashed line to the coil in the control circuit. The dashed line means "these move together" — it's mechanical linkage, not a wire.

3

-F1 — the overload

Its heaters sit in the power circuit; its contacts sit in the control circuit (95-96 NC and 97-98 NO). Same device, two places on the drawing. This trips people up constantly.

4

Rung 1: the start button

Momentary NO. Press it and the rung is complete: L → E-stop (closed) → stop (closed) → start (now closed) → overload (closed) → coil → N. The contactor pulls in.

5

The seal-in — the clever bit

When KM1 pulls in, its own auxiliary contact 13-14 closes in parallel with the start button. Now you can let go of the button and the rung stays complete through the contactor's own contact. The contactor holds itself in.

This is also why it drops out on a power blip and won't restart by itself — which is deliberate and is a safety feature, not a fault.

6

The coil, A1–A2

Always the last thing on the rung, always against the right rail. If you ever see something after the coil, look again — you've misread a branch.

7

Rung 4: telling the PLC

A spare aux contact drives a PLC input through terminal -XT1:21. This is "run feedback" — the PLC now knows the contactor actually pulled in, rather than assuming it did because it asked.

8

The contact mirror

The little table under the coil lists every contact of KM1 and which rung uses it. When you're hunting "where else does this contactor do something?", this table is the answer — you don't have to search the sheets.

⚠
Why stop is NC and start is NO

If the wire to the stop button breaks, an NC stop opens the rung and the motor stops — safe. If stop were NO, a broken wire would mean you can never stop the motor. The same logic drives the overload's 95-96 NC contact. Safety functions are always drawn so that a failure de-energises.

Fault-finding this exact circuit — the order a good sparky works in

Symptom: press START, nothing happens.

  1. Is the control supply live? Check across the rails. If -FU1 is blown, stop and find out why before replacing it.
  2. Walk the rung with a meter, left to right, referenced to N. You should read 240 V at each point until you find the open one. The first point that reads 0 V is where the break is.
  3. Check the overload hasn't tripped. 95-96 open = the whole rung is dead. The TRIP lamp on rung 3 tells you this without opening the door.
  4. Check the E-stop is released and the selector isn't in AUTO with the PLC not calling.
  5. If the coil is energised but the motor doesn't turn, you've moved from the control circuit to the power circuit: check QF1, check all three phases at the contactor's load side, check the motor.

The habit worth building: the schematic tells you where to put the probe before you open the door. Decide your test points from the drawing, then go and take the readings — under proper isolation and testing procedure.

Do this at work this week

  • Pick any starter schematic in the shop and find: the seal-in contact, the overload's NC contact, and the contact mirror.
  • Cover the drawing and sketch a DOL control rung from memory on a scrap of paper. Do this three days in a row.
  • Ask someone to show you a starter that has an extra interlock in the rung, and work out what it prevents.

Numbering

Source-destination — how to think in it

The rule: the ferrule you read tells you where to walk to next. It is an address, not an identity.

  • The two ends of one wire carry different text. That is correct, not a mistake.
  • Reading XT1:14 on a wire at the contactor means "my other end is on terminal 14 of strip XT1".
  • Fault-finding becomes physical: open the door, read the ferrule, go there. No schedule needed at the bench.
  • The cost is at build time — every wire needs two different ferrules, so the crimping and checking is slower and more error-prone.
  • Never invent an address. If a wire isn't on the drawing it doesn't get installed until the drawing says so, and if the drawing is wrong that's an RFI, not a decision.

The numbers: six calculations that decide a drawing

22 min

Every rating on an SLD came from a calculation somebody did. These six are the ones behind almost all of them. Work them once by hand and the drawings stop being arbitrary.

TL;DR

The chain always runs the same way: nameplate → current → device → cable → check volt drop → check fault withstand. Change anything upstream and everything downstream has to be re-checked. That re-checking is the job.

Worked example: one 15 kW pump, end to end

1

Motor full load current

I = P ÷ (√3 × V × pf × η)

  • P = 15 000 W, V = 415 V, pf = 0.85, η = 0.90
  • I = 15 000 ÷ (1.732 × 415 × 0.85 × 0.90)
  • I = 15 000 ÷ 549.7 = 27.3 A

Sanity check: 2 × 15 kW ≈ 30 A. Close enough.

Decides: the overload dial setting (28 A off the nameplate), the contactor's AC-3 rating, and the starting point for the cable.

2

Cable size and voltage drop

Vd = (mV/A/m × I × L) ÷ 1000

  • Pick 6 mm² Cu from AS/NZS 3008 for 28 A in the chosen installation method
  • Table gives roughly 7.3 mV/A/m for 6 mm² three-phase
  • Vd = 7.3 × 28 × 42 ÷ 1000 = 8.6 V
  • As a percentage: 8.6 ÷ 415 = 2.1 % ✓

Decides: the cable on the schedule. AS/NZS 3000 allows 5 % total from the point of supply to the far end — and the submain has already used some of it, so 2.1 % here has to fit inside what's left.

3

Transformer full load current

I = kVA × 1000 ÷ (√3 × V)

  • 1000 kVA at 415 V
  • I = 1 000 000 ÷ (1.732 × 415)
  • I = 1391 A

Decides: the size of the main incoming device. 1391 A means a 1600 A frame at minimum; the drawing specifies a 2000 A ACB for headroom and because the busbar is rated 2000 A.

4

Prospective fault current

Isc ≈ IFL ÷ Z%

  • IFL = 1391 A, Z = 5.75 % = 0.0575
  • Isc = 1391 ÷ 0.0575 = 24.2 kA from the transformer alone
  • Plus network contribution and motor back-feed → the study says 43.5 kA

Decides: the Icu of every device in the board and the Icw of the busbar. Get this wrong and the board is a bomb that passes its functional test perfectly.

5

Maximum demand and diversity

A board can have 2500 A of connected load and a 1600 A main, because everything never runs flat out at once.

  • AS/NZS 3000 Appendix C gives the assessment methods — by installation type, by calculation, or by measurement on an existing installation
  • RDF (rated diversity factor) under AS/NZS 61439 is the assembly's own version: e.g. 0.8 across a group of outgoing circuits

Decides: the main breaker, the busbar rating, the submain, and how much the job costs. It's also the number clients argue about, because it's the one with judgement in it.

6

Power factor correction sizing

kVAr = P × (tan φ₁ − tan φ₂)

  • 500 kW at pf 0.80 → φ₁ = 36.9°, tan φ₁ = 0.750
  • Target pf 0.95 → φ₂ = 18.2°, tan φ₂ = 0.329
  • kVAr = 500 × (0.750 − 0.329) = 211 kVAr
  • Specify a 200 kVAr bank in steps (e.g. 8 × 25 kVAr)

Decides: the PFC cubicle size, the number of steps, the controller, and whether you need detuned reactors — which you do, if there are VSDs on site.

ℹ
These are teaching numbers, not design numbers

Real sizing uses the actual AS/NZS 3008 tables for the exact cable construction, installation method, grouping, ambient temperature and soil conditions, plus a proper fault-level and discrimination study. The arithmetic above is right; the table values are illustrative. The point is to understand what the drawing is claiming — the certified design is somebody's signed responsibility.

Two more numbers you'll be asked about

I²t — let-through energy. When a fault happens, the cable has to survive the heat until the device clears it. That energy is proportional to current squared × time. A current-limiting device clears so fast that it lets through far less energy than the fault could deliver — which is how a smaller cable can be legal downstream of it. It's also why "the breaker is big enough" and "the cable survives the fault" are two separate checks.

Earth fault loop impedance. For a protective device to disconnect in time, enough fault current has to flow, which means the loop — active out, through the fault, back via the earth conductor and the MEN link to the transformer — has to be low enough impedance. Long runs and undersized earth conductors push it up. AS/NZS 3000 sets maximum disconnection times, and AS/NZS 3017 covers the verification. This is measured on site with a loop impedance tester, and it's a common reason a long submain gets upsized.

  • Find the volt drop figure on a drawing and check it with the mV/A/m method. Ask which table row was used.
  • Ask to see a fault level study or a discrimination study. Read the assumptions page, not just the curves.

Motor control: DOL, star-delta, soft start, VSD

18 min

Four ways to start the same motor. They exist because starting current and starting torque are a trade-off, and every one of them buys you something at a cost.

TL;DR

DOL = simple and brutal (6–8× FLC). Star-delta = a third of the current and a third of the torque. Soft starter = smooth ramp, still no speed control. VSD = full control of speed and torque, most cost, most things to go wrong.

DOL

Direct on line

Start current 6–8× FLC
Start torque 100 %

One contactor. Cheapest, most reliable, hardest on the mechanics and the supply. Fine up to roughly 7.5–15 kW depending on your supply authority's limit.

Star-delta

Two-stage

Start current ~2× FLC
Start torque ~33 %

Three contactors + timer, six motor leads. Cheap current reduction, but the torque drops the same amount — useless on a loaded conveyor, fine on a fan.

Soft starter

Ramped voltage

Start current 2–4× (adjustable)
Start torque tunable

Smooth, no transition bump, gives you soft stop too (big for water hammer on pumps). Still runs at full speed only. Bypass contactor after the ramp.

VSD

Variable speed

Start current ≤ 1.5× FLC
Start torque up to 150 %

Full speed and torque control, big energy savings on pumps and fans. Costs the most, needs cooling, EMC discipline and shielded motor cable.

VSD gotchas that bite people on site
  • Never put a contactor between the VSD and the motor and switch it while running — the drive sees an open output and can fail. If you must have an isolator there, it needs an aux contact wired to inhibit the drive first.
  • Never fit power-factor capacitors on a VSD output. Ever.
  • Screened motor cable, glanded 360° at both ends, EMC gland at the drive. A pigtailed screen is the #1 cause of "the PLC keeps losing comms when the pump starts".
  • Overload protection is inside the drive — set the drive's motor parameters (FLC, kW, poles) from the motor nameplate, not from the drive's defaults.
  • Long motor cables cause voltage reflection at the motor terminals; over about 50–100 m you may need an output reactor or dV/dt filter.
  • DC bus takes time to discharge. Wait for the stated time and the LED to go out before opening the drive, regardless of what the isolator says.
  • Earth leakage from cable capacitance can trip a Type AC RCD. Type B or a dedicated arrangement is usually needed.

  • "Show me a star-delta starter and point out the mechanical interlock between KM2 and KM3."
  • "When do we quote a soft starter versus a VSD?"
  • "What's our standard for VSD cable glanding and screen termination?"

24 volts, PLCs and 4–20 mA loops

16 min

The low-voltage side of the board is where most modern fault-finding happens. It's also the part nobody explains, because it looks like nothing is happening.

TL;DR

A digital input asks is 24 V present at this terminal, yes or no? An analogue input asks how many milliamps are flowing round this loop? Both are answered with a multimeter and the I/O schedule, before you ever open the PLC software.

Wiring

Sink vs source (PNP vs NPN)

Source / PNP — the field device supplies +24 V to the input. The common is 0 V. This is the normal arrangement in Australian and European panels.

Sink / NPN — the field device pulls the input down to 0 V. The common is +24 V. Common on Japanese equipment.

Mixing them means nothing works and nothing is broken. Check the card's common terminal before you wire the first sensor.

Screens

Earth the screen at one end only

An instrument cable screen earthed at both ends creates an earth loop — mains hum injected straight into your 4–20 mA signal, showing up as a reading that dances.

Convention: earth at the board end, cut back and insulate at the field end. Whatever your standard says, be consistent across the whole job.

Reading the I/O schedule

The most useful table in the pack

Every row is one signal: address · type · terminal · device tag · description · range. When commissioning, you work down this list and prove each row physically. Tick, tick, tick.

If a row can't be proved, it goes on the punch list with a reason. Never tick something you haven't seen move.

Safety circuits

E-stops are not ordinary inputs

A real safety function uses dual-channel wiring into a safety relay or safety PLC, with cross-monitoring so a single short or broken wire is detected.

Rated to AS 4024 / ISO 13849 with a required performance level (e.g. PLd, Cat 3). An E-stop wired into a normal PLC input is not a safety system, no matter how good the code is.

The letter

What a and b actually mean

For Forms 2 and 3, the letter is about the busbars: a = the terminals for external conductors are not separated from the busbars; b = they are.

The real question

"What can I work on while the rest is live?"

  • Form 1 — nothing. Kill the board.
  • Form 2 — you're protected from the busbars, but all the devices share one space.
  • Form 3b — isolate one functional unit and work on it, but you're still in a shared cable zone with live terminals of other circuits.
  • Form 4b — isolate one unit and work on it and its terminations in a fully enclosed space, with everything else live.

That is the entire commercial argument. Higher Form = higher availability = more metal, more labour, more width.

Watch out

Three traps

  • Form is not a safety rating. It doesn't reduce arc flash energy and it doesn't make live work safe. It reduces the chance of an accidental contact spreading.
  • "Form 4 Type 7" is a British (BS EN) sub-classification. AS/NZS 61439.2 uses 4a / 4b. If a spec quotes Types, raise an RFI rather than guessing which one they mean.
  • Separation must be maintained. A Form 4b board with a barrier left out after maintenance is a Form 2 board with a Form 4b label on it.
Copper

Busbar — what the numbers mean

  • Continuous rating depends on the copper cross-section, the plating, and how well the cubicle sheds heat. The same bar in a sealed IP56 enclosure carries less than in a ventilated one.
  • Icw (kA for 1 s) — thermal withstand. Fixes the minimum cross-section.
  • Ipk (kA peak) — mechanical force. Fixes how close together the bar supports go. Fault current makes parallel bars slam apart with real violence.
  • Torque and joint prep — every bolted joint torqued to spec, faces cleaned and (for aluminium) compounded. A loose joint is a heat source that finds you six months later on a thermal scan.
Ratings on the label

The board's own nameplate

  • InA — rated current of the whole assembly
  • Inc — rated current of a circuit
  • RDF — rated diversity factor: not every way runs at full load at once
  • Icw / Ipk — withstand ratings
  • IP / IK — ingress and impact
  • Form, pollution degree, indoor/outdoor, manufacturer and standard

Under AS/NZS 61439 this is a verified design — the manufacturer has to be able to demonstrate every one of those numbers.

Space

Escutcheons, doors and access

The escutcheon is the panel with the cut-outs that keeps you at IP2X (finger-safe) when the door is open. Doors get flexible earth bonds. Hinged escutcheons get them too.

Switchroom clearances, door swing direction and egress are set by AS/NZS 3000 — a door that opens the wrong way in a switchroom is a real defect, not a preference.

Labels

Labelling is part of the build

Traffolyte labels to the label schedule, exact wording, exact colours. Circuit identification on every way. Danger and warning labels where the drawing calls for them.

The board is not finished when it works. It's finished when someone who has never seen it can safely operate it from the labels alone.

How a board gets built: enquiry to as-built

18 min

This is the map of the whole business you're joining — and, usefully, the map of which document exists at which moment. When someone says "we're waiting on IFC" or "that's a routine verification issue", this is what they mean.

TL;DR

A switchboard is a manufactured product with a verified design, not a box of parts wired together. AS/NZS 61439 splits that into design verification (proving the design works, once) and routine verification (proving this particular board was built right, every time).

AS/NZS 61439, in plain terms

This is the standard your boards are built and sold under, and it is the reason a switchboard is a manufactured product rather than a custom one-off. It splits responsibility in a way worth understanding properly.

Who's who

Original manufacturer vs assembly manufacturer

Original manufacturer — the organisation that carried out the original design and the design verification. Usually a Schneider or an ABB, who tested a system (enclosure, busbar, devices) and published the verified design data.

Assembly manufacturer — the organisation that assembles this particular board. Often your company.

Why it matters: if you build strictly within the original manufacturer's verified system, you inherit their verification. Step outside it — a different busbar spacing, a device the system wasn't tested with, an enclosure modification — and you become responsible for verifying that variation. That's the commercial heart of the standard.

Once per design

Design verification

Proving the design is sound. Done by one of three routes for each characteristic: testing, calculation or comparison with a tested reference design, or assessment against design rules.

The characteristics verified include:

  • Strength of materials and parts, corrosion, IP and IK
  • Clearances and creepage distances
  • Protection against electric shock, continuity of the protective circuit
  • Temperature rise — the one that limits how much current the board really carries
  • Dielectric properties
  • Short-circuit withstand — the Icw and Ipk numbers
  • EMC and mechanical operation
Every single board

Routine verification

Proving this board was built to that verified design. Done in your factory, on every board, before it leaves:

  • Inspection of the assembly, including wiring and — where necessary — an operational electrical test
  • Verification of the protective circuit: continuity of the earth path, and that all exposed conductive parts are bonded
  • Dielectric (insulation) verification
  • Checks against the drawings, the label schedule and the BOM

This is the paperwork worth reading early. A routine test sheet tells you more about how a shop actually works than any procedure document.

On the plate

What the compliance plate is claiming

The rating plate on the board is a legal statement. Every value on it is backed by verification:

  • InA rated current of the assembly · Inc rated current of a circuit
  • RDF rated diversity factor
  • Icw / Ipk / Icc withstand ratings
  • Ue / Ui / Uimp voltages
  • IP, IK, Form, pollution degree, indoor/outdoor
  • Manufacturer, type designation, serial, date, standard

If a number on the plate can't be traced back to a verification route, it shouldn't be on the plate.

Process

ITP — inspection & test plan

The list of every check on a job, in order, with who does it and who signs it off. Hold point = work stops until it's signed off. Witness point = the client is invited but work continues if they don't show. Learning to read an ITP makes you useful in about a week.

Process

FAT vs SAT

Factory Acceptance Test — the client comes to your shop and watches the board be tested before it ships. Cheap place to find problems. Site Acceptance Test — the same functions proved again after installation, with the real cables and the real loads. Expensive place to find problems.

Process

RFIs and variations

A Request for Information is the formal way to ask "this drawing is ambiguous / wrong — what do you want?". A variation is what happens when the answer costs money. Both create a paper trail. An engineer who raises clear, early RFIs is worth a lot; one who guesses is expensive.

★
Where a new engineer adds value immediately

You don't need to be able to wire a board to be useful. Checking that the BOM matches the SLD, that the label schedule matches the drawing, that revisions are consistent across the pack, that the ITP has been signed at each hold point, and that RFI answers actually made it back onto the drawings — that's real work, it's within your scope, and most shops are short of someone doing it.

Ask at work this week

  • "Are we the assembly manufacturer working inside someone's verified system, or do we verify our own designs? Show me where that's documented."
  • "Can I see a completed routine verification test sheet and an ITP from a finished job?"
  • "What does our IFA → IFC → as-built process look like, and how do superseded drawings get removed from the floor?"

The HV switchroom

16 min

Above 1000 V the physics is the same but the consequences aren't. HV work is procedural: authorisations, switching sheets, permits. Nothing here authorises you to touch HV — it's so you can read the drawings and understand what the HV crew are doing.

⚠
HV is licensed, authorised, permit-based work

HV switching is done only by people specifically authorised for that site and that equipment, under a written switching program, with a permit and test-for-dead and earthing. Do not operate HV equipment. Read, learn, ask, and stay behind the line until you are trained and authorised.

Numbers

ANSI device numbers, decoded

Protection relays are labelled with numbers, not words. The ones you'll meet:

  • 50 instantaneous overcurrent · 51 time overcurrent
  • 50N / 51N the same, but earth fault
  • 27 undervoltage · 59 overvoltage · 81 under/over frequency
  • 87 differential (compares current in vs out of a zone) · 87T transformer differential
  • 49 thermal overload · 46 negative sequence / phase unbalance
  • 86 lockout relay — trips and stays tripped until hand-reset
  • 79 auto-reclose (utility feeders, not usually inside a plant)
Procedure

What HV switching actually involves

  1. A written switching program, checked and approved before anyone touches anything
  2. Operate in sequence, each step signed off as it's done
  3. Isolate, then test for dead with an approved HV voltage detector — proved on a known source before and after
  4. Apply earths (integral earth switch or portable earths) — this is what makes it safe, not the isolator
  5. Permit to work issued to the person doing the job; danger tags and locks applied
  6. Reverse at the end, permit cancelled, earths removed, and only then re-energise
Transformer

The transformer's own protection

Buchholz relay — detects gas from an internal fault (alarm) or a surge of oil (trip). WTI/OTI — winding and oil temperature indicators, alarm then trip. Pressure relief device. Dyn11 — delta HV, star LV with neutral out, 30° phase shift. That neutral is where the LV MEN system starts.

Arc flash

The hazard nobody sees coming

An arc fault releases enormous energy as heat, light, pressure and molten metal in milliseconds. Distance and time are your only real protections.

Sites will have an arc flash study, incident-energy labels on boards, and PPE categories. Read the label before you open the door — on LV boards too, not just HV.

The switchroom itself

18 min

The board is only half the job. The room it lands in decides whether it can be installed, maintained, cooled, and escaped from — and most site problems are room problems, not board problems.

TL;DR

Three things ruin switchroom installs: not enough space in front of the board, nowhere sensible for the cables to come in, and nowhere for the heat to go. All three are visible on the drawings weeks before anyone turns up with a truck.

Space

Clearances and escape

  • Working space in front of a switchboard is set by AS/NZS 3000 and depends on the board — get the actual figure from the Wiring Rules for the case, don't work from memory.
  • Door swing — switchroom doors open outward, in the direction of escape, and must be openable from inside without a key.
  • Two exits are often required on long rooms so you are never trapped past a fault.
  • Board doors open into the space — check the escape path is still clear with every cubicle door swung wide, not just when it's tidy.
  • No storage. Ever. Switchrooms attract brooms, ladders and boxes; all of them are defects.
Heat

Ventilation and derating

  • Losses come from busbar I²R, device losses, transformers and VSDs. A big MSB dissipates kilowatts.
  • AS/NZS 61439 rates the assembly at a reference ambient (typically 35 °C average / 40 °C peak). Hotter room = lower real current rating.
  • Options: natural ventilation with vermin-proof louvres, forced ventilation with filters, or air conditioning. Filters need a maintenance regime or they become the problem.
  • Anti-condensation heaters in outdoor or humid installations, thermostat controlled — and remember they're a live circuit inside the board when everything else is off.
Cables

Getting cables in and supported

  • Top or bottom entry — decided early; it changes the GA, the cable zone and the ladder route.
  • Bending radius — big XLPE cable has a large minimum radius. Plan the cable zone depth for it, or the cables physically won't fit.
  • Ladder, tray or basket, with support spacing to suit the load, and cleats on single-core runs to survive fault forces.
  • Single-core cables must be arranged in trefoil or flat with correct spacing, and steel gland plates need non-magnetic inserts or slotting — otherwise you induce circulating currents and heat the plate.
  • Segregation — power away from data, comms and fire services. Crossings at right angles where unavoidable.
  • Fire-stopping every penetration back to the wall's rating, with the certificate to prove it.
Earthing

Bonding the whole room

  • Main earth bar in the MSB, sized and accessible, with a labelled connection for every incoming earth.
  • Cable ladder, tray, trunking, gland plates, doors, escutcheons and structural steel all bonded.
  • Earthing conductor sizes come from AS/NZS 3000 — they are calculated, not chosen.
  • Where there's HV on site, the HV and LV earthing arrangement comes out of an AS 2067 earthing study. Don't improvise it.

On site: install, test, commission, isolate

18 min

The board leaves the shop tested and works on the bench. Everything that goes wrong afterwards happens in this module.

The one that matters

Prove — Test — Prove. Prove your tester works on a known live source. Test the conductors you're about to touch. Prove your tester still works. A tester that failed silently between step 1 and step 2 is how people die.

The commissioning sequence

Before power

Dead tests, in this order

  1. Visual — against the drawings, at the current revision. Foreign objects, swarf, loose ferrules, missing barriers, correct labels.
  2. Torque check — every bolted joint and lug to spec, witness marked.
  3. Point-to-point / buzz out — continuity test every control wire against the wiring schedule. Tedious, and it catches the errors that would otherwise cost a day.
  4. Earth continuity — every door, escutcheon, gland plate and enclosure back to the main earth bar. Low resistance, measured, recorded.
  5. Insulation resistance — 500 V or 1000 V megger, all conductors to earth and between conductors. Disconnect electronics first (VSDs, PLCs, meters, SPDs) or you'll destroy them.
  6. Phase sequence and correct phase rotation at every outgoing way.
After power

Live tests and functional checks

  1. Energise the control circuit first, power circuit isolated. Prove the logic before anything can turn.
  2. RCD tests — trip current and trip time at rated and 5× rated.
  3. Primary or secondary injection on protection relays and trip units, against the settings schedule.
  4. Functional test every starter — start, stop, seal-in, overload trip, lamps, PLC feedback. Work the I/O schedule row by row.
  5. Interlocks and E-stops — physically operate each one and confirm what it stops. Every single one, not a sample.
  6. Rotation check on load — bump the motor, confirm direction before coupling.
  7. Thermal scan under load once the plant is running, to catch loose joints.
ℹ
Red-line as you go

Every difference between the drawing and what you actually built or found gets marked in red on the drawing, on the day. Those red-lines become the as-built drawings — which are what the next person, in five years, will bet their safety on. An unrecorded change is a trap you set for someone else.

Site reality

Deliveries and lifting

Check the board against the GA before it goes on the truck: shipping splits, lifting lugs, plinth, weight, and whether it fits through the switchroom door and around the corner. Re-connecting busbar joints on site is a torque-and-record job, not a "she'll be right" job.

Site reality

The punch list

Everything not finished, written down, with who owns it and when. If it's not on the list it doesn't exist and it won't get fixed. Sign off items as they're closed and keep the list visible.

Site reality

Handover pack

As-built drawings, test sheets, settings schedules, O&M manuals, spares list, and the arc flash / labelling data. The job isn't finished when it runs — it's finished when the pack is handed over and accepted.

Test equipment: what it proves, and what it lies about

16 min

Instruments don't measure reality — they measure what they're built to measure, under conditions they assume. Knowing where each one is honest is what separates a result from a number.

The big one

A normal multimeter has a very high input impedance, so it happily reads a voltage that can't deliver any current. You never prove a conductor dead with a multimeter. You prove it dead with a low-impedance two-pole tester, and you prove the tester on a known live source before and after.

Safety

CAT ratings — the number that keeps the meter from exploding

The category describes the transient energy the instrument can survive at the point you're using it, not the steady voltage.

  • CAT II — appliances, socket outlets at the end of a run
  • CAT III — distribution boards, fixed installation, busbars in a DB
  • CAT IV — the origin of the installation: service entrance, main switchboard, outdoor lines

At a main switchboard you want CAT IV rated at the working voltage, with fused leads and shrouded probes. A cheap CAT II meter used on an MSB is how meters become shrapnel.

Accuracy

True-RMS vs averaging

An averaging meter assumes the waveform is a clean sine and applies a fixed correction factor. Feed it the chopped output of a VSD, the current drawn by a switch-mode supply, or anything harmonic-rich, and it can be wildly wrong — often reading low.

Rule: anything downstream of a drive, a rectifier or a dimmer needs a true-RMS instrument, and even then a VSD's PWM output is not something a handheld meter measures meaningfully.

Records

A test result with no context is worthless

Every recorded result needs: what was tested (the tag), what instrument (make, model, serial), when it was last calibrated, the test voltage or current used, the ambient conditions where they matter, the value, the pass criterion, and who did it.

Calibration dates are the first thing an auditor checks. An out-of-calibration instrument invalidates every result taken with it.

Your angle

What you can do with all this, unlicensed

You can write the test plan, specify the instruments and their calibration requirements, define the pass criteria, witness the tests, check the recorded results against the design, and reject a test sheet that's incomplete or internally inconsistent.

That is a genuinely valuable role, and it's the one that catches the errors — because the person holding the leads is concentrating on the leads.

Ask at work this week

  • "Can I see the calibration register for our test equipment?"
  • "Show me a two-pole tester and a proving unit, and walk me through prove–test–prove once."
  • "What do we disconnect before an insulation resistance test, and is there a checklist for it?"

3E-stop released? Selector in the right position? PLC actually calling?
4Walk the rung left→right with the meter referenced to N. First 0 V reading = the break.
5Coil energised but nothing turns? Now it's the power circuit: breaker, all three phases, motor.
6Contactor chattering? Low control voltage or a marginal contact upstream.
7Trips again straight away? It's a real fault. Stop resetting it.
8Red-line anything you found that differs from the drawing.

The best habit in the trade

Keep a small notebook. Every time you hear a word or see a symbol you don't know, write it down — don't stop the conversation. Look up five of them at the end of the day. Within a month there will be almost nothing left to write down.

SHEET 01

Relationship Decision Tree

Ask the questions in order. Stop at the first yes.

Can B start only after A is completely finished? └─ YES ──▶ FS Finish-to-Start ← ~90% of your logic └─ NO ──▶ next question Does B start partway through A, then run alongside it? └─ YES ──▶ SS + lag Start-to-Start "Control wiring starts 3 days after power wiring starts" Can B start early, but not FINISH until A finishes? └─ YES ──▶ FF + lag Finish-to-Finish "Cable labelling finishes 2 days after terminations finish" Must A finish before B can finish, where B is already running? └─ YES ──▶ SF Start-to-Finish ← almost never. Justify in writing.
TypeSwitchboard exampleHealth target
FSBusbar torque & inspect → Install ACB cassettes≥ 90% of links
SS+2Erect cubicles 1–4 → Erect cubicles 5–8 (crew moves through)use with care
FF+2Terminations complete → Cable schedule verification completeuse with care
SFShift handover; temporary supply removed as permanent supply energises< 1%, justify each

Lags — when legitimate

Powder coat cureFS+2
Concrete plinth cureFS+7
Contractual FAT noticeFS+5
Sea freight transitFS+35

Lags — when a defect

"Client will take a while"make it an activity
Lag > the activity durationhidden work
Any negative lag (lead)use SS instead
Lag used to force a datethat's a constraint
The rule: a lag is time nobody owns, nobody resources, and nobody can progress. If real work happens in that gap, it must be an activity. If real waiting happens, a lag is fine — but write down why.
SHEET 02

Float, Critical Path & the Passes

The engine, in one page.

Forward pass — earliest dates

ES = latest EF of all predecessors
EF = ES + Duration − 1

Run left to right. Answers: how soon can this happen?

Backward pass — latest dates

LF = earliest LS of all successors
LS = LF − Duration + 1

Run right to left. Answers: how late can this happen without moving the end date?

Float

Total Float = LS − ES (or LF − EF)
  How long this activity can slip before
  the PROJECT finish moves.

Free Float = earliest ES of successors − EF − 1
  How long it can slip before the NEXT
  activity moves.
Free float ≤ Total float. Always. If free float is 0 but total float is 12, you can't move without shoving your successor — but the end date still holds.
TermMeaningWhat to do about it
Critical pathThe chain with the least total float (usually 0). Controls the finish date.Protect it. Resource it first. Report it weekly.
Longest pathThe chain that actually drives the finish, traced backwards through driving relationships. Ignores float value.On multi-calendar jobs like yours, trust longest path over TF=0. Calendars distort float.
Negative floatLatest dates are earlier than earliest dates. You are already late — or a constraint is lying.Find the cause first. Constraint-generated negative float is noise, not delay.
Driving relationshipThe one predecessor actually setting this activity's start.Trace it backwards to find the true delay source in 30 seconds.
High float (>44d)Usually a missing successor, not genuine slack.Check for open ends before you celebrate.
SHEET 03

Constraints — Safe, Risky, Never

Every constraint is a confession that logic is missing.

ConstraintEffectVerdictWhen it's acceptable
Start On or AfterCannot start before a date. Can still push later.SafeSite access date, contract commencement, client-imposed no-earlier-than.
Finish On or BeforeGenerates negative float if breached — a visible alarm.SafeContractual completion dates. Use it to surface pressure, not to hide it.
Start On or BeforePulls activity earlier; can create negative float.RiskyRare. Usually better modelled as logic.
Finish On or AfterHolds an activity out.RiskySeasonal or shutdown windows.
Start On / Finish OnPins to an exact date, both directions.RiskyA genuinely fixed event: a scheduled outage, a witnessed test date.
As Late As PossibleConsumes all free float on the activity.RiskyDeliberately delaying delivery to avoid site storage. Legitimate — but document it.
Mandatory StartOverrides logic. Predecessors slip; this does not move.NeverEffectively never. It breaks CPM.
Mandatory FinishOverrides logic. Hides delay completely.NeverEffectively never. The most destructive field in P6.
Expected FinishRecalculates remaining duration to hit a date.RiskyUpdating from a supplier's promised date. Understand what it does to RD first.
Before you add any constraint, ask: "What real-world dependency am I failing to model?" Nine times out of ten there is a missing predecessor. Add the logic instead. If you must constrain, record the reason in the activity Notebook — your future self, and the delay expert, will need it.

The dialog everyone clicks through without reading.

Every single time you press F9: read the log. P6 tells you how many activities have no predecessor, no successor, are out of sequence, and how many constraints exist. That log is a free schedule health check and almost nobody reads it.

DCMA 14-Point Health Check

The industry-standard audit. Run it before anyone else does.

#MetricThresholdWhat failing it means
01Logic — missing predecessor or successor≤ 5%Open ends. Activities floating free of the network.
02Leads (negative lags)0Hidden overlap that distorts float.
03Lags≤ 5%Untracked, unresourced time.
04Relationship types — FS≥ 90%Over-use of SS/FF usually means compressed logic hiding risk.
05Hard constraints≤ 5%Dates typed in place of logic.
06High float (TF > 44 days)≤ 5%Usually a missing successor, not real slack.
07Negative float0Late, or constrained. Either way, explain it.
08High duration (> 44 days remaining)≤ 5%Too coarse to manage or progress honestly.
09Invalid dates0Actuals in the future or forecasts in the past. Data date discipline failure.

12Critical path testPassAdd a 600d lag to a critical activity. The finish must move ~600d. If it doesn't, your network is broken.

CPLI = (Critical path length + Total float) ÷ Critical path length
BEI  = Activities actually completed ÷ Activities baselined to complete by the data date
SHEET 06

The Weekly Update Cycle

Same eleven steps, every week, forever. Do them in order.

  1. Back up first. Export the XER before you touch anything. P6 has no meaningful undo.
  2. Collect progress from the factory floor and site — actual starts, actual finishes, and remaining duration. Not percentages. Ask "how many more days?"
  3. Set the new data date — same day of the week, every week. Friday close of business is standard.
  4. Enter actual starts for activities that began. Never a date after the data date.
  5. Enter actual finishes for activities that completed.
  6. Re-estimate remaining duration on activities in progress. This is the number that matters. Do not accept P6's arithmetic guess.
  7. Check for out-of-sequence progress — work that started before its predecessor finished. Decide deliberately: fix the logic, or accept it under Retained Logic.
  8. Press F9. Read the scheduling log.
  9. Compare to baseline — variance columns, baseline bars, milestone slip.
  10. Identify the driving delay. Trace the longest path back from the finish milestone. Name the activity and the owner.
  11. Issue the updated programme, the two-week lookahead, and the narrative. Same day, every week.
The three cardinal sins of updating: typing a % complete instead of a remaining duration; leaving activities with actual dates in the future; and moving the data date before entering the actuals. Any one of them makes the update worthless.
SHEET 07

Earned Value — Every Formula

Three numbers in, everything else out.

The three inputs

BAC Budget At Completion — total budget
PV   Planned Value — budgeted cost of work
       SCHEDULED by now
EV   Earned Value — budgeted cost of work
       ACTUALLY DONE by now
AC   Actual Cost — what you have really spent

Variances

CV = EV − AC   negative = over budget
SV = EV − PV   negative = behind schedule

Indices

CPI = EV ÷ AC   <1 = losing money
SPI = EV ÷ PV   <1 = behind plan

Forecasts

EAC = BAC ÷ CPI
       current performance continues ← default

EAC = AC + (BAC − EV)
       the overrun was a one-off

EAC = AC + [(BAC − EV) ÷ (CPI × SPI)]
       schedule pressure costs money too

ETC = EAC − AC   cost still to come
VAC = BAC − EAC   forecast over/under
TCPI = (BAC − EV) ÷ (BAC − AC)
       efficiency needed from here
Worked example. BAC $2.4M. At Update 3: PV $800k, EV $700k, AC $780k.
CPI = 0.90 · SPI = 0.88 · EAC = $2.67M · VAC = −$267k.
Translation: you are 12% behind and every dollar buys 90c. Expect to finish $267k over.
The trap: SPI always drifts back to 1.00 as a project finishes, even on a job that lands six months late — because eventually EV catches up to PV. Never report schedule performance from SPI alone. Report the milestone dates.
SHEET 08

Calendars & Durations

Where switchgear schedules quietly go wrong.

CalendarPatternApplies toGetting it wrong costs you
FACTORY-5DMon–Fri, 8hEngineering, drafting, assembly, wiring, works testing, FATOverstating factory output if you leave site work on it
SITE-6DMon–Sat, 10hOffload, setting, terminations, commissioning~20% understated site productivity — you look slower than you are
CAL-7D7 days, 24hSupplier lead times, vendor manufacture, sea and road freightThe big one. A 90-day ACB lead time on a 5-day calendar adds ≈36 calendar days of fiction

Duration types — pick before you assign resources

TypeWhat stays fixedUse for
Fixed Duration & Units/TimeDuration and crew rate; total units flexMost factory work. Your default.
Fixed Duration & UnitsDuration and total hours; rate flexesFixed-window activities like a 3-day FAT.
Fixed UnitsTotal hours; duration flexes with crewCable pulling — add labour, finish sooner.
Fixed Units/TimeCrew rate; duration flexes with scopeFixed crew, variable scope.
Duration sanity test. Every duration must trace to something real: a production rate (2 cubicles/day), a supplier's written lead time, a crew size × quantity, or a contract clause. "Two weeks feels about right" is not an estimate — it is a guess wearing a suit.

The hard part

Money you might need in 6–12 months should not be in shares. Not in the S&P 500, not in Nasdaq, not in a stock fund. That isn't caution — it's arithmetic. A 12-month horizon isn't long enough to recover from a bad quarter, and the one time you'd be forced to sell is exactly when prices are down.

The seven things

  • Build a $15k emergency fund first. Right now $130k is all you have. Without a buffer, one bad month forces you to sell investments at the worst moment.
  • The First Home Super Saver is the best return available to you. Salary-sacrificing into it converts 32c tax into 15c tax. That's roughly a 15% guaranteed gain. No market beats that reliably over 12 months.
  • You may already have enough deposit. Under the expanded Home Guarantee Scheme, an eligible first home buyer can buy with 5% down and no LMI. On an $850k place that's ~$42.5k, not $170k.
  • Franked dividends are genuinely special for you. At a 32% marginal rate, a fully franked Australian dividend arrives essentially tax-free. Foreign dividends don't do this.
  • Your deposit isn't the constraint — your borrowing power is. On $113k alone you can likely borrow around $510k, so a Perth median house at $850k is out of reach no matter how well you invest. Applying with your partner roughly doubles that ceiling. That one decision matters more than every allocation choice here.
  • If you buy, an offset account beats shares. A dollar in offset saves you the mortgage rate, tax-free — worth about 8.8% pre-tax at your marginal rate. That's more than shares are expected to return, with none of the risk.
  • The Pakistan sleeve is a no for this money. Not because it's bad, but because the currency drag and your Australian tax residency remove nearly all the benefit. Detail in its own section below.

Hedged or not

When global markets crash, the Australian dollar almost always falls too. If you're unhedged, that falling AUD cushions your loss — in 2008 the S&P 500 lost about 55% in US dollars but only around 28% for an unhedged Australian investor. The honest scorecard

Where buying genuinely wins

  • Leverage. Your deposit controls a property worth many times it. Nobody will lend you half a million dollars at 6% to buy index funds. If prices rise 5%, you gain 5% of the whole property, not 5% of your deposit — that multiplier is the real argument for buying.
  • Forced saving. A mortgage makes you build equity whether you feel like it or not. Most people save far more as owners than as renters.
  • No capital gains tax on your main residence — a genuinely large exemption over decades.
  • Security. No lease ending, no inspections, no landlord selling.

Where renting and investing wins

  • Liquidity. You can sell shares on a Tuesday. Selling a house takes months and about 2.5%.
  • Mobility. At a 1-in-4 chance of leaving Australia, this is worth real money — see the section below.
  • No transaction drag. Buying and selling a house costs 5–8% all-in. Buying an ETF costs $10.
  • Diversification. Buying puts your entire net worth into one asset, in one suburb, in one city, funded by debt.
The catch

Rent-and-invest beats buying on paper far more often than it does in practice, for one reason: most renters don't invest the difference. They spend it. If you genuinely will not maintain the discipline over twenty years, buying wins by default — not because the maths says so, but because the mortgage enforces the saving for you.

Can you time it?

Straight answer

You asked for timing forecasts for buying versus investing. I'm going to be direct with you rather than give you a number I'd be making up.

No

Nobody can reliably forecast house prices or share markets over one to two years — not me, not economists, not the bank forecasts you'll read. Anyone giving you a confident 12-month number is guessing with a chart attached. What I can give you is what's actually knowable, which turns out to be more useful.

What we do know

  • Time in the market beats timing it, on the evidence. Studies of perfect-timing versus buy-immediately consistently show the gap is small, and that waiting for a better entry usually costs more than it saves — because markets rise in most years.
  • Your binding constraint is borrowing capacity, not price forecasts. If rates fall, your capacity rises — but so does everyone else's, which pushes prices up. Falling rates are not the buying opportunity people expect.
  • Perth and Melbourne have moved very differently. Perth has run hard recently while Melbourne has been broadly flat for several years. That's an argument for looking at Melbourne on relative value — but "it hasn't gone up lately" has never been a reliable forecast that it will.
  • The two things you control are worth more than the forecast: your savings rate and your borrowing capacity. Both respond to your decisions. Neither requires predicting anything.

A rule instead of a forecast

Since you can't forecast, set conditions in advance and act when they're met. This removes the decision from the moment, which is when people get it wrong.

1
Buy when you and your partner have both confirmed you're doing this together, your combined borrowing capacity covers the property, and you've held the deposit in cash for at least three months. Not before — regardless of what prices are doing.
2
Keep investing when the house is more than two years away or genuinely undecided. Set the monthly amount and don't change it based on headlines.
3
Revisit on a date, not on a feeling — put a reminder 14 months out. Decide then with fresh numbers rather than reacting between now and then.

The 1-in-4 you mentioned

Expensive if missed

You put the chance of leaving Australia at around one in four in the next five years. That's high enough to change the recommendation, and it interacts with a tax rule most people don't know about.

The trap

If you own an Australian home and sell it while you are a foreign resident for tax purposes, you can lose the main residence exemption entirely — not proportionally for the years you were away, but for the whole ownership period. On a property that gained $200,000, that can mean a tax bill in the tens of thousands that an Australian resident would not have paid at all. Verify the current rules and the limited exceptions with an accountant before you buy, not after you leave.

If you buy and then leave

  • Sell before departing, and the exemption is intact — but you're forced to sell on someone else's timetable.
  • Keep it and rent it out, and you're a landlord from another country, paying management fees and non-resident tax rates on the rent from the first dollar.
  • Victoria also charges an absentee owner surcharge on land tax, which is a recurring cost on top.

If you rent and invest instead

  • Shares travel. You can hold or sell them from anywhere with a few clicks.
  • Leaving Australia triggers a deemed disposal on shares — but you can elect to defer it, and you control the timing either way.
  • No property to manage, no tenant, no surcharge, no forced sale.
How I'd weigh it

A one-in-four chance isn't enough to rule out buying, but it is enough to rule out buying marginally — stretching to the edge of your borrowing capacity for a place you'd need to sell in three years. If you buy, buy something you'd be comfortable holding and renting out from overseas. And get the residency and main-residence rules checked by an accountant before you sign, because that conversation costs a few hundred dollars and the mistake costs tens of thousands.

Money market fund Yes — core holding

What it is. A fund holding very short-term government and bank paper. In practice, for you, an Australian high-interest savings account or term deposit does the same job with less complexity and government guarantee protection up to $250k per institution.

Past. Tracked the cash rate — roughly 2.1% a year over the past decade, dragged down by the near-zero years, and around 4% recently. It has never had a negative year. That's the whole point of it.

Forward. Whatever the cash rate does. Rates have been falling, so assume this drifts down rather than up. After 32% tax and ~2.8% inflation, a 4% return is roughly break-even in real terms — you're preserving purchasing power, not growing it.

Shariah option. Conventional savings interest is riba. The compliant equivalents are an Islamic cash-management fund (Hejaz and Crescent both offer one) or a sukuk fund. Expect slightly lower net returns after their higher fees.

This is the single most important holding you own right now, and that's not a compliment to it — it's a statement about your timeline. Every dollar you might need within 12 months belongs here. Shop the rate: the gap between a lazy account and a competitive one is often more than 1%, which on $85,000 is $850 a year for one afternoon's work.

Fixed income fund Small role only

What it is. A bond fund — lending to governments and companies. Prices move opposite to interest rates, which is why it isn't the same as cash.

Past. Poor: about 2.0% a year over ten years, because 2022 delivered −9.7%, the worst bond year in modern history. That year taught a lot of "conservative" investors that bonds are not cash.

Making the money work — dividends

You asked whether you can get income from this. Yes, and in Australia there's a genuine edge — but it isn't where most people think it is.

The edge

Australian companies have already paid 30% tax on their profits before paying you. You get a credit for that tax. At your 32% marginal rate, a fully franked dividend arrives almost entirely tax-free — you owe about 2c in the dollar. The same dollar of interest from a savings account costs you 32c.

The franked row includes the grossed-up credit. This is why Australian investors on middle-to-high incomes hold local shares even when global returns look better.

Three things to be honest about

  • A dividend isn't free money. The share price drops by roughly the dividend when it's paid. What's genuinely valuable here is the franking credit, not the dividend itself.
  • High-yield funds have a cost. Funds like VHY yield 5–6% mostly franked, but they concentrate into banks and miners and have generally lagged the broad index on total return. You're trading growth for income.
  • For a 12-month horizon it's not worth it. A savings account pays a similar headline rate with zero chance of capital loss. Chasing a 5% franked yield while risking a 20% capital fall is a bad trade when the money is spoken for.
My recommendation

Don't build an income portfolio yet. If the house doesn't happen and this becomes long-term money, then adding an Australian index fund for the franking makes real sense — perhaps a quarter of the growth sleeve. Reinvest the dividends automatically; at your stage you want compounding, not cash flow. Note that franking has no Shariah-compliant equivalent, since compliant funds screen out the banks that generate most of it — that's a genuine cost of the compliant route, worth roughly 1% a year after tax.

Bottom line

Keep about 88% of the $130k completely safe, put roughly $30k into a single global share fund and forget it exists, and put your monthly savings through First Home Super Saver rather than into the market. The tax saving is a bigger, more certain win than anything the allocation choice can deliver over 12 months.

The reason isn't pessimism about markets — it's that your timeline and your certainty don't match. You're moderately comfortable with risk, which would normally justify a decent equity allocation. But "might buy a house, depends on prices" plus "this $130k is everything" plus "no emergency fund" is a combination where the downside of being wrong is severe and the upside is modest. On $100,000 over 12 months, the difference between cash and a 100% share portfolio is maybe $4,000 in the good case — and $25,000 in the bad one.

The $30,000 growth sleeve exists because you're not certain about the house, and because if you hold nothing but cash for three years while markets run, you'll feel it. Size it so that a 30% fall — around $9,000 — is annoying rather than plan-changing. That's the number I'd anchor to.

On the Pakistan side: your dual citizenship is genuinely useful for family, property and options later in life. It is not useful for this portfolio. Australian tax residency strips the VPS of its point, and the rupee has historically taken back most of what Pakistani markets gave. Keep that connection for things it's actually good for.

On the 14-month question you raised: it's a genuinely sensible horizon, and better than 12 for a specific reason — holding past twelve months halves the tax on your gains. If you're going to delay the decision anyway, delaying past the one-year mark rather than just short of it is close to free money. But be honest that 14 months is still short enough that a bad quarter can undo a year of gains, which is why the deposit itself stays in cash even on that timeline.

On never buying: it's a real option and I've modelled it properly rather than treating it as the fallback. Renting and investing wins on the numbers more often than people expect, especially at Melbourne's growth rates and your $520 a week rent. It fails for one reason only — most people don't actually invest the difference. If you'd genuinely automate it and leave it alone for twenty years, renting and investing is defensible. If you know you'd spend it, the mortgage is a savings mechanism disguised as a house.

The single most useful thing this expansion turned up: at $113k on your own, you can borrow roughly $510k. Perth's median house is around $850k. That gap doesn't close by picking better investments — it closes by applying with your partner, buying something cheaper, or waiting for rates to fall. Any plan that ignores it is fiction, however good the portfolio looks.

And the thing I'd most want you to act on isn't an investment at all: find out what your super is invested in this week. On a 30-year horizon that single decision will very likely outweigh everything else discussed here.

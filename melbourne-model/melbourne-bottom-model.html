The one rule that survives every cycle: Melbourne has never put in a durable bottom while the RBA was still tightening and the market expected no cuts. In 2019 the trough landed the same month as the first cut. In 2012 it landed mid-easing-cycle. In 2023 it landed as the hiking cycle was visibly ending. In 1992 rates collapsed but the bottom took years longer — because a banking crisis had broken the transmission from rates to credit. Today the cash rate is 4.35% after three 2026 hikes, and no major bank forecasts a cut before May 2027 at the earliest.

The two failure modes, named

  • It signals far too early when credit breaks. In 1992 the cash rate had collapsed from 17.5% to under 6% — the model's heaviest factor screamed “bottom”. But the State Bank of Victoria and Pyramid Building Society had failed, and cheap money could not reach borrowers. Melbourne did not regain 1989 prices until 1996. If a credit event hits, ignore the rate factor.
  • It is late at sharp, rate-driven V-bottoms. In late 2022 the market front-ran the end of the hiking cycle. The trough was January 2023; the model was still saying “approaching”. Cost: roughly two months of the cheapest prices.

Limits you should hold against this

  • The weights were chosen knowing the answers. This is a reconstruction, not an out-of-sample test. Real predictive accuracy will be lower than 5/8.
  • n = 8. Eight cycles cannot support a confidence interval. The percentages here are calibrated judgement, not statistics.
  • Historical factor readings are reconstructed from documented conditions, not from a retrieved quarterly dataset — the network policy in this environment blocked the primary sources.
  • It targets nominal bottoms. The inflation-adjusted bottom is typically later, and with CPI at 3.8% that gap is currently wide.
  • It has never been tested against a tax change like the July 2027 one. There is no historical analogue for abolishing negative gearing on established housing.

One structural factor sits underneath all five scenarios. From 1 July 2027, negative gearing ends for established homes bought after 12 May 2026, and the 50% CGT discount is replaced by indexation plus a 30% minimum tax. New builds keep both concessions. Established outer-suburb houses — exactly your target stock — are the most investor-exposed segment of the market, so this removes a slice of the demand you would otherwise be bidding against. It is bearish for prices and good for you as an owner-occupier. It is also why the model keeps meaningful probability on a 2028 bottom: the full effect does not land until the 2027–28 tax year.

The recommendation

Based on the available evidence, I would currently WAIT — but start inspecting in about nine months, not at the bottom.

The model reads 22/100. That is not a close call, and it is not a generic “markets are uncertain” hedge — it is four specific readings all pointing the same way:

  • Listings are at their highest since SQM's records began in 2010, up 42.8% year-on-year in Melbourne. Bottoms happen when listings peak and fall. They are still climbing.
  • The RBA hiked three times in 2026 and no major bank forecasts a cut before May 2027. Melbourne has never bottomed durably before the rate cycle turned, except when a credit crisis was doing the work instead.
  • Price falls are accelerating, not decelerating — −3.9% over three months against −4.7% over twelve. A bottom is preceded by falls getting smaller.
  • The July 2027 tax change is still ahead of the market, and it removes investor demand from precisely the established outer-suburb stock you are targeting.

The practical move

Waiting costs you nothing in access — you can already buy in your band. It costs you rent, and it risks the bottom arriving while you are not ready. So use the wait: get finance pre-approved and renewed through the window, and start physically inspecting in the middle-ring suburbs (Glenroy, Sunshine West, Braybrook) from around mid-2027, so that when three of the four triggers fire you already know what a fair price looks like on the street. The buyers who catch bottoms are the ones who were already looking.

Important This is a model of a market, not financial advice, and it does not know your income, deposit, job security, rent, or how much a two-year wait costs you. A stable buyer with a long horizon who finds the right house at a fair price in 2026 is not making a mistake — the model is about the average outcome across a market, and you buy exactly one house.

Honest limits of this build

  • No primary-source downloads. The network policy in the environment that built this blocked direct access to rba.gov.au, abs.gov.au, domain.com.au, abc.net.au and tradingeconomics.com. Every figure comes from search-result citations of those sources, not from the source files themselves. Treat each number as needing one verification click before you act on it.
  • No suburb-level time series. Suburb medians are single recent snapshots, so I cannot tell you which of these suburbs is falling fastest — only where each sits today.
  • The long price history is eight sourced points and an interpolated line. Shown explicitly in Panel 2.
  • Auction clearance is a Melbourne-wide figure. Outer growth corridors sell far more by private treaty than auction, so clearance is a weaker signal for your specific target stock than for the inner ring.
  • The model has one structural blind spot: it assumes the future rhymes with 1992–2023. The July 2027 tax reform has no historical analogue in Australian housing.

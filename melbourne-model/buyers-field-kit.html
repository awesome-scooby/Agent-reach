Step 2

Build a comparable-sales file

A "comp" is a sale that tells you what your target is worth. Most buyers do this badly by comparing asking prices. Asking prices are marketing. Only sold prices count.

Rules for a valid comp

  • Sold in the last 3 months. In a market falling ~1% a month, a six-month-old comp overstates value by ~6%.
  • Same suburb, or an adjoining one you've checked is comparable. Never average across a freeway or a rail line.
  • Same type: house to house, same bedroom count, land size within ~20%.
  • At least five comps. Three is an anecdote. Discard the highest and lowest before you average.
  • Note the sale method. A passed-in auction that sold later privately is a weak sale — that's a seller who had to take less.

What to record for each comp

  • Address, sold price, sold date, days on market
  • Beds / baths / cars / land size (m²)
  • Sale method: auction, private, passed in then sold
  • Condition: original, part-renovated, renovated
  • Price per m² of land — the single most useful number in the outer suburbs, where you are largely buying land

Then: your fair offer is the median of your five comps, adjusted down for every month since they sold while the market is falling, and adjusted for condition.

Step 3

Read the Section 32 before you fall in love

Ask the agent for it on day one. If they stall, that itself is information.

The Section 32 Vendor Statement is the disclosure document a Victorian seller must give you before you sign. Your conveyancer reviews it properly — but you should read it first, because it is free and it kills bad properties early.

Look forWhy it mattersIf you find it
Cooling off: 3 clear business days on a private sale, and the penalty for withdrawing is 0.2% of the price or $100, whichever is greater — about $1,242 on a $621,000 house. There is no cooling off at auction, or in the 3 days either side of one. That is why every check in this kit happens before auction day, not after. Separately: if a Section 32 is defective, you may have a right to rescind even after cooling off has passed — another reason a conveyancer reads it, not just you.
Step 4

Test the commute before you inspect

Your whole plan — buy, live in it, move near work, rent it out — exists because of commute distance. So measure it properly before you get emotionally attached to a house, not after.
  • Never trust the default map estimate. It gives you an off-peak number. Set the departure time to the actual weekday hour you'd leave.
  • Drive it once for real, on a Tuesday or Wednesday morning, from the actual street. Wyndham and Melton corridor traffic is not well modelled.
  • Check the train, not just the line. Werribee, Melton and Craigieburn lines differ hugely in frequency and reliability. Peak frequency and whether you'll get a seat both matter more than distance.
  • Measure the walk to the station in minutes, and check parking — station car parks in the growth corridors fill before 7:30am.
  • Double it and add the return. A 50-minute each-way commute is about 8 hours a week, or 400 hours a year. Price that against what you save on the house.
The one defect most likely to cost you money in Melton, Wyndham or Werribee is soil movement. The western growth corridor sits on highly reactive clay — classes M to H1 under AS 2870, with H2 pockets in the older Werribee and Wyndham Vale flats. The ground swells in a wet winter and shrinks in a dry summer, and the house moves with it. Wall cracks, sloping floors, sticking doors and slab heave are the standard findings. Underpinning a heaved slab runs into tens of thousands and is rarely covered by insurance.

Reactive clay: what to actually look at

Signs the ground is moving

  • Diagonal cracks radiating from window and door corners — the classic signature. Vertical hairlines in render are usually cosmetic; diagonals through brickwork are not.
  • Cracks wider at one end (tapered) rather than parallel-sided.
  • Doors and windows binding, especially several on the same side of the house.
  • Floors out of level — put a marble or a spirit level down in each room. Take a $10 level with you.
  • Gaps opening between cornice and wall, or skirting lifting off the floor.
  • Recent patch-and-paint on one wall only — ask what was repaired and when. Fresh filler over a structural crack is a selling tactic.

What makes it worse — check these on site

  • Stormwater discharging next to the slab. Follow every downpipe to where it ends. If it empties onto soil beside the footing, that is the cause of a lot of movement in the west.
  • Ground falling towards the house instead of away from it.
  • Large water-seeking trees close to the building — especially willows, poplars, liquidambars, eucalypts. Rule of thumb: trouble if the distance to the house is less than the tree's mature height.
  • Leaking taps, pipes or air-conditioner condensate soaking one patch of ground.
  • A recently removed large tree — the soil rehydrates and can heave for years afterward. Ask.

Categories follow the damage classification used with AS 2870 and AS 4349.1 pre-purchase inspections. Width alone does not determine the classification — a narrow crack that is actively growing can be worse than a wide dormant one. Anything from 5 mm up should be assessed by a structural engineer, not a general building inspector.

Ask the agent these — and know which answers bind them

QuestionWhyBinding?

Nothing an agent says verbally is worth anything in a dispute. If an answer matters to your decision, get it in writing by email, or get it into the contract as a special condition. Agents legally cannot mislead you, but "I don't recall saying that" is very hard to disprove.

Go back a second time — and change one variable

  • In the rain. The only way to see where water actually goes, whether the yard drains, and whether the roof leaks. Worth waiting a week for.
  • On a weekday, at the hour you'd leave for work. Traffic noise, school traffic, street parking pressure, and sun position all change completely.
  • At night. Street lighting, neighbour noise, who's around, whether you'd walk from the station.
  • Open every cupboard, every window, and the manhole. Inspections are choreographed; the roof space is where nobody has staged anything.
  • Run the taps and the shower simultaneously and watch the pressure. Then check the ceiling of the room below the bathroom.

01Your diagnosis & priority map

Your problem is not English. It is that you are running a quality filter designed for engineering drawings on a conversation that only needs a draft. You wait until the sentence is correct, complete and worth saying — and by then the room has moved on. Everything below is built to shorten the gap between "I have a thought" and "it is out of my mouth."

What your answers actually tell me

You said three things that, put together, explain almost all of it: you process slower than the room's pace, you assume your point isn't worth adding, and you fear being judged for how it comes out. Those are not three problems. They are one loop:

The loop you are stuck in

Thought arrives → you check it for correctness → you check the wording → you check whether it's worth saying → window closes → you conclude "I had nothing to say" → next time you check even harder.

The dangerous part is the second-last step. You are collecting false evidence that you have nothing to contribute, when what actually happened is that you had something and it expired in the queue. Nine of your ten "I went blank" moments are really "I went slow."

Your genuine strengths — do not train these away

  • You are a quality-over-quantity thinker. When you do speak, people are more likely to listen than the person who talks constantly. This is a lead-engineer trait. Keep it.
  • You listen properly. Most people don't. This is the raw material for the single best conversational skill there is: the specific follow-up question (Section 09).
  • You're fluent. Vocabulary is not your bottleneck — which means fixes will land fast. You don't need six months of word lists.
  • You have daily site and office contact. Unlimited free practice reps. Most people learning this have to manufacture them.
  • You are technical. Frameworks, structures and repeatable procedures suit how you already think. This whole system leans on that.

The one sentence to remember

Speed beats polish. A B-grade sentence said at the right moment beats an A-grade sentence said after the topic changed. For the next 30 days you are optimising for timing, not quality. Your quality is already fine.

02Thought frameworks — how to organise an idea in 2 seconds

You don't ramble because you think badly. You ramble because you start talking before you've chosen a shape. A framework is a pre-built shape. Choose the shape first, and the words follow — the same way you'd never start drawing a schematic without knowing whether it's a single-line or a wiring diagram.

The seven shapes

Learn two properly this month (PRE and SAR). Add the rest later. Memorising all seven at once is exactly the kind of thing that makes you use none of them.

ShapeStructureUse whenMemory hook
PREPoint → Reason → ExampleSomeone asks your opinion. The default for meetings."Pre" = say it before you explain it.
SARSituation → Action → Result"What did you work on?" Updates, interviews, achievements.SAR = search-and-rescue: what happened, what you did, how it ended.
WSNWhat → So what → Now whatReporting a fault or a finding. Stops you dumping raw data.Three "what"s, escalating.

Each one, applied to your life

PRE — Point, Reason, Example

Rambling"Yeah so I looked at the switchboard and there's a few things, the terminals were a bit tight and I think maybe the CT wiring, um, could be an issue possibly, so I don't know, maybe we should have a look at it."
PRE"I'd hold off energising until Thursday. [Point] The CT wiring doesn't match the schedule. [Reason] Cores 3 and 4 are swapped at the terminal block, so the protection relay would read reverse power. [Example]"
WhySame information, opposite effect. The rambling version makes the listener do the work of finding your point. PRE hands them the conclusion first, then earns it. In a meeting the first eight words decide whether people listen to the rest.

SAR — Situation, Action, Result

Flat"Yeah, been busy. Just the usual, the substation job mostly."
SAR"We had the RCDs tripping every time the compressor kicked in. [S] I split the circuit and put the compressor on its own feed. [A] Hasn't tripped since — three weeks now. [R]"
Why"Been busy" is a door closing. SAR is a door opening — the listener now has three places to ask a follow-up question. This is also exactly the shape interviewers want, so every time you use it socially you're rehearsing for the lead role you said you want.

WSN — What, So what, Now what

Data dump"The megger reading on core 2 came back at 1.8 megohm and core 3 was 2.1 and core 1 was fine, about 400."
WSN"Two cores are reading under 2 megohm. [What] That's below spec — it means moisture has got into the gland. [So what] I'd like to pull the gland and re-terminate before we hand over. Half a day. [Now what]"
WhyYour manager cannot convert megohms into a decision. WSN does the conversion for them. This is the shape that gets you labelled "someone who thinks like a PM, not just a tech."

PPF — Past, Present, Future (your small-talk workhorse)

Dead end"Where am I from? Originally overseas, but I've been here a while now."
PPF"I grew up overseas and did my engineering there. [Past] I've been in Melbourne about four years now, mostly doing substation and switchboard work. [Present] Long-term I'm trying to move more into the project management side of it. [Future] How about you — have you always been in this area?"
WhyPPF is your emergency shape because it works on literally any topic. Work, hobbies, the city, a project, a car. If your mind goes blank, ask yourself "what was it, what is it, what will it be" and you have thirty seconds of speech immediately.

PAR — Problem, Analysis, Recommendation

Dumping"The switchgear delivery's been pushed to the 20th. What do you want to do?"
PAR"Switchgear's slipped to the 20th. [P] That pushes commissioning past the shutdown window, so we'd lose the outage. [A] Two options: hold the outage and pay for expedited freight, or move the outage to the following month. I'd go with expedited — the second outage will cost more in downtime. [R]"
WhyThe first version hands your manager a problem. The second hands them a decision. That difference — over about six months — is what actually gets you promoted. Bring a recommendation even when you're only 70% sure; say "my recommendation would be" and let them adjust it.

OOQ — Observation, Opinion, Question

Silence[You are standing next to someone at the coffee machine and say nothing.]
OOQ"They've finally fixed this machine. [Observation] Honestly I'd almost given up on it — I was buying coffee downstairs for a month. [Opinion] Do you actually drink this stuff or do you go out for it? [Question]"
WhyThis is the whole of small talk in three moves, and none of them require material. An observation is free — the room supplies it. The opinion is what makes you a person rather than a weather report. The question hands over cleanly.

AEF — Answer, Example, Follow-up

One word"Do you like it here?" — "Yeah, it's good."
AEF"Yeah, I do — more than I expected. [A] The thing that surprised me was how much people just ask you directly instead of going through your boss. Took me a while to get used to. [E] Was it a big adjustment for you coming in? [F]"
WhyNotice the answer isn't longer because of fancier words. It's longer because it contains one specific detail and one honest reaction. That's the whole trick, and it's Section 08's entire system.

The emergency framework — when your mind is completely blank

S.O.S. — Stall, Observe, Score
  1. STALL out loud (2–4 sec). "Good question — let me think about that for a second." Now the silence is authorised. Nobody is judging you; they're waiting for you.
  2. OBSERVE. Reach for the nearest true fact: something in the room, something you actually saw this week, or the last thing the person said. You are not inventing — you are reporting.
  3. SCORE it — say one honest reaction. "Which surprised me." "Which I thought was a bit much." "Which is fine by me." That's your opinion, and one clause is enough.

Example, cold: someone asks "So what do you think of the new site rules?" and you have no opinion at all.

You, using S.O.S."Hmm — haven't really formed a view yet. [Stall] The only bit I've noticed is the induction now takes an extra forty minutes. [Observe] Which I don't love on a day when you're only there for one job. [Score] Has it slowed you down much?"

You had nothing, and you produced three sentences and a question. Nobody can tell the difference between that and a prepared answer.

How to actually remember these

Do not try to recall seven acronyms mid-conversation — that's more processing load, not less. Instead: put PRE and PPF on a sticky note on your monitor for two weeks. Use PRE every single time someone asks your opinion at work, and PPF every single time someone asks a personal question. Two shapes, two triggers. Once they're automatic (about three weeks), add SAR and PAR.

03Thinking on the spot — the freeze fix

The freeze isn't a thinking failure. It's a silence failure. Your brain is working fine; the problem is that while it works, your mouth produces nothing, the silence gets loud, panic adds load, and the thought you had gets dropped. Fix the silence and the thinking sorts itself out.

The three-step system: STALL → FRAME → FILL

Step 1 · buy 3 seconds
Stall out loud

Any spoken stall converts dead air into "he's considering it." Never stall silently.

Step 2 · buy 3 more
Announce a frame

Say how you'll answer before you answer. This buys time and makes you sound structured.

Step 3 · speak
Fill the frame

Now you're just completing a form you designed. Much easier than generating from nothing.

Step 1 — your stall bank (pick 4, use them for life)

  • Neutral · safest"Good question — let me think about that for a sec."
  • Neutral"Hmm. Let me have a proper think about that."
  • Engaged"Yeah, that's actually come up a couple of times."
  • Buying more time"Depends a bit — can I check one thing first? Are you asking about the design side or the install side?"
  • Honest and totally acceptable"I haven't thought about that one. Give me a second."
  • When you truly need to defer"I don't want to guess on that. Let me check and come back to you before end of day."
Read this twice

Clarifying is the strongest stall there is, because it is genuinely useful. "Are you asking about cost or about timeline?" buys you six seconds and makes you look precise. Engineers get away with this constantly. Use it.

Step 2 — your frame bank

  • Two-sided"I'd look at it from two angles — the technical side and the programme side."
  • Ranked"There's probably three things, but the main one is…"
  • Hedged but structured"My initial thought is X, though I'd want to check Y before I commit to it."
  • Experience-led"Based on what happened on the last job, I'd say…"
  • Position + caveat"Short answer, yes. Longer answer, it depends on…"
  • Reframe"I think the real question underneath that is whether…"

Why frames work: "There's three things, but the main one is..." commits you to one point, not three. If you only have one, you say it and stop. Nobody counts. But announcing the frame made your brain produce the structure, and it bought you two seconds of talking time in which the answer actually assembled.

Step 3 — fill it with PRE, PAR or WSN (Section 02)

The drill — 5 minutes, alone, daily

"Random question, 45 seconds" drill
  1. Open your phone timer. Pick a random question from the list below (or open a news app and react to the first headline).
  2. Speak out loud — not in your head. This is the entire point. Your mouth is the muscle that's untrained, not your brain.
  3. Force yourself through all three steps: stall, frame, fill. Even alone. Especially alone.
  4. Talk for 45 seconds. If you dry up at 20, keep the silence and then continue — do not stop the timer. Learning to survive your own pause is half the skill.
  5. Record it on your phone once a week and listen back. You will discover you sound far more competent than you feel.

04Confidence without fake confidence

You do not need to sound certain. You need to sound settled. There is a large, learnable difference between "I don't know" said as an apology and "I don't know yet" said as a status update — and it's carried almost entirely by wording, not personality.

Confidence vs certainty vs arrogance

What it sounds likeEffect on the room
Certainty"It's definitely the CT."Fine when true. Career-damaging when wrong. You don't need it.
Confidence ✓ target"I haven't confirmed it, but everything points to the CT. I'll know by Thursday."Trusted. You own the uncertainty instead of leaking it.
Arrogance"Obviously it's the CT. I said that last week."People stop bringing you problems.
Under-claiming ✗ your habit"I don't know, maybe this is wrong, but I think possibly it might be the CT?"Your correct answer gets discounted. Someone else says it later and gets the credit.

Hedge surgery — keep the honesty, drop the apology

You are not being asked to over-claim. You are being asked to state uncertainty once, cleanly, instead of five times, weakly.

You currently saySay insteadWhat changed
"I don't know, maybe this is wrong, but…""I haven't confirmed it yet, but my current understanding is…""Not confirmed yet" is a process statement. "Maybe I'm wrong" is a character statement.
"Sorry, just a quick question…""Quick question —"Delete the apology. You are allowed to ask questions.
"This might be a stupid idea but…""One option we haven't looked at is…"Never pre-insult your own contribution. The room takes your framing at face value.
"I think maybe we could possibly try…""I'd suggest we try…"Three hedges collapsed into one clean modal.
"I'm not an expert on this but…""From the electrical side, what I'd say is…"Replaces a disclaimer with a scope. Same humility, none of the damage.
"Does that make sense?" (after every point)"Happy to go deeper on any part of that."Stops asking permission to have been understood.
"Sorry to chase…""Just following up on…"Following up is your job, not an imposition.
"I could be completely wrong here…""Push back on this if I've missed something —"Same openness, but framed as inviting scrutiny rather than expecting failure.
The one-hedge rule

Allow yourself one hedge per statement. Say it at the front, then commit. "I'm not certain yet, but the readings point to a moisture ingress and I'd re-terminate before handover." One hedge, then a clear position. Two or more hedges and the listener stops hearing content and starts hearing anxiety.

How to sound settled (delivery, not words)

  • End sentences downward. Rising intonation at the end of a statement turns it into a request for approval. Record yourself — this is the most common single fix for ESL professionals and it changes perception more than vocabulary ever will.
  • Land the last three words. Don't fade out. Most people's volume drops at the end of a sentence, which reads as retreat. Say the final words at the same volume you started.
  • One breath before you start. Not a nervous habit — a deliberate one. It slows your pace by about 15% and it costs nothing.
  • Stop talking when you've finished. The urge to add "…yeah, so, anyway" is the urge to check you were received. Resist it. Silence after your point is powerful, not awkward.

Recovering from an awkward moment

You will occasionally say something that lands wrong. The recovery matters more than the mistake — and the recovery is short.

05The “I have nothing to talk about” fix

You have plenty to talk about. What you don't have is a retrieval system. Interesting people are not living more interesting lives than you — they are noticing, filing and reusing ordinary events that you discard within an hour of them happening.

The actual mechanism

Watch what a naturally talkative colleague does. They don't invent stories. They take a five-second event — a courier delivered the wrong pallet — and they've told it four times this week, better each time. The event was ordinary. The reuse is what made them seem full of material.

The rule that changes everything

A story is not an unusual event. A story is an ordinary event with a specific detail, a small surprise, and an honest reaction attached. That's it. Those three ingredients are all that separate "my weekend was good" from something people lean in for.

The same weekend, four ways

Dead"Yeah, it was good. Pretty quiet."
+ Detail"Pretty quiet — spent most of Saturday trying to fix the garage light."
+ Surprise"Pretty quiet — spent Saturday fixing the garage light. Turned out the previous owner had wired it off the smoke alarm circuit."
+ Reaction + hand-off"Pretty quiet — but I lost half of Saturday to the garage light. Turned out the previous owner had it running off the smoke alarm circuit. I'm an electrician by trade and I still sat there for twenty minutes going 'why is this live.' What did you get up to?"
WhyNothing was added except one specific fact (smoke alarm circuit), one honest reaction (I'm an electrician and I was still confused), and one hand-off. No vocabulary was upgraded. This is the entire skill.

Where material actually comes from

Not from doing more. From noticing more. These are the fifteen places an ordinary week hides tellable material — scan this list on Sunday and you'll find three every time.

Something went wrong

The mistake, the wrong delivery, the drawing that didn't match site. Highest-value category. People love hearing about problems that got solved.

Someone said something odd

A client's strange request, a supplier's excuse, an apprentice's question. Quoting people directly is the single fastest way to make a story alive.

You were wrong about something

You assumed X, it was Y. Self-deprecating, safe, universally likeable. Costs you nothing socially.

A small win

Something you fixed, figured out or got away with. Keep it short and don't oversell — the understatement is what makes it land in Australia.

Something you learned

A fact from a video, a podcast, a datasheet. "Apparently…" is a legitimate opener with zero personal risk.

A comparison

Here vs where you grew up. Old job vs new job. This site vs the last one. Comparison is instant structure and instantly interesting.

Food

A meal that was surprisingly good or bad, a place you'd recommend. Universally safe, universally reciprocated.

A near miss

Almost late, almost got it wrong, almost missed the flight. Tension without consequence.

Something that annoyed you

Traffic, a process, a form. Keep it light and short — complaint is bonding when it's 20 seconds, draining at 2 minutes.

A recommendation

A tool, a show, a place, a tradie. Ends with value for the listener, which makes you memorable.

A plan

Something you're considering buying, booking or building. Invites opinions, which invites conversation.

An observation about people

"I've noticed everyone here does X." Low risk, high engagement, and you're strong at this — you're a good observer.

A coincidence

Ran into someone, two things happened at once. Cheap and effective.

A skill you're bad at

Cooking, dancing, parking. Instantly warm, instantly relatable.

Something from your past

First job, apprenticeship, moving country. You have far more of this than a local colleague and it's genuinely interesting to them.

The 90-second capture habit

This is the one habit that does the heavy lifting. Without it, everything else in this section is theory.

Setup — do this once, today, takes 3 minutes
  1. Create a note in your phone called STORY BANK. Pin it to the top.
  2. Add it to your home screen as a widget or shortcut so it's one tap.
  3. Set one daily alarm at a dead time you already have — the drive home, 5:30pm, whenever. Label it "anything worth telling?"
The capture format — never more than 3 lines

— WHAT: courier dropped 40 boxes of the wrong terminals
— TWIST: right part number, wrong voltage rating, nobody caught it for 2 days
— MY LINE: "we'd already installed nine of them"

The "MY LINE" field is the important one. It's the one sentence that carries the surprise. When you retell the story later, you build toward that line and stop shortly after it. Most people's stories die because they have no idea where the end is — you'll always know.

Rules for the capture habit

  • Never write full sentences. Fragments only. It has to take under 90 seconds or you'll stop doing it.
  • Capture boring things too. You are a bad judge of what's interesting in the moment. Your filter is the whole problem. Capture first, filter never.
  • Aim for 4 a week, not 4 a day. That's 200 a year. You need about 25 good ones to never run dry.
  • Re-read the bank on Sunday for 2 minutes. This is what loads them into fast memory. Capture without re-reading gives you an archive, not a bank.

Your starter shelf — build this in week 2

Ten slots. Fill them with your own material. These ten cover about 80% of every conversation you'll have this year.

06Your story inventory — 60 memory triggers

Do not read this in one sitting. Take one category per day for three weeks, spend four minutes on it, and write whatever surfaces into your Story Bank as three-line fragments. Most prompts will produce nothing. About one in three will produce something you'd completely forgotten.

Read the prompt, then wait. The first thing that surfaces is usually the right one — don't reject it for being small. 07How to tell a story without sounding rehearsed

The reason rehearsed stories sound rehearsed is that people memorise words. Memorise the beats instead. Same story, different words every time, and it stays alive.

The spine: six beats

#BeatJobExample wording
1HookBuy permission for the next 30 seconds. One sentence, and it must promise something."We nearly energised a board with the CTs wired backwards."
2ContextOnly what's needed to understand the problem. This is where 90% of stories die."This was a hospital job, tight shutdown, we had one weekend."
3The problemWhat was at stake or what was strange."Everything checked out on paper, but the meter was reading negative."
4TurnThe moment it changed. Slow down here — this is the part they came for."And then the sparkie says, dead casual, 'oh, those two cores were labelled wrong from the factory.'"
5OutcomeHow it ended. Short."Two hours to re-terminate. We made the window with about forty minutes spare."
6LandingThe line that tells people you're done. Then stop."Now I check factory labelling on everything. Costs me an hour. Saved me a weekend."
Your specific risk: beat 2

Engineers over-build context. You'll want to explain the CT ratio, the relay model, why the shutdown was scheduled that weekend. Cut all of it. Rule: give context only for things the listener needs to feel the problem. If they need more, they'll ask — and them asking is a good outcome, not a failure.

Length formats — pick before you start talking

Format A · under 30 sec
The three-beater

Hook → Turn → Landing. Skip context entirely. Use in the kitchen, in a lift, mid-meeting.

"Courier delivered 40 boxes of the wrong terminals. Right part number, wrong voltage rating — and we'd already installed nine. Now I check ratings, not part numbers."

Format B · about 1 min
The standard

All six beats, one sentence each. Your default. Use at lunch, at the pub, in a one-to-one.

If you're unsure which format to use, use this one. It is almost never wrong.

Format C · 2–3 min
The full version

Six beats + dialogue + one detour. Only when you've been explicitly invited: "tell them about the hospital job."

Never open with this format uninvited. Earn it with a Format A first.

Ten stories: bad version → better version

01 · WorkThe fault you couldn't find
Bad"We had a fault on site and it took ages to find. It was pretty frustrating. Eventually we sorted it out."
Better"We spent two days chasing an earth fault that didn't exist. Every test came back clean, and the breaker kept tripping anyway. Turned out a possum had got into the cable pit and was leaning on a terminal. Two days of megger testing, and the answer was a possum."
WhyThe bad version has no image and no turn. The better version has a specific number (two days), a contradiction (tests clean, still tripping), and a landing that repeats the absurd word. Repeating the key word in the last line is a reliable, easy landing technique.
02 · MistakeSomething you got wrong
Bad"I made a mistake once on a job with some cable sizing. It was a bit embarrassing but it got fixed."
Better"Early on I sized a submain off the wrong table — used the free-air rating for a cable that was going in a conduit. My supervisor found it two days before install. He didn't even tell me off, he just said 'go and stand next to that conduit for a minute and tell me if it's free air.' I've never made that mistake again."
WhyDialogue. One quoted line does more work than three sentences of description. Also notice the mistake is specific enough to be credible and small enough to be safe — that's the right calibration for a work story.
03 · WeekendThe most-asked question of your life
Bad"Not much really. Just relaxed, did some stuff around the house."
Better"Tried to be productive and failed. I got as far as buying the paint, then spent four hours watching videos about how to paint properly. The tin's still in the hallway. What about you?"
WhySelf-deprecation costs nothing and buys warmth. Note the structure: intention → failure → evidence (the tin in the hallway). The physical detail is what makes it funny rather than just an admission.
04 · ClientThe strange request
Bad"Clients ask for weird stuff sometimes. This one client wanted something unusual."
Better"Had a client ask if we could make the switchboard quieter. I said the switchboard doesn't make noise. He said 'no, the buzzing.' Took us a week to work out he could hear the fluorescent ballast in the room next door. We swapped four tubes and he thought we were geniuses."
WhyBack-and-forth dialogue creates rhythm. The landing is a reversal — huge effort, trivial fix, undeserved credit. Reversal is the most reliable structure for a funny work story.
05 · TravelWhere "it was lovely" goes to die
Bad"We went to Tassie last year. It was really nice, beautiful scenery."
Better"We went to Tassie and I badly misjudged the weather. Packed for what I thought was Australia. Ended up buying a beanie in Cradle Mountain from a shop that clearly makes its entire living off people like me — they had a wall of beanies, nothing else."
Why"Beautiful scenery" is a review, not a story. A travel story needs friction. The wall of beanies is the specific detail; the implication that you're one of many idiots is the joke you never have to explain.
06 · Origin"So where are you from?"
Bad"I'm from overseas. I came here for work about four years ago. It's quite different."
Better"I moved here four years ago for work. The thing that got me in the first week wasn't the weather or the accent — it was that people call the boss by his first name. Where I trained, you'd address the chief engineer as 'sir' and wait to be spoken to. Took me about six months to stop doing that."
WhyContrast is the engine of every good origin story. One before, one after, one specific behaviour. Not a summary of two countries — one concrete habit that changed.
07 · LearningSomething you found out
Bad"I read something interesting about power grids the other day."
Better"Apparently the grid frequency is what old clock radios used to keep time — they counted the mains cycles. So when the grid ran slightly slow, every clock radio in the country ran slow with it. I'd never thought about the fact that the whole country's clocks were essentially one shared instrument."
Why"Apparently…" is a perfect low-risk opener. The story works because it ends on a reframe, not a fact. Facts are forgettable; a new way of seeing something is what people repeat to other people.
08 · AwkwardA moment you'd rather forget
Bad"I had an embarrassing moment at work once. It was pretty awkward."
Better"I spent a whole site meeting calling the client's project manager 'Dave.' His name was Dan. Nobody corrected me. At the end he shook my hand and said 'good to meet you — it's Dan, by the way.' Very calm about it. I would not have been."
WhyAwkward stories need a generous other person or they're just uncomfortable. His calm is what makes it funny instead of painful. The short final sentence — "I would not have been" — is the landing, and short landings land hardest.
09 · OpinionHaving a view without an argument
Bad"I think safety inductions are a bit much these days."
Better"I've become a bit of a sceptic about inductions. I did one recently that was ninety minutes, and about eighty of those were legal disclaimers. The ten minutes about where the actual isolation points were — that was the useful bit. I'd happily do a two-hour induction if it was two hours of that."
WhyThe bad version is a complaint that invites disagreement. The better version splits the opinion — against the bad version of the thing, for the good version. You get to have a view without picking a fight, which suits how you actually think.
10 · WinTalking about yourself without bragging
Bad"Yeah, I did a really good job on that project, it went really well."
Better"That one nearly got away from us. We were three weeks behind and the client wanted a date. I broke the commissioning into two stages so they could have the ground floor early — bought us the three weeks back. Only worked because the site super was willing to reprogramme around it, honestly."
WhyIn an Australian workplace this is the correct register for a win: lead with the difficulty, state your action plainly, share the credit at the end. You come across as capable and easy to work with. The bad version reads as insecure, not confident.

How to stop talking (your over-explaining brake)

Three hard stops
  1. Say your landing line, then close your mouth. Do not add "so yeah, anyway." That phrase is the sound of a story with no ending, and it undoes the whole thing.
  2. Watch for the nod-and-glance. If they nod while looking away, you're 15 seconds past the end. Land it in your next sentence: "— anyway, it worked out."
  3. Hand back within 10 seconds of finishing. "Have you had one like that?" turns your monologue into a conversation and removes any risk of having hogged the floor.

The under-telling insurance policy: if you're worried about talking too long, deliberately stop early — before you've explained everything. If they want more, they'll ask, and being asked is the best possible outcome. A story that ends too soon reads as confident; a story that ends too late reads as nervous.

08The conversation continuation system

One formula runs this entire section. Learn it once and every question anyone ever asks you becomes answerable.

The formula

ANSWER → DETAIL → REACTION → RETURN

Answer the actual question. Add one specific fact. Give one honest reaction to it. Hand the ball back. Four beats, about fifteen seconds, works on everything.

The four levels below show the same answer at increasing strength. Level 3 is your target. Level 4 is for when you want the conversation to continue — which is most of the time, but not always, and knowing when to stop at Level 3 is its own skill.

09Asking better questions — stop interviewing people

This is your cheapest win. You are already a good listener, and a good listener who asks one specific follow-up is experienced as a great conversationalist — while doing about 30% of the talking. This section suits you more than almost anything else in the playbook.

The problem: the interview pattern

Interview"What did you do on the weekend?" — "Went to the footy." — "Oh nice. Any plans for next weekend?"
Why it failsYou asked, they answered, you discarded their answer and asked a new question. It feels like a form being filled in. The other person has to keep generating content while you keep changing the subject.
Q → R → Q"What did you do on the weekend?" — "Went to the footy." — "Oh, at the G? I've only been once and I couldn't believe how loud it got. Are you a member or was it a one-off?"
Q → R → Q

Question → React → Question. The React is the whole thing. It is one sentence proving you heard them — a detail, a reaction, or a small piece of yourself. Without it you're interviewing. With it you're talking.

The reciprocity rule

Never ask a question you wouldn't answer yourself, and be ready to answer it first. If you ask "what's the worst job you've been on?", have your own answer loaded. Asking without offering is interrogation; asking with offering is conversation.

13Meetings — your #1 priority

You said you want the lead/PM track. Meetings are where that decision gets made, and they're where you're currently most invisible. This section is your highest-leverage 30 days.

The reframe that fixes most of it

You are waiting for a point good enough to justify interrupting. That threshold is far higher than anyone else in the room is using. Most contributions in most meetings are clarifications, confirmations and small additions — not insights. Lower your bar to match the room's.

The Meeting Participation Ladder

Do not try to be insightful. Climb one rung at a time. Each rung is a legitimate contribution on its own — and each is easier than it looks from below.

RungMoveExact wordingDifficulty
1Ask one question"Sorry, can I just check — is that the 20th of this month or next?"Trivial. Zero risk. Do this in every meeting for week 1.
2Confirm one point"Just so I'm clear — we're going with option B?"Easy. Also genuinely useful; half the room is unsure too.
3Add one observation"Worth noting the switchgear's still not confirmed."Moderate. A fact, not an opinion — nothing to defend.
4Give one opinion"I'd go with B. It takes the long-lead item off the critical path."Harder. Use PRE (Section 02).
5Summarise a decision"So to close that off — we're on option B, I'm chasing the date, and we review Thursday. That right?"Hardest, and the highest status. Whoever summarises the meeting is read as leading it.
Your 30-day meeting protocol
  • Week 1: Rung 1 in every meeting. One question. Quality irrelevant.
  • Week 2: Rung 1 + Rung 2. Question and a confirmation.
  • Week 3: Add Rung 3. One observation per meeting.
  • Week 4: Add Rung 4 in your safest meeting only — the small internal one, not the client one.
  • Rung 5 lands in month two. Don't rush it. Do it first in a meeting you half-own.

The first 90 seconds rule

Speak within the first 90 seconds of any meeting, about anything. "Morning, everyone." "Is Dave joining?" "Can everyone hear me?" The cost of speaking rises sharply the longer you've been silent — after twenty minutes of silence, your first contribution feels like an announcement. After ninety seconds, it's just talking.

14Explaining things clearly

The instinct that makes you a good engineer — build up from first principles, in order — is exactly the wrong order for explaining. Non-experts need the conclusion first and the mechanism only if they ask.

B-O-W-R — the technical explanation system

Big picture → Occurrence → Why it matters → Recommendation
  1. Big picture — one sentence a non-engineer could repeat to their boss.
  2. Occurrence — what actually happened, in plain words.
  3. Why it matters — translate into their currency: cost, time, safety, disruption.
  4. Recommendation — what you'd do, with a time and a cost.
ScenarioExplaining a CT ratio error to a client's project manager
Engineer-brain"So the CT on the incomer is a 400:5, and the meter's been configured for a 800:5, which means the secondary current the meter's seeing is half what it should be for a given primary, so the scaling factor in the meter config is out by a factor of two, which is why the kWh totals are…"

Half a day, no outage. I'd do it this week and get ahead of the retailer. [Recommendation]"
WhyNot one technical fact was lost. They were re-ordered and translated. The client can now make a decision, brief their own manager, and — crucially — they trust you more, not less, for having made it simple. Simplifying is not dumbing down; it's the hardest version of the skill.

Daily-life version

Same problem, lower stakes: explaining something to a friend, family member, or someone at a party.

Headline → Detail → Check

Golden rule for social explanations: stop after 20 seconds and check. If they ask a question, continue. If they say "oh cool," you're done — move on and ask them something. Most people over-explain in social settings because they're nervous, not because the topic is complicated.

16Australian workplace communication

The Australian workplace register is direct in content, casual in tone, understated in claim. Most miscalibration by newcomers goes in one direction: too formal, too deferential, too much hedging. You do not need slang. You need to loosen the register and tighten the claims.

The four calibrations

01
Formality: dial it down

First names for everyone, including senior people, unless told otherwise. "Hi Mark" not "Dear Mr Thompson." Emails are short. "Cheers" is a normal sign-off between colleagues.

02
Directness: dial it up

Say the actual thing. "I don't think that'll work" is normal and not rude. Vagueness reads as evasive here, not polite. On site especially, indirect requests get ignored — not out of disrespect, but because they don't register as requests.

03
Claims: dial them down

Understatement is the local prestige register. "Yeah, it went alright" about a job that went brilliantly is higher status than "it was a great success." Over-claiming reads as insecure.

04
Warmth: keep it constant

Directness without warmth reads as aggressive. The pattern is: blunt content, friendly delivery. "Nah, that won't work, mate — here's why" is the whole formula.

Direct vs rude — where the line actually is

"I need that by Friday.""Why isn't this done yet?"Forward-looking ask, not backward-looking blame.

"Nah, I can't take that on.""Not my problem."Decline the task, don't disown the team.
"This needs re-doing.""Who did this rubbish?"Name the work, not the worker — especially in front of others.

Workplace humour

Australian workplace humour is heavily self-deprecating and understated. You don't need to be funny — you need to be un-precious, and that's a much lower bar.

  • Safe and effective: jokes about yourself, the weather, traffic, the coffee machine, how long a meeting ran, your own mistakes.
  • Understatement is the main tool. A disaster is "not ideal." A great result is "not bad." Learning this one register shift will do more than any joke.
  • Teasing is affection here — being gently mocked by colleagues generally means you've been accepted. The correct response is to take it well and give a little back, not to be hurt or over-apologise.
  • Avoid: sarcasm about someone's competence, anything about appearance, jokes that need someone to be the target, and humour in your first month with a client.
  • You don't have to join in. Laughing genuinely and saying "yeah, fair" is full participation. Nobody requires you to be the funny one.

19Filler words

Do not try to eliminate fillers. Everyone uses them, including excellent speakers, and hunting them adds exactly the kind of self-monitoring load that made you slow in the first place. The goal is to halve them and replace the rest with silence.

What fillers are actually doing

A filler is a floor-holding device. You use it to signal "I'm not finished, don't take the turn." That's a legitimate function — which is why deleting fillers without replacing the function makes people talk over you. The replacement is a held pause: stop making sound, but keep your hand, your gaze and your posture mid-sentence. It holds the floor just as well and sounds considered rather than hesitant.

The three stages

Stage 1 · week 1
Notice only

Do not try to stop. Just count. Record 60 seconds daily and tally your fillers. Awareness alone typically cuts them 20–30% with no effort.

Stage 2 · weeks 2–3
Replace with silence

Target one filler — your most frequent. When you feel it coming, close your mouth instead. The pause feels like four seconds to you and reads as half a second to everyone else.

Stage 3 · week 4+
Replace with structure

Swap the filler for a phrase that does real work: "The main point is…", "Two things…", "In short…". Now the pause is doing a job.

Longer than a role-play — a full conversation, ten to fifteen exchanges, played straight. Say "Run a simulation" in chat and pick a setting.

The rules I'll follow

#Rule
1I open in character with a realistic first line and stay in character throughout.
2I react like a real person — sometimes interested, sometimes distracted, sometimes brief.
3I won't make it easy. Some of my replies will be short and give you nothing to work with. That's the training.
4I'll occasionally misunderstand you, or ask a follow-up you didn't prepare for.
5If your answer is too short, I'll break character briefly and show you the Level 3 version, then we continue.
6If you over-explain, I'll show you where you should have stopped.
7If wording is unnatural — grammatically fine but not how people say it here — I'll flag it. I won't correct small grammar that doesn't affect meaning.
8I'll track recurring patterns across sessions and name them in the scorecard.
9At the end: scorecard, then one improvement challenge. Never a list.

One skill a day, 10–30 minutes, progressive. Every day has a drill (alone, safe) and a real-world challenge (live, small). Tick them off — your progress ring in the sidebar tracks it.

Three rules that decide whether this works
  1. The 3-minute minimum. On a bad day, do only the challenge and skip the drill. A 3-minute day keeps the chain alive; a skipped day breaks it. Never skip two.

  3. Drills are out loud. Reading them silently does nothing. Your mouth is the muscle being trained.

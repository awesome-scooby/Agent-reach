1.1 What electricity actually is

In plain words

Everything around you is made of tiny bits called atoms. Atoms have even tinier bits inside them called electrons, and in metals like copper, some of those electrons are loose — they can wander about. Electricity is just those loose electrons all being pushed in the same direction at the same time. That's it. A wire is a pipe full of loose electrons, and a power station is a giant pump that pushes them.

When electrons get pushed through something, they bump into it and make it warm, or spin it, or light it up. That's how we get heat, motion and light out of a wire.

Copper is used for wiring because it has a lot of loose electrons and lets them move easily. Plastic has almost none that can move, which is why we wrap copper in it — the plastic stops the electrons escaping sideways into you.

You will never need to think about electrons again after this page. Engineers work one level up, with three quantities that describe what the electrons are doing: how hard they're being pushed, how many are flowing, and how much the material is fighting them. Those three are voltage, current and resistance.

Memory trick

Voltage pushes. Current flows. Resistance fights. Say it three times. Nearly every fundamentals question is a rearrangement of those six words.

1.2 Voltage, current and resistance

In plain words

Voltage is how hard the electricity is being pushed. Like water pressure. Measured in volts (V). A torch battery pushes with 1.5 V. An Australian power point pushes with 230 V. The wires in the street push with 22,000 V.

Current is how much electricity is actually flowing. Like litres per second in a pipe. Measured in amps (A). A phone charger draws about 1 amp. A kettle draws about 10. A big data centre switchboard carries 4,000.

Resistance is how much the thing fights the flow. Like a kink in a hose. Measured in ohms, written with the symbol Ω. A thick copper bar has almost none. A heater element has a lot — which is exactly why it gets hot.

These three are tied together by one equation, and it is the single most useful thing in electrical engineering. It's called Ohm's law, after Georg Ohm, who worked it out in 1827.

V = I × R
V = voltage in volts  ·  I = current in amps  ·  R = resistance in ohms (Ω)

Why is current called "I"? It's from the French intensité du courant — intensity of current. There's no logic to remember, it's just historical. Everyone uses I.

Because it's a simple multiplication, you can rearrange it three ways depending on what you're missing:

Worked example 1.1 — a heater element
A 230 V heater element has a resistance of 23 Ω.
Find the current it draws.
1
We want current, so cover I on the triangle. That leaves V over R. I = V ÷ R
2
Put the numbers in. I = 230 ÷ 23 = 10 A
3
Sanity check: a 10 A load on a 230 V socket is about a kettle. That's believable for a heater, so we haven't slipped a decimal point.
Answer10 amps. Which is why it needs a 16 A circuit breaker and not a 10 A one — a breaker sized exactly at the load current will nuisance-trip.

Why resistance makes heat

When electrons are forced through a resistance, they collide with the material and give up energy as heat. This is not a side effect you can design away — it is a law. It is why:

  • A heater element is deliberately made with high resistance.
  • A cable that is too thin gets hot and eventually catches fire — a thin cable has more resistance than a thick one.
  • A loose bolted connection on a busbar is dangerous. A loose joint has resistance where there should be none, so it heats up, which makes it looser, which makes it hotter. This is called thermal runaway, and it is the number-one cause of switchboard fires.
Key fact — one you can use at work tomorrow

Heat produced = I² × R. Notice the current is squared. Double the current and you get four times the heat, not twice. This is the reason temperature rise testing exists in AS/NZS 61439, and the reason a switchboard that's fine at 2,000 A can cook itself at 3,000 A.

Interview question

"Why does a loose termination in a switchboard cause a fire?"

Good answer: A loose joint introduces resistance in a place designed to have almost none. Current through that resistance produces heat at I²R. The heat oxidises and degrades the joint, raising the resistance further, producing more heat. It's a runaway cycle. That's why torque settings on terminations are specified, why we thermographically scan boards, and why re-torque checks appear on the ITP.

1.3 Power — how much work is being done

In plain words

Voltage and current on their own don't tell you how much work is happening. A tiny trickle at huge pressure does very little. A huge flow at almost no pressure also does very little. You need both together.

Power is pressure multiplied by flow — how fast energy is actually being delivered. It's measured in watts (W). A thousand watts is a kilowatt (kW). A million watts is a megawatt (MW).

A phone charger is about 20 watts. A kettle is 2,000 watts (2 kW). A house at its busiest might be 10 kW. One rack of servers is 10–20 kW. A big Melbourne data centre is 80,000,000 watts — 80 MW. Same idea, just bigger numbers.

P = V × I
P = power in watts  ·  V = volts  ·  I = amps
(this is the single-phase version — three-phase gets one extra term, coming in 1.7)

Combine it with Ohm's law and you get two more useful forms. You don't need to memorise these — just know they exist, because sometimes you know the resistance but not the current:

P = I² × R — when you know current and resistance
P = V² ÷ R — when you know voltage and resistance

Power versus energy — the difference that trips people up

Power is a rate: how fast energy is being used, right now. Energy is power multiplied by time: how much was used in total.

  • A 2 kW kettle running for 1 hour uses 2 kilowatt-hours (kWh) of energy.
  • Your electricity bill charges you for kWh — energy. It doesn't care how fast you used it.
  • A generator is rated in kW — power. A fuel tank is sized in kWh — energy. That's the whole logic of sizing a generator's fuel storage.
  • A UPS is rated in kVA — power. Its battery is sized in kWh (or amp-hours) — energy. Same logic again.
Common mistake

Saying "the UPS is 500 kVA so it'll last 10 minutes". A kVA rating tells you how much load it can carry, not for how long. How long is decided entirely by the battery. A 500 kVA UPS might have 5 minutes of battery or 30 — the UPS rating says nothing about it. Power and energy are different questions.

Worked example 1.2 — a rack of servers
A server rack draws 12 kW at 230 V single phase (simplified — real racks are three-phase).
(a) What current does it draw? (b) How much energy in a 30-day month?
1
Rearrange P = V × I to find current. I = P ÷ V
2
Convert kW to W first — always work in base units. 12 kW = 12,000 W I = 12,000 ÷ 230 = 52.2 A
3
For energy, multiply power by hours. Servers run flat out, 24 hours a day. Hours = 30 × 24 = 720 h Energy = 12 kW × 720 h = 8,640 kWh
Answer(a) 52.2 A   (b) 8,640 kWh per month — from one rack. Multiply by 40 racks in a data hall and you can see why data centres negotiate their own electricity contracts.

1.4 AC and DC

In plain words

There are two ways to push electrons down a wire.

DC (direct current) pushes them steadily in one direction, always. Like a tap left running. Batteries do this. Your phone runs on DC.

AC (alternating current) pushes them one way, then the other way, then back again — very fast, 50 times a second in Australia. The electrons barely move at all; they just jiggle back and forth. But the energy still travels down the wire. Everything from the street runs on AC.

It sounds mad that jiggling works. It does, and it won, because AC has one enormous advantage: you can change its voltage very easily with a transformer. DC can't do that nearly as simply.

Why voltage-changing matters so much: to send power a long way you want high voltage and low current, because heat loss is I²R and low current means tiny losses. But high voltage is lethal and needs huge clearances, so you want low voltage at the point of use. AC lets you have both — high for transport, low for use — by stepping it up and down with transformers.

Where you'll meet DC in a data centre

Even though the building runs on AC, DC is everywhere inside it:

  • UPS batteries are DC. The UPS converts incoming AC to DC to charge them, then back to AC to feed the load.
  • Server power supplies convert AC to DC internally. Chips run on about 1 volt DC.
  • Control and protection circuits in switchboards often run on 24 V or 110 V DC, fed from a small battery — so protection still works when the main power is gone. This matters enormously, and it's a classic hidden single point of failure.
  • Solar generates DC and needs an inverter to make AC.
Key fact

An inverter turns DC into AC. A rectifier turns AC into DC. A UPS is basically a rectifier and an inverter with a battery in the middle. Once you see it that way, UPS topology in Part 4 becomes easy.

1.5 Frequency and the sine wave

In plain words

Frequency is how many times per second AC swings back and forth. In Australia it's 50 times a second, written 50 Hz (hertz, named after a physicist). America uses 60 Hz. It's just a choice made a century ago, but everything is built around it.

The shape of the swing is a smooth wave called a sine wave — it comes out that shape naturally because generators work by spinning a magnet in a circle, and going round in a circle traced against time makes exactly that shape.

Why 50 Hz matters practically

  • Motor speed depends on it. A motor's speed is set by frequency. Change the frequency and the motor changes speed — that's exactly how a variable speed drive works.
  • Generators must match it before connecting. Two AC sources can only be joined if they're at the same frequency and lined up in the swing. Connecting them out of step is violent and can destroy the machine. This is called synchronising, and it's why generator paralleling is a specialist job.
  • Frequency tells you if the grid is healthy. If demand exceeds generation, the whole grid slows very slightly — frequency drops below 50. It's the grid's pulse.
  • A UPS must produce a clean 50 Hz sine wave. Cheap ones produce a blocky approximation, which some equipment hates.

RMS — the number on the label

Here's something that confuses everyone. If AC is constantly changing, what does "230 V" even mean? It's changing between about +325 V and −325 V.

The answer is RMS (root mean square). It's a kind of average that answers this question: what steady DC voltage would produce the same heating effect as this wobbling AC voltage? For a sine wave, the answer is the peak divided by 1.414 (which is √2).

VRMS = Vpeak ÷ 1.414
Australian mains: peak ≈ 325 V, RMS = 325 ÷ 1.414 ≈ 230 V
Key fact

Every AC voltage and current you will ever see written down — 230 V, 415 V, 22 kV, a 400 A breaker, a 50 kA fault rating — is an RMS value unless it explicitly says "peak". You'll almost never calculate RMS yourself. But you need to know the word, because one number in Part 2 is deliberately a peak value: Ipk, the peak withstand rating of a switchboard. Knowing why that one is peak and everything else is RMS is a genuine mark of understanding.

1.6 One phase and three phases

In plain words

A phase is one live wire carrying one AC wave. A house power point uses one phase — one live wire and one neutral. That's single phase, 230 V.

Anything big uses three live wires instead of one, each carrying its own wave, and the three waves are deliberately staggered so they don't peak at the same moment. They're spaced evenly around the cycle — a third of a turn apart each, which is 120 degrees. That's three phase.

Why bother? Imagine three people pushing a roundabout. If all three push at the same instant, it lurches — shove, nothing, shove, nothing. If they take turns evenly spaced, it spins smoothly and never stops being pushed. Three-phase power is that: the power delivered is smooth and constant instead of pulsing.

The three real advantages

  1. Smooth power. Constant delivery, not pulsing. Motors run without vibrating themselves apart.
  2. Less copper for the same power. Three-phase delivers roughly twice the power of single-phase for about 1.5 times the conductor. On a data centre busway carrying 4,000 A, that saving is measured in tonnes and hundreds of thousands of dollars.
  3. Motors start by themselves. Three staggered phases create a naturally rotating magnetic field, which drags the motor round. A single-phase motor needs extra components — a capacitor or a start winding — to fake this.

1.7 Star, delta and the √3

In plain words

You've got three live wires. There are only two sensible ways to wire the three coils of a generator, motor or transformer to them.

Star — join one end of all three coils together at a common point, and take the other three ends out as your three wires. The joined-together point is the neutral. Star looks like the letter Y, which is why it's also called wye.

Delta — join the coils end-to-end in a loop, like three people holding hands in a ring, and take a wire from each corner. It makes a triangle. The Greek letter for D is delta, Δ, which is a triangle. Hence the name. There's no neutral in delta — there's no common point to make one from.

That's genuinely all it is: a Y or a triangle.

Where the 415 comes from — the famous √3

In an Australian star system, each phase sits at 230 V relative to neutral. So you'd expect two phases to give 460 V between them, right? They don't. They give 415 V.

The reason is the 120° stagger from FIG 1.6. The two phases never peak at the same moment, so you can't just add them. The geometry works out to a factor of √3, which is 1.732.

230 V × 1.732 = 398 V ≈ 400 V
nominal declared value in Australia is 400/230 V; you will hear 415/240 V used interchangeably
(older nominal figures, same system — don't let it confuse you)
Key fact — memorise this number

√3 = 1.732. It appears in every single three-phase calculation you will ever do. If a number in a three-phase sum looks wrong by roughly 1.7×, you've either forgotten the √3 or used it twice.

The three-phase power formula

Remember P = V × I for single phase. For three phase, you add the √3:

P = √3 × V × I × pf
P = power in watts · V = line-to-line voltage (415 V) · I = line current in amps
pf = power factor, explained in 1.8 — it's a number between 0 and 1
Worked example 1.3 — sizing from a load, the sum you'll do most often
A three-phase load draws 250 A at 415 V with a power factor of 0.9.
How many kW is it?
1
Write the formula out before touching the numbers. Always. P = √3 × V × I × pf
2
Substitute. P = 1.732 × 415 × 250 × 0.9
3
Work left to right. 1.732 × 415 = 718.8 718.8 × 250 = 179,700 179,700 × 0.9 = 161,730 W
4
Convert to kW by dividing by 1,000. 161,730 ÷ 1,000 = 161.7 kW
Answer161.7 kW. Notice how big the number is — 250 A doesn't sound like much until you realise it's 160 kW, or about 80 kettles.
Worked example 1.4 — the reverse, which you'll need even more often
A data hall has 400 kW of IT load at 415 V, power factor 0.95.
What current will the busway carry?
1
Rearrange to make I the subject. I = P ÷ (√3 × V × pf)
2
Convert kW to W, then work out the bottom of the fraction first. P = 400,000 W Bottom = 1.732 × 415 × 0.95 = 682.9
3
Divide. I = 400,000 ÷ 682.9 = 585.7 A
Answer586 A. So you'd specify an 800 A busway — the next standard size up with headroom. You would never specify a 600 A busway for a 586 A load.
Memory trick — the quick mental check

At 415 V and pf ≈ 1, 1 amp ≈ 0.7 kW, and 1 kW ≈ 1.4 A. So 400 kW ≈ 560 A. Close enough to catch a mistake in your head before you reach for a calculator. Engineers who can do this in a meeting look like they know what they're doing — because they do.

1.8 kW, kVA, kVAr and power factor

In plain words

This is the topic that confuses people most, and it's actually simple once you see the picture.

Some electrical equipment — anything with coils in it, like motors, transformers and older fluorescent lights — needs to borrow extra current to build up a magnetic field. It borrows that current, uses it to make the magnetic field, then gives it straight back, fifty times a second. It never actually consumes it. But it still has to travel down your cable, and your cable doesn't care whether the current is being consumed or just borrowed — it heats up either way.

So there are two different numbers:

kW is the useful power — the bit that actually does work, makes heat, turns a shaft. You pay for this.
kVA is the total power your cables and switchgear actually have to carry — useful plus borrowed. You size equipment for this.
kVAr is just the borrowed part on its own.

Power factor is simply the ratio: what fraction of what you're carrying is actually useful. A power factor of 0.8 means only 80% of what your cable carries is doing real work.

Analogy — the beer glass

Pour a beer badly and you get a head of froth. The beer is the useful part — that's kW. The froth is kVAr: it takes up room in the glass but you can't drink it. The whole glass, beer plus froth, is kVA — that's the size of glass you have to buy.

Power factor is how much of your glass is actually beer. Pour it well (pf = 1.0) and it's all beer. Pour it badly (pf = 0.7) and you're buying a pint glass to get 70% of a pint.

power factor = kW ÷ kVA
kVA = kW ÷ power factor
kVA² = kW² + kVAr² — Pythagoras, because it's a right triangle

Why you must care

  • Everything is rated in kVA, not kW. Transformers, generators, UPS, switchboards. Because they're limited by the current they carry — and current is kVA, not kW.
  • A bad power factor costs you real money. At pf 0.8 you need a 25% bigger transformer, bigger cables and bigger breakers to deliver the same useful kW. Utilities also charge large customers a penalty for poor power factor.
  • You fix it with capacitors. A capacitor does the opposite of a coil — it supplies the borrowed current locally instead of dragging it all the way from the substation. A power factor correction (PFC) unit is a bank of capacitors that switch in automatically. You've almost certainly built boards containing them.

Saying "a 500 kVA UPS supports 500 kW of IT load". It depends entirely on the UPS's rated output power factor. An older UPS rated 500 kVA at 0.8 pf only delivers 400 kW. Modern unity-rated units deliver the full 500 kW. Getting this wrong in a design means a 20% capacity shortfall discovered at commissioning — which is a career-shaping mistake, in the wrong direction.

Worked example 1.5 — sizing a transformer for a mixed load
A building has 800 kW of load at an overall power factor of 0.85.
What size transformer is needed?
1
Transformers are rated in kVA, so convert. kVA = kW ÷ pf
2
Substitute. kVA = 800 ÷ 0.85 = 941 kVA
3
Transformers come in standard sizes: 500, 750, 1000, 1500, 2000, 2500 kVA. Pick the next one up. 941 kVA → select 1,000 kVA
4
Check the headroom: 941 ÷ 1000 = 94% loaded. That's tight — no room for growth and it'll run hot. In a real design you'd go to 1,500 kVA, or fix the power factor first.
Answer1,000 kVA minimum; 1,500 kVA in practice. And notice this: if you corrected the power factor to 0.95, the requirement drops to 842 kVA — the same 1,000 kVA unit now sits at a comfortable 84%. Power factor correction can save you an entire transformer size.

2.1 What's inside a cable

In plain words

A cable is a metal wire to carry the electricity, wrapped in plastic to stop it escaping, sometimes with a metal jacket for protection, all wrapped in more plastic.

The metal in the middle is called the conductor — almost always copper, sometimes aluminium (cheaper and lighter, but you need a fatter one for the same job). It's usually made of many thin strands twisted together rather than one thick rod, because a bundle of thin wires bends and a solid rod doesn't.

The plastic around it is the insulation. Its only job is to stop electricity going where you don't want it. Different plastics can take different temperatures — and how hot the plastic can get is what decides how much current the cable is allowed to carry.

2.2 Voltage drop

In plain words

Copper is good at carrying electricity but not perfect — it has a little bit of resistance. So as electricity travels down a long cable, some voltage gets used up on the journey.

It's exactly like water pressure in a very long garden hose. Full pressure at the tap, weak dribble at the far end. The longer the hose and the thinner it is, the worse it gets.

This matters because equipment at the far end needs its proper voltage to work. Give a motor too little voltage and it draws more current to compensate, gets hot, and eventually burns out. So there's a rule about how much voltage you're allowed to lose.

The Australian rule

Total voltage drop from the point of supply to any point of use must not exceed 5% of the nominal voltage. At 230 V that's 11.5 V. At 415 V that's 20.75 V. In practice designers split it: about 2% for the submain, about 3% for the final circuit.

Data centres are often designed tighter than this — 2 to 3% total — because voltage quality matters to sensitive equipment and because you cannot easily upgrade a cable once the room is live.

2.3 Current rating and derating

In plain words

Every cable has a maximum current it can carry safely. Go over it and the insulation gets too hot, goes brittle, cracks, and eventually you get a fire.

But that maximum isn't a fixed number — it depends entirely on how easily the cable can get rid of its heat. A cable lying in open air on a ladder rack in a cool room can shed heat easily. The same cable buried in a hot ceiling, in a conduit, bundled with eleven others, can't. It'll cook itself at a much lower current.

So we start with the cable's ideal rating from the book, then reduce it for every unhelpful thing about where it's installed. Reducing it is called derating. Each unhelpful thing gives you a number less than 1 to multiply by.

Interview question

And I'd raise it as an RFI, because it's a change to the design basis.

2.4 Fault current — what happens in a short circuit

In plain words

Normally, current is limited by the load. A kettle only takes 10 amps because the kettle element resists the flow.

But if a live wire touches earth or another live wire directly — a screw through a cable, a spanner dropped across a busbar, water in a joint — then there's almost nothing left to resist the flow. The only thing limiting the current is the resistance of the supply system itself, which is tiny.

So the current goes enormous. Not 10 amps — fifty thousand amps. For a fraction of a second, until something breaks the circuit.

That much current does two violent things at once: it makes an enormous amount of heat (remember I²R — and I is now huge), and it makes an enormous magnetic force that physically throws conductors apart. A busbar that isn't braced properly will be bent like a banana. This is why switchboards are tested for it, and why bracing is not optional.

The three ratings you must be able to explain

Key fact — the one that connects to your day job

Icw is why switchboard design verification exists. When a data centre client specifies "50 kA for 1 second", they are specifying Icw, and the only honest way to prove it is type-test evidence or verified design to AS/NZS 61439. You already work with this. Most engineers applying for data centre roles have never had to prove it to anyone. Say so in an interview.

Common mistake

Confusing Icu (a breaker breaks the fault) with Icw (an assembly holds the fault without breaking it). They sound similar and mean nearly opposite things. If a candidate mixes these up, an experienced interviewer stops trusting everything else they've said.

What makes fault current bigger or smaller

  • Closer to the transformer = bigger fault. Less cable in the way means less resistance limiting it. The main switchboard always has the highest fault level in a building.
  • Bigger transformer = bigger fault. A 2,500 kVA transformer delivers far more fault current than a 500 kVA one.
  • Lower transformer impedance = bigger fault. Impedance is the transformer's built-in resistance to fault current, quoted as a percentage (typically 5–6%).
  • Long thin cables reduce it. By the time you reach a distribution board on the third floor, the fault level might be a tenth of what it is at the main switchboard.
  • Generators produce less fault current than the grid — typically 3 to 5 times their full load current, versus 20+ times for a utility supply. This causes a real problem: protection settings that work on utility supply may not trip at all on generator. It's a classic data centre design issue and an excellent thing to raise in an interview.

2.5 The protection family

In plain words

A protective device is an automatic switch that turns the power off when something goes wrong. That's all it is. It watches the current, and if the current is too big for too long, it opens.

There are two different "too big" situations, and a good device handles both:

Overload — a bit too much current for a long time. Someone plugged in one appliance too many. Not dangerous immediately, but it'll cook the cable over minutes. The device should wait a while, then trip.

Short circuit — an enormous current, instantly. The device must open in milliseconds, before anything melts or explodes.

The devices come in sizes, like spanners. Small ones protect a lighting circuit; enormous ones protect a whole building.

Key fact — the two mechanisms inside a breaker

An MCB or MCCB contains two separate trip mechanisms in one box:

Thermal — a bimetallic strip that bends as it warms. Slow, handles overloads. Takes seconds to minutes. It's why a slightly overloaded breaker doesn't trip instantly.

Magnetic — an electromagnet that yanks the contacts open when current spikes. Nearly instant, handles short circuits. Takes milliseconds.

An ACB replaces both with an electronic trip unit that measures current digitally and applies programmable settings. That programmability is what makes discrimination possible on large systems.

Common mistake

Thinking a 100 A breaker trips at 101 A. It doesn't. A breaker will carry its rated current indefinitely, and typically won't trip until around 1.13 to 1.45 times its rating, and even then it takes an hour or more. Protective devices protect the cable from long-term damage — they are not precise ammeters. If you need to know a load is at 101 A, you need metering, not a breaker.

2.6 Trip curves

In plain words

A trip curve is just a graph that answers one question: "if this much current flows, how long before the breaker opens?"

Current goes along the bottom. Time goes up the side. The line slopes downward to the right, because more current means less waiting.

At the far left — a small overload — the line is high up: it'll take minutes. In the middle it drops steeply. At the right — a proper short circuit — the line falls off a cliff to a few milliseconds.

Both axes are squashed (logarithmic), because you need to show milliseconds and hours on the same picture. Don't let that put you off; you read it the same way.

Interview question

"A motor circuit keeps tripping on start-up but runs fine once it's going. What's happening and what would you do?"

Good answer: That's the motor's starting inrush — typically 6 to 8 times full load current for a few seconds — hitting the magnetic pickup of the breaker. The thermal element is fine because the running current is within rating. Most likely a Type B curve was fitted where a Type C or D is needed. I'd confirm the running current is genuinely within rating first, check the actual inrush against the curve, then change the curve type rather than simply increasing the breaker size — because increasing the rating would leave the cable under-protected.

2.7 Discrimination — the one that matters in data centres

In plain words

Imagine a fault in one server rack. What should happen: the little breaker feeding that rack opens, and nothing else in the building notices.

What must never happen: the big main breaker upstream opens too, taking out the entire data hall because of one faulty machine.

Making sure the nearest breaker to the fault opens first, and nobody upstream does — that's called discrimination. It's also called selectivity; the two words mean exactly the same thing.

You achieve it by making each breaker slower and less twitchy than the one below it. The small one trips fast; the one above waits a moment to give it a chance; the one above that waits a little longer still.

The three ways to get discrimination

  • Current discrimination. Make the upstream device a much bigger rating. Simple, but only works when there's a big difference in fault level between the two points — which often isn't true near the main switchboard.
  • Time discrimination. Deliberately delay the upstream device by a set time (say 200 ms) to let downstream clear first. This is what ACB trip units are for. The catch: everything upstream has to hold that fault current for that time without damage — which is precisely what Icw is about. Time discrimination and Icw are the same conversation.
  • Energy / zone discrimination. Modern trip units talk to each other. The downstream unit says "I've seen this fault, I'm dealing with it", and the upstream one waits. If no downstream device speaks up, the upstream one trips immediately. Best of both. Expensive, and needs commissioning and testing — which is a real scope item on an ITP.
Why this is a data centre topic, not a general one

In an office, a nuisance trip upstream is an inconvenience. In a data centre, it is an outage with contractual penalties. So discrimination is studied formally, documented in a protection coordination study, proven by relay injection testing during commissioning, and signed off. If you can say that sentence in an interview, you sound like someone who has been on a data centre project.

2.8 Earthing and the MEN system

In plain words

Electricity always tries to get back to where it came from. Not "to the ground" — back to its source. That's the bit everyone gets wrong.

Now imagine a live wire comes loose inside a metal washing machine and touches the case. The case is now live at 230 V. If you touch it, you become the path back to the source, through your heart, and it can kill you.

The fix: connect every metal case in the building to a big fat wire that runs back to the source. Now if a live wire touches a case, there's a lovely easy path back — far easier than through you. An enormous current flows down that wire, the breaker sees it and trips within a fraction of a second.

So earthing isn't about "draining electricity into the ground". It's about giving fault current such an easy path home that the breaker trips before you can be hurt. That's the whole idea.

Analogy

Earthing is a fire escape for electricity. You hope it's never used. But it has to be wide, unobstructed and lead straight outside — because when it's needed, everything depends on it being quick.

Key facts about the MEN

MEN = Multiple Earthed Neutral. Neutral and earth are joined at exactly one point in an installation — the MEN link in the main switchboard — and the neutral is also earthed at the substation.

Never link neutral and earth anywhere else. A second link creates parallel paths, so normal load current starts flowing in the earthing system. That makes earth conductors live-ish, upsets RCDs, and creates dangerous, hard-to-find faults.

Earth fault loop impedance is the total resistance around that red path. It must be low enough that enough current flows to trip the breaker within the required disconnection time. This is a measured, recorded test on every circuit — you'll see it on every commissioning sheet you ever sign.

Data centre specifics

  • Equipotential bonding. Every metal thing — cable tray, racks, containment, pipework, structural steel — is bonded together so nothing can sit at a different voltage from anything else. If two metal objects a person can touch simultaneously are at different potentials, that difference is across them.
  • Clean earth / signal reference. Some equipment specs ask for a separate "clean" earth. Treat this carefully: it must still be bonded to the main earthing system. A genuinely isolated earth is both non-compliant and dangerous.
  • Earthing is a hidden common point. In a 2N design with two fully separate power paths, the earthing system is usually shared. It has to be, for safety. Being able to point that out when reviewing an SLD is exactly the kind of observation that gets you hired.

2.9 RCDs — protecting people, not cables

In plain words

A normal circuit breaker protects the cable. It trips at tens or hundreds of amps. But it only takes about 0.03 amps through a person to stop their heart. A breaker would never notice that.

So we need a different device that protects people. It's called an RCD — residual current device. In older language, a safety switch or an ELCB.

Here's the clever bit. It measures the current going out on the active and the current coming back on the neutral. In a healthy circuit, those two are exactly equal — everything that goes out comes back.

If someone touches a live part, some current goes out through them to earth instead of coming back on the neutral. Now the two numbers don't match. The RCD spots the difference and opens the circuit in about 30 thousandths of a second — fast enough to prevent a fatal shock.

This is why IT load circuits in data centres generally use RCD monitoring rather than RCD tripping, or higher thresholds with careful circuit splitting. Knowing this distinction marks you out as someone who has thought about real critical-power design rather than just read the Wiring Rules.

Interview question

"What's the difference between an MCB and an RCD, and can one replace the other?"

Good answer: They protect different things. An MCB protects the cable from overload and short circuit, and trips at many amps. An RCD protects a person from electric shock by detecting an imbalance between active and neutral of around 30 milliamps. Neither replaces the other — an MCB won't detect a person being electrocuted, and an RCD won't detect an overload that's cooking a cable. That's why RCBOs exist: both functions in one device.

3.1 The voltage ladder

In plain words

Power stations make electricity, then it has to travel hundreds of kilometres to get to a building. Remember from 1.4: to travel efficiently you want very high voltage and low current, because low current means almost no heat lost on the way.

So the voltage gets pushed way up for the journey, then stepped down in stages as it gets closer, until it's finally low enough to be safe in a building.

Think of it as a staircase. Each step down is a transformer.

Key fact — a distinction people get wrong constantly

In Australia, HV means anything above 1,000 V. So 11 kV and 22 kV are HV, and they require HV-licensed switching, permits to work, and AS 2067 compliance. Many people from an international background call 22 kV "medium voltage" and assume LV rules apply. They don't. Getting this wrong is a safety issue, not a vocabulary issue.

3.2 Transformers

In plain words

A transformer changes voltage. High in, low out (or the other way round). It has no moving parts, no motor, nothing to wear out — it's just two coils of wire wound on an iron core.

Here's how it works. AC in the first coil makes a magnetic field that keeps flipping direction 50 times a second. That flipping field passes through the iron core into the second coil, and a changing magnetic field makes electricity flow in any wire it passes through. So power gets across without the two coils ever touching.

The trick is the number of turns. If the first coil has 100 turns and the second has 10, the voltage comes out ten times smaller. Wind more turns, get more voltage. That's the whole device.

And this is exactly why AC won and DC lost — a steady DC current makes a steady magnetic field, and a steady field induces nothing. It has to be changing. AC changes constantly.

Why the vector group matters (and it really does)

If you ever want to run two transformers in parallel — or connect a generator alongside a transformer, or transfer load between two supplies without a break — they must have the same vector group. Two transformers with different vector groups have their outputs offset by 30°, which means connecting them together creates an enormous circulating fault current.

In a data centre with two supplies and a possible tie between them, this is not academic. It is checked at design, verified at FAT, and proven again during commissioning with a phasing check before any two sources are ever paralleled.

Transformer impedance — the number that sets your fault level

Every transformer has an impedance, written as a percentage, typically 5% or 6%. It means: if you short-circuit the output, how much of the rated voltage do you need on the input to push rated current through? A low number means the transformer barely resists fault current, so the fault level downstream is high.

Fault current ≈ Full load current ÷ (impedance %) × 100
a 6% transformer delivers roughly 16.7 × its full load current into a bolted fault

3.3 The switchboard family

In plain words

A switchboard is a metal cabinet full of circuit breakers. Power comes in through one big breaker at the top, gets shared out across a big copper bar, and leaves through lots of smaller breakers going to different places.

Most main switchboards are built as two sections with a tie breaker between them. Normally the tie is open and each half runs off its own transformer. If one transformer fails, you close the tie and one transformer feeds everything.

Why not just leave the tie closed all the time? 3.4 Inside a switchboard — forms of separation

In plain words

Inside the cabinet there are three kinds of thing: the big copper busbars carrying all the power, the breakers (each one is called a "functional unit"), and the terminals where the outgoing cables land.

The question "what form is it?" simply means: how many internal metal walls are there between those things?

No walls at all is Form 1 — open the door and everything is in one big space. More walls means an electrician can work on one outgoing circuit safely while everything else stays live. More walls costs more money and takes more room.

You build these. Most engineers applying for data centre jobs have only ever read about them.

Form 4b and "Type 7" — the bit clients actually specify

Form 4a — terminals for an outgoing circuit are in the same compartment as the busbar or an adjacent unit.
Form 4b — terminals are in their own separate compartment, with the functional unit. This is the fully segregated version.

"Type 7" is a sub-classification (from the British/BEAMA lettering scheme, widely used here) describing exactly how the cable terminations and busbar are separated — glanded cables into their own compartment, with the outgoing terminals separated from both busbar and other units.

Form 4b Type 7 is the phrase you will see repeatedly in data centre switchboard specifications. It means: maximum segregation, work on any circuit with the rest of the board live. It is expensive, physically large, and takes longer to build — all facts you already live with, and all facts a contractor's project engineer needs to understand when a programme is being argued about.

Design verification versus type test

AS/NZS 61439 requires the manufacturer to prove the assembly performs as claimed. There are three routes to verification: testing, comparison with a tested reference design, and assessment/calculation. Which route was used for which characteristic is a real question a client's engineer may ask you.

The characteristics that matter most in a data centre submittal:

  • Temperature rise — does it stay cool enough at full load, with all those breakers packed in?
  • Short-circuit withstand (Icw and Ipk) — will the busbar and bracing survive a fault?
  • Protection circuit continuity — is the earth path through the enclosure adequate?
  • IP rating — dust and water ingress. IK rating — impact resistance.
  • Clearances and creepage — the air gaps and surface distances between live parts.
Interview question — this one is yours to win

"Tell me about AS/NZS 61439 and what Form 4b Type 7 means in practice."

Your answer should include something most candidates cannot say: what it costs. Form 4b Type 7 means more copper, more sheet metal, more compartments, a physically longer board, and a longer build programme. On a data centre where the switchroom footprint is fixed early, that has real consequences for layout and lead time. Then say: "I've built these — I can tell you exactly what drives the lead time and where the programme risk actually sits." That sentence is worth money.

3.5 Busway and busduct

In plain words

Instead of running dozens of fat cables, you can run one long metal box with solid copper bars inside it. That's busway (also called busduct or bus trunking). It bolts together in straight lengths like a Meccano set.

The clever part: along its length you can plug in tap-off boxes — little units containing a breaker that clamp onto the busway and take power out at that point. Move a rack, unplug the box, move it, plug it in somewhere else. No re-cabling.

In a data hall this is enormous. Racks change constantly. Cables would have to be pulled out and re-run every time; busway just needs a tap-off moved.

3.6 CTs and VTs — how we measure big electricity

In plain words

You can't put 3,000 amps or 22,000 volts through a little meter — it would vaporise. So you need something to shrink the measurement down to a safe, standard size.

A CT (current transformer) is a ring that goes around a cable or busbar. The magnetic field from the current passing through it makes a small, exactly proportional current in the ring's winding. A CT marked 1000/5 means: when 1,000 amps flow through the bar, exactly 5 amps come out of the CT. Meters and relays are all built to accept 5 A (or 1 A).

A VT (voltage transformer) does the same for voltage — it's a small transformer that turns 22,000 V into 110 V so a meter can read it safely.

So CTs and VTs are the eyes of the protection system. Every relay, meter and trip unit sees the world through them.

The two kinds of CT — and why it matters

  • Metering CTs are accurate at normal load current but deliberately saturate (stop responding accurately) at high fault currents, which protects the meter.
  • Protection CTs must stay accurate right up into fault conditions — otherwise the relay wouldn't see the fault properly. They're specified with a class like 5P20, meaning 5% accuracy up to 20 times rated current.

Using a metering CT for protection is a real design error: the relay under-reads a large fault and either trips late or not at all. CT class is checked at design, verified on the submittal, and proven at commissioning by primary or secondary injection.

3.7 Protection relays and ANSI numbers

In plain words

A small breaker decides for itself when to trip. On big or high-voltage systems that's not clever enough, so we use a separate box — a protection relay — that watches the CTs and VTs and decides when to tell the breaker to open.

Relays can do lots of different kinds of watching: "is the current too high?", "has the current been too high for too long?", "is current leaking to earth?", "has the voltage collapsed?", "is power flowing backwards?"

3.8 ATS — automatic transfer switch

In plain words

An ATS is an automatic changeover switch. It watches the normal supply. If that supply fails, it automatically switches the load over to a backup supply — usually a generator — and switches back when the normal supply returns.

It has a mechanical interlock so it physically cannot connect both supplies at once. That's important: connecting a generator to the live grid without synchronising them properly is violent enough to wreck the machine.

Think of it as a very reliable, very fast, very stubborn light switch that can only ever be in one of two positions.

4.1 Why data centres are different

In plain words

In an office, if the power goes off, the lights go out, everyone grumbles, and half an hour later it comes back. Nothing is really lost.

In a data centre, if the power goes off for one fiftieth of a second, thousands of servers stop dead. Data being written is corrupted. Banks stop processing payments. Hospitals lose records. The operator pays enormous contractual penalties, and possibly loses the customer.

So a data centre isn't designed to "have a backup". It's designed on the assumption that everything will fail eventually, and the question is only: when this bit fails, what carries the load, and does anybody notice?

That single question is the whole discipline. Every piece of equipment in this Part exists to answer it for a different kind of failure.

4.2 The utility supply

In plain words

Power arrives from the electricity network — in Melbourne, usually at 22,000 volts. A big data centre doesn't take one connection; it takes two, and ideally the two come from different parts of the network, so that a problem in one place doesn't take out both.

Two cables leaving the same substation aren't really two supplies — if that substation fails, both die. Two supplies from genuinely different zone substations is much better, and much more expensive and slower to arrange.

  • Radial supply — one cable from the network to you. Simple, cheapest, single point of failure.
  • Ring supply — the network cable loops in and out of your site, so power can reach you from either direction. Much better availability. This is what an RMU (ring main unit) is for — it's the HV switch that lets the ring pass through your site and tap off it.
  • Dual radial from separate zone substations — the gold standard, and what large data centres negotiate for.
Key fact — the thing that actually delays Melbourne data centre projects

Getting a large HV connection is often the longest lead item on the entire project — longer than the building. It involves the distributor, network augmentation studies, possibly new zone substation capacity, easements and connection agreements, and it can take 12 to 36 months. Power availability, not land, is currently the binding constraint on data centre development in Melbourne's west. Knowing this makes you sound commercially aware, not just technical.

Things that go wrong with a utility supply, and which the site must ride through:

  • Complete outage — the obvious one, and the easiest to handle because it's unambiguous.
  • Voltage sag / brownout — voltage drops for a few cycles, usually because of a fault somewhere else on the network. Far more common than a full outage, and to sensitive IT equipment it's just as bad.
  • Transient / spike — lightning, switching surges.
  • Harmonics and distortion — the waveform isn't a clean sine any more.
  • Frequency excursion — the grid slows or speeds slightly.

A double-conversion UPS (4.4) deals with all of these continuously, which is precisely why data centres use that topology rather than a cheaper one.

4.3 Generators

In plain words

A generator is a big diesel engine — like a truck engine, but much bigger — bolted to an alternator, which is the thing that actually makes electricity when you spin it.

It sits there doing nothing for months. When the power fails, it gets a start signal, cranks up, comes to speed, and takes the load. Then it runs for as long as there's fuel.

Two things have to be controlled very precisely: the speed (which sets the frequency — 50 Hz means exactly 1500 rpm on a common machine) and the voltage. Two devices do this: the governor controls fuel to hold speed, and the AVR (automatic voltage regulator) controls the magnetic field to hold voltage.

Sizing a generator — the four things that catch people out

The naive approach is "add up the load, add 10%". It is wrong, and it's wrong in a way that only shows up at commissioning, when it is very expensive to fix.

A generator supplies only about 3 to 5 times its full load current into a fault, compared with 15 to 20 times from a utility transformer. Raising this in an interview signals real depth.

4.4 UPS — uninterruptible power supply

In plain words

A UPS is a box that sits between the incoming power and the servers, with a big battery attached. Its job is to make sure the servers never see a gap, a dip or a spike — not even for a thousandth of a second.

The good kind works like this. Incoming AC is converted straight to DC (that's the rectifier). The DC charges the battery and also feeds a second device that turns DC back into AC (that's the inverter). The servers are fed from the inverter.

So the servers are never actually connected to the incoming mains. They're fed by the inverter, always, and the inverter is fed from the DC in the middle. If the mains disappears, the DC keeps coming — now from the battery instead of the rectifier — and the inverter doesn't even notice. There is no switchover, because nothing switches.

That's why it's called double conversion: AC to DC, then DC back to AC. It looks wasteful, and it is slightly, but it buys you perfect isolation from everything the grid does wrong.

The two bypasses — a distinction interviewers love

  • Static bypass — automatic and instant. If the inverter fails, or the load draws a huge surge the inverter can't supply (like a downstream short circuit), the static switch instantly connects the load directly to raw mains. The load stays alive but is now unprotected. Critically: for this to happen without a break, the inverter output must be synchronised with the mains. That's why a UPS constantly tracks the incoming mains frequency and phase — so it can hand over seamlessly if it has to.
  • Maintenance bypass — manual, external, usually a set of interlocked switches in the switchboard. It lets you take the entire UPS out of circuit and physically remove it while the load continues on raw mains. Without it, servicing a UPS means shutting down the load.
Key fact — this is what "concurrent maintainability" means in practice

A maintenance bypass is the difference between "we can service the UPS on a Tuesday afternoon" and "we can only service the UPS during a scheduled outage at 3 am on a Sunday, with the client's written approval". When a specification says the facility must be concurrently maintainable, provisions like this are exactly what it is buying.

4.5 Batteries

In plain words

The battery is the only thing in the building that can deliver power instantly. Everything else needs time to start. So the battery's only job is to carry the entire data centre for the 10 to 15 seconds it takes the generator to get going — plus a safety margin in case the generator doesn't start first time.

How long it lasts is called autonomy or runtime. Typical data centre autonomy is 5 to 15 minutes. People are often surprised it's so short — but it isn't there to run the site, it's there to bridge to the generator. If the generator doesn't start within a few minutes, you're shutting down regardless.

A UPS battery isn't one battery. It's dozens of individual cells wired in a long chain (a string) to build up to several hundred volts DC.

Battery room requirements you should know

  • Ventilation — VRLA cells release hydrogen when charging. Hydrogen is explosive above 4% concentration, so mechanical ventilation with airflow calculated to the battery's gassing rate is mandatory.
  • Temperature control — usually 20–25 °C, tightly. This is about lifespan, not performance.
  • Floor loading — a large VRLA installation can exceed 1,000 kg/m². This is a structural input, and it has to be given to the structural engineer very early.

4.6 STS — static transfer switch

In plain words

Most modern servers have two power supplies, so you plug one into path A and the other into path B. If A dies, B carries on. Easy.

But some equipment only has one power inlet — older kit, some network gear, some storage. You can't plug it into two paths. So what do you do?

You give it an STS. It takes both A and B as inputs, picks one, and gives a single output. If the chosen source fails, it flips to the other in about 4 to 8 milliseconds — faster than a server's power supply can notice, because a power supply can typically ride through about 20 ms.

It's fast because it has no moving parts at all. It uses solid-state switches (thyristors) instead of a mechanical contact. An ATS takes seconds; an STS takes milliseconds.

Key fact

For an STS to transfer without disturbing the load, the two sources must be in phase. If A and B have drifted apart in phase, the transfer causes a voltage step the load may not survive. So STS installations require both paths to be synchronised — which in turn constrains how the two UPS systems are configured. It's a good example of how one component's requirement propagates back up the design.

4.7 PDU and RPP

In plain words

The UPS puts out a very large amount of power on a few very fat cables. The racks need small amounts of power on lots of thin cables. Something has to sit in between and split it up. That's the PDU — power distribution unit.

It's essentially a floor-standing distribution board built for a data hall, often with its own transformer inside and, importantly, metering on every single outgoing circuit — because in a data centre you have to know exactly how much power each customer or each row is using, for billing and for capacity management.

An RPP — remote power panel — is a smaller version placed out on the data hall floor closer to the racks, fed from a PDU or from busway. It's just another splitting stage, closer to the load.

Confusingly, "PDU" also means the power strip inside a rack. Context tells you which one people mean: the big floor-standing one is a "floor PDU" or "unit PDU"; the strip is a "rack PDU" or "vertical PDU".

Why metering matters more than you'd think

A colocation data centre sells power, not space. The commercial product is kilowatts. So per-circuit metering isn't a nice-to-have — it's the cash register. It also drives capacity management: knowing precisely how loaded each path is, so you never breach the 50% rule on a 2N system. Expect metering accuracy class to be specified, and expect it to be verified at commissioning.

4.8 Rack power and the A/B principle

In plain words

Every modern server has two power supplies built in. You plug one into a power strip fed from path A and the other into a strip fed from path B. The server happily runs off either one, or both.

Normally both are working, and the server draws roughly half its power from each. Which leads to the most important rule in data centre electrical engineering, and the one candidates most often fail to state:

Each path must be able to carry 100% of the load on its own — so in normal operation, each path can only ever be loaded to 50%.

If you let path A creep up to 70% because "there's spare capacity", and then path B fails, path A is suddenly asked to carry 140% of its rating. It trips. You have just lost the entire data hall by being efficient.

Common mistake — and the most commercially damaging one in the industry

Selling or allocating capacity based on installed equipment ratings rather than on the N-1 condition. Sales says "we have 2 MW of UPS, so we can sell 2 MW". Engineering knows that in 2N it's 1 MW of sellable IT load, because each path must be able to carry the lot. This tension is real, it happens on live sites, and the electrical engineer is the person who has to hold the line. Being able to explain why — clearly, to a non-engineer — is a genuinely valuable skill.

Interview question

"We have a 2N facility with 2 × 1,000 kW UPS systems. How much IT load can we support, and why?"

Good answer: 1,000 kW, not 2,000. In 2N each path must independently carry the entire load, because the design promise is that losing a whole path causes no impact. So each 1,000 kW UPS runs at a maximum of 50% — around 500 kW each — and the total supportable IT load is 1,000 kW. If we went above that, an A-path failure would overload B and we'd lose the room. In practice you'd design to something below that too, leaving margin for load steps and measurement error.

4.9 Redundancy topologies

In plain words

N just means "exactly enough". If you need three units to carry the load, N = 3.

N+1 means one spare. Four units where three would do. If one dies, the spare picks up. Cheap and effective — but they all still feed one shared set of copper, and if that shared bar has a fault, everything goes.

2N means two of everything. Two completely separate systems, from the street to the rack, that never touch. Twice the cost. Nothing shared, so nothing common to fail.

The single most important idea: the difference between N+1 and 2N is not how many units you have. It's whether there's a shared bit. N+1 shares a bus. 2N shares nothing.

4.10 Tiers, concurrent maintainability and fault tolerance

In plain words

There are two different questions people confuse constantly.

They're not the same. A facility can be perfectly maintainable and still go dark if something fails while a component is out for service — because at that moment, the redundancy you were relying on is the thing you took away.

Key fact — the sentence to memorise word for word

"Tier III is concurrently maintainable: I can take any single component out for planned work without dropping load. Tier IV is fault tolerant: I can survive any single unplanned failure, including one that happens while I'm doing that maintenance."

Say that in an interview and you have demonstrated the single distinction that most candidates get wrong. It is worth rehearsing until it's automatic.

Two more things worth knowing about Tiers, because they come up:

  • Tier ratings are a formal certification from the Uptime Institute, awarded against a specific design and then against the constructed facility. Many buildings are "designed to Tier III principles" without ever being certified. It is worth knowing the difference — a client saying "Tier III certified" means something quite specific and expensive.
  • ANSI/TIA-942 is a separate standard with its own Rated 1–4 levels covering telecommunications, architecture, electrical and mechanical. It overlaps with Uptime but is not the same scheme, and mixing the vocabulary is a giveaway.

5.1 The symbol alphabet

In plain words

Drawings don't show pictures of equipment — a transformer doesn't look like two circles. They use agreed shapes so that everyone draws the same thing the same way, and so a drawing stays readable when there are two hundred items on one sheet.

You only need about twenty of them to read almost any electrical drawing. Learn these and you can read a single line diagram.

Cover the labels and test yourself. This page is worth coming back to five or six times over the weekend.

Memory trick

Circles are machines. Rectangles are boxes. Thick lines are copper. A circle with a letter is a rotating machine or a meter (G, M, A, V). A rectangle is an assembly or a component (fuse, UPS, breaker). A thick line is busbar. That one rule gets you 70% of the way on an unfamiliar drawing.

5.2 Anatomy of a drawing sheet

In plain words

Before you look at any of the electrical content, look at the edges of the sheet. The border tells you whether you're even looking at the right drawing — and reading an out-of-date revision is one of the most expensive mistakes on a construction site.

Check four things, in this order, every single time: what is it, what revision, what's changed, and what do the notes say. It takes fifteen seconds and it will save you weeks.

Common mistake — and it costs real money

Working from a superseded revision. On a live project, drawings are reissued constantly. Someone prints Rev B, the design moves to Rev C, and a switchboard gets built to the wrong information. As a project engineer you must have a document control discipline: check the revision every time, work only from the controlled issue, and destroy or clearly mark superseded prints. "Which revision are you working to?" is one of the most useful questions you can ask on site.

5.3 The seven-step eye path

In plain words

Beginners read a drawing like a page of text — top left to bottom right, taking in every detail as they go. They get lost, because there's too much on the sheet.

Experienced engineers don't do that. They run the same seven-step sequence every time, on every drawing, and they ignore everything that isn't relevant to the current step. It's a search pattern, not a reading pattern.

Learn the sequence. Use it deliberately, out loud, until it becomes automatic. Within a couple of weeks you will look like you've been doing this for years — because the sequence is what experience looks like from the outside.

5.7 Schematics and wiring diagrams

In plain words

Three drawings show the same equipment three different ways, for three different people.

A single line diagram squashes all three phases into one line. It shows what connects to what. It's for understanding the system.

A schematic shows the actual logic, wire by wire, laid out so you can follow how it works — not where anything physically is. It's for understanding why something operates.

A wiring diagram shows where every wire physically goes: this terminal to that terminal, wire number 47. It's for the electrician doing the terminating. It's useless for understanding, and essential for building.

Key fact — normally open and normally closed

"Normally" means at rest, with nothing happening and nothing energised. Not "usually". A normally closed contact is closed when the device is sitting there doing nothing, and it opens when the device operates. Getting this backwards is the single most common mistake when reading control drawings, and it's worth saying the definition out loud a few times.

5.8 Control diagrams and cause & effect

In plain words

A cause and effect matrix is just a big table. Down the side: things that might happen (causes). Across the top: things the system should do (effects). Put a mark where a cause should produce an effect.

It sounds almost too simple. But it is the single most useful document in commissioning, because it turns "the system should behave sensibly" into a list of things you can individually test and tick off. Every mark in the table is one test.

When you're doing integrated systems testing, you're essentially proving the cause and effect matrix line by line, in front of the client.

5.9 The schedules

In plain words

Drawings show shapes. Schedules are the tables that carry all the numbers, because you can't fit them on the drawing without it becoming unreadable. A drawing without its schedules is only half the information.

Load schedule

Every load on a board, with its power, power factor, and diversity factor, adding up to the maximum demand.

What to check: does the arithmetic work? Is the diversity realistic (IT load has almost none)? Is power factor consistent? Is there spare capacity for growth?

Cable schedule

Every cable: from, to, size, cores, type, length, installation method, protective device.

What to check: does the size match the calculation? Is the route length realistic? Does the installation method match the derating that was assumed?

Switchboard schedule

Every device in a board: designation, rating, breaking capacity, curve, settings, what it feeds.

What to check: breaking capacity ≥ fault level at that point. Settings consistent with the discrimination study.

Equipment schedule

Every major item: tag, description, rating, manufacturer, model, weight, dimensions, lead time.

What to check: lead times. This is the document that tells you what will delay your project. As a project engineer this is arguably the most important schedule of all.

Common mistake

Reading the drawing and never opening the schedules. The drawing says "MCCB". The schedule says "250 A, 50 kA, adjustable trip set to 0.8, Type C". Almost everything that goes wrong on site is a mismatch between the drawing, the schedule and what was actually delivered. Checking those three against each other is a genuine part of a project engineer's job — and it's how you catch a problem before it's installed.

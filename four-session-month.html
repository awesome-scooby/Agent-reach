The six things that decide this

  1. The niche multiplies everything by up to 28×. Same 15,000 views a month earns ~$30 on a kids cartoon channel and ~$850 on a software/AI channel. Niche choice matters more than every other decision combined.
  2. Kids cartoons: don't. "Made for kids" forces non-personalised ads only — roughly $1–3 RPM against $13–38 elsewhere — and switches off comments, memberships and end screens, killing every non-ad income layer. Animation is also the most expensive thing AI can make.
  3. Review channel: yes — but software, not physical products. Amazon pays 1–2% on electronics with a 24-hour cookie. SaaS pays 20–50% recurring, for the life of the customer. Same work, roughly 50× the economics.
  4. Finance: the best RPM on the board, and a real legal line for you. As an Australian creator, ASIC's finfluencer rules mean specific recommendations can constitute unlicensed financial advice. Education is fine; "buy this" is not.
  5. Never let AI hold the microphone. Your real voice over AI visuals is your defence against the inauthentic-content policy that terminated channels in January 2026. It is also the single biggest retention factor.
  6. Twelve months, four sessions a month. Median realistic outcome is $200–400/month at month 12, not $1,000. Read section 05 before you commit — there's an option that pays better in year one.

Your three ideas, examined

You named children's cartoons, a review channel, and finance. Here's what the research says about each, in the order you should take them seriously.

No

Children's cartoon channel

This is the trap option. It looks perfect for you — no face needed, AI can draw, kids rewatch endlessly — and it is the worst fit on the board. Four independent reasons:

  • The RPM collapse. Anything marked "made for kids" can only serve non-personalised, contextual ads under COPPA. That's roughly $1–3 RPM against $13–38 in the S-tier niches.
  • The features that earn are switched off. Made-for-kids disables comments, personalised ads, the notification bell, channel memberships, and end screens. No email list, no funnel, no product upsell — the two income layers that were going to carry you are simply unavailable.
  • Animation is the most expensive AI output. Keeping a character consistent across episodes is still where AI video is weakest. This is a 15+ hour-per-video format, not a 4-hour one.
  • It's the most heavily policed corner of YouTube. Kids content sits at the intersection of AI-slop enforcement and tightening child-privacy regulation. Highest risk, lowest reward.

Verdict: maximum effort, minimum revenue, maximum regulatory risk. If you want to make animation, make it as a hobby, not as the income plan.

Careful

Finance — best rates, real legal line

You're right that the money is there: $12–35 RPM, the highest-paying category on the platform, because viewers researching credit cards and index funds are worth $22–65 CPM to advertisers.

But there's something specific to you being in Australia that you need to know before you start. ASIC's information sheet INFO 269 sets out how financial services laws apply to "finfluencers". Sharing general financial information and education does not require a licence. Making specific recommendations can constitute providing unlicensed financial advice, which requires an AFS licence or authorised-representative status. Penalties for individuals reach up to five years imprisonment and substantial fines, and ASIC has issued warning notices to influencers and is actively reviewing licensees supervising them as of 2026.

This applies to you as an Australian-based creator regardless of where your audience is.

Safe lane
  • "How compound interest actually works"
  • "What an ETF is, explained in 8 minutes"
  • "How the US tax bracket system works"
  • "How Costco makes money on $1.50 hot dogs"
Do not
  • "Buy this stock before it runs"
  • "My portfolio — copy it"
  • "This fund will outperform"
  • Anything a viewer could act on as personal advice

The other problem: you said you'd need to learn finance first. Explaining a regulated subject you learned last week, from an AI-generated script, is the highest-risk content on this list — wrong facts in finance get you destroyed in the comments and potentially noticed by a regulator.

Verdict: viable and lucrative in the education and case-study lane. Not as your first channel while you're still learning the subject. If the finance pull is strong, start with business case studies — nearly the same RPM, none of the licensing exposure.

The two things AI must never do

1 · Hold the microphone. A flat synthetic voice signals low effort and kills retention inside 30 seconds — and it is the exact signature YouTube's inauthentic-content enforcement looks for. Your voice over AI visuals is a completely different category from AI voice over AI visuals. That distinction is worth your entire channel.

2 · Be the final word on facts. AI research is a first draft with confident errors in it. In finance, health, or anything a viewer acts on, an unverified claim is the thing that ends the channel. Verify before you record, every time.

The tool stack, by job

Scripting & research: a capable AI assistant with a locked style guide — the single highest-leverage thing you'll set up, and it costs one hour.

Voice cleanup: an audio-enhancement tool that strips room noise and filler. Turns a cheap recording into a professional one.

Editing: text-based editors let you cut video by deleting words in a transcript. This is the tool that takes editing from 3 hours to 1.

Visuals: AI image and video generation for b-roll, plus screen recordings for anything you're demonstrating. Screen recordings are free and outperform generated b-roll for tutorials.

Shorts: automatic clip detection and vertical reframing from your long-form video.

Rule: pick one per row in week one and don't change any of them for 90 days. Tool-shopping feels exactly like progress and produces exactly zero videos.

05

Is it actually worth it?

You asked directly, so here's a direct answer, including the part that argues against the whole plan.

The honest expected value

Of people who start a faceless channel with your constraints, roughly speaking: most earn under $50/month at twelve months, largely because they stop publishing before video 20. Among those who actually publish 48 videos in a high-RPM niche, a median outcome lands somewhere around $200–400/month, and a good outcome is $800–1,500/month. The $5k/month faceless-channel screenshots you've seen are the top few percent, usually with more hours and several channels.

So: the honest expectation for 12 months of four-session months is a few hundred dollars a month and an asset that keeps compounding — not a replacement income. If that's worth ~200 hours to you, it's a good deal, because the asset keeps paying in year two and three while the hours don't repeat.

The argument against doing this at all

You're technical enough to build automation pipelines. That skill currently bills at $60–150/hour as freelance or contract work, starting immediately, with no 12-month ramp and no algorithm risk. Five hours a week of that is $1,200–3,000 a month — from month one, and reliably.

On pure money-per-hour in year one, freelancing beats this plan decisively and it isn't close. YouTube only wins on one axis: the freelance hours stop paying the moment you stop working, and the video library doesn't. That's the actual trade you're making. Choose it knowingly.

Do it if
  • You can genuinely commit 12 months before judging it
  • You pick an S-tier niche and stay in it
  • You want an asset, not a wage
  • You'd find the work interesting even unpaid for 5 months
Don't if
  • You need money inside 6 months
  • You'd pick kids, motivation, or compilations
  • You'd use a synthetic voice to save 30 minutes
  • You'd quit at month 3 because nothing happened
The option most people miss

Do both, deliberately. Freelance for the income now; run the channel as the long asset on four sessions a month. The channel then doubles as your portfolio — "here's the automation pipeline I built" is a better sales pitch than any proposal, and the two feed each other instead of competing.

Software reviews are also a trust purchase. Nobody signs up for a $69/month tool on the recommendation of a robot voice. Your voice is simultaneously your policy defence and your conversion rate.

Do toggle the "altered or synthetic content" disclosure in YouTube Studio when you use realistic AI-generated visuals.

Affiliate placement and the disclosure you must make

Placement beats volume. Tool link on the first line of the description, and say once, mid-video, "link's in the description." That's it. No link dumps.

Disclosure is not optional in Australia. The ACCC requires clear disclosure of commercial relationships — say "these are affiliate links" in the video and write it in the description. Also flip YouTube's paid promotion toggle where a brand relationship exists. It costs nothing in conversion and protects you completely.

Sign up to affiliate programs before you need them — most take a few days to approve, and several require an existing channel.

Twelve months, four phases

Each phase has one job and one gate. Don't judge yourself on anything except the gate.

Phase 0 · Weeks 1–2 — Setup

Job: exist. Channel created, tax admin done, first video live.

Gate: one video published. That's the whole bar.

Do not build automation. Do not buy a course. Do not design a logo for three days.

Phase 1 · Months 1–3 — Find the format

Job: publish 12 videos and find out which format the algorithm likes from you.

Watch two numbers only: click-through rate (aim >4%) and average view duration (aim >50%). Ignore subscribers entirely — a lagging indicator, and checking it daily is self-harm.

Gate at month 3: has any single video passed 1,000 views? If yes, make four more like it. If no, change the format — not the niche.

Phase 2 · Months 4–6 — Affiliate first, ads later

Job: more of whatever worked. Apply to the Partner Program at 500 subscribers + 3,000 watch hours (early tier — memberships and tips, no ads yet). Get affiliate links into your top 3 videos and every new one.

In this niche affiliate revenue arrives before ad revenue. A video with 800 views and two SaaS signups beats a video with 8,000 views and none.

Gate at month 6: 500+ subscribers and at least one recurring commission — any amount.

Phase 3 · Months 7–9 — Product + list

Job: hit 1,000 subscribers + 4,000 watch hours for full ad revenue. Start an email list — it's insurance, not a nice-to-have, because a channel termination takes you to zero overnight and the list is the only asset you own outright.

Ship v1 of a digital product: the actual automations and templates you built for yourself, $29–49. Ugly, cheap, real.

Gate at month 9: $100+/month total across all layers.

Phase 4 · Months 10–12 — Automate and compound

Job: now build the pipeline in section 11. If it genuinely halves production time, go to 2 videos a week. If not, stay at one and put the hours into the product.

Gate at month 12: $500+/month, and a library of 48 videos that keeps earning whether or not you show up in month 13.

Operating rules

The plan above is ordinary. These are the part that decides whether you're still doing it in March.

One mode per session

Never script and edit on the same day. Switching modes costs ~20 minutes of ramp-up each time, and you only have twelve hours.

Publish at 80%

A video you don't publish earns $0 forever. Video 40 will be better than video 4 — but only if video 4 goes out.

Write "done" before you start

One line at the top of each session defining what finished looks like. Without it you'll polish until the session ends and produce nothing.

No new tools for 90 days

Tool-shopping feels exactly like progress and produces exactly zero videos. Lock the stack in week one.

One metric per month

Check analytics once a month on Package Day, for ten minutes. Daily dashboard-checking is a slot machine with worse odds.

Novelty budget: 1 in 4

One video a month can be an experiment. This contains the itch inside the channel instead of it becoming a new channel.

Start with the worst part

First 10 minutes of every session: the bit you're avoiding. Momentum is easier to steer than to summon.

Tell one person the schedule

Send them your four session dates each month. External accountability beats any productivity app you'll be tempted to buy.

Your specific failure mode

You're technical, which means building the system will always feel better than using it. Rewriting your pipeline is procrastination wearing a lab coat. The rule in section 11 exists specifically to protect you from this, and it's the most important line in the document.

12

The Australian admin

You already have an ABN as a sole trader, which covers most of it. Five things left.

Do in week 1

Submit US tax info in AdSense

Google withholds US tax on the share of earnings from US viewers. Australian residents claim treaty benefits via the W-8BEN inside AdSense, reducing that rate substantially. Skip the form and you're withheld at the maximum default rate on worldwide earnings. Ten minutes.

GST: almost certainly not yet

Registration is required once turnover reaches the $75,000/year threshold. At a $500–1k/month target you're well below it — but check annually rather than assuming.

Income goes in your personal return

Sole-trader income is reported in the business section of your individual tax return — no separate company return. Set aside a portion of every payout for tax from the first dollar.

Separate account + receipts, day one

One bank account for this only, and keep every receipt. Microphone, software subscriptions, and a reasonable portion of internet costs are likely deductible.

If you go near finance

ASIC's finfluencer rules

General financial information and education doesn't require a licence. Specific recommendations can constitute unlicensed financial advice, requiring an AFS licence or authorised-representative status — with penalties up to five years imprisonment and substantial fines. Get advice before publishing anything in this lane.

Disclose affiliate relationships

The ACCC requires clear disclosure of commercial relationships. Say it out loud in the video and write it in the description, every time.

Verify before you rely on this

These are general rules as I understand them, not tax or legal advice, and thresholds change. Confirm specifics with the ATO or an accountant before your first return — one cheap conversation, and it covers hobby-versus-business classification too, which decides whether you can deduct anything at all.

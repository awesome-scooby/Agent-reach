01

Voltage

4 min
60-second version

Voltage doesn't just tell you about electricity. It tells you who is legally allowed to touch it, what permits you need, and how many months of lead time that adds to your programme.

1.1 / Levels

The four voltage worlds

You will meet all four. The line at 1000 V is the one that changes your job.

The rule

Nominal LV in Australia is 230 V single-phase / 400 V three-phase (harmonised with IEC via AS 60038). Everyone still says 240/415 V, including on drawings and nameplates. It means the same system.

Red flag

Don't raise an RFI because a drawing says 415 V. Do raise one if a calculation mixes the two — a volt drop or fault study that uses 415 V in one line and 400 V in the next has been assembled from two sources and needs checking.

Ask this in week one

"Is there anything above 1000 V on this project?" If yes: who holds HV authorisation, what switching and permit procedures apply, and is there a DNSP or asset-owner interface? Those approvals run in months, and they sit on your critical path.

02

The Maths

6 min
60-second version

You need three rules of thumb, not a textbook. They let you glance at a drawing and say "that doesn't look right" before you open a spreadsheet. That instinct is most of the job.

2.1 / Rules of thumb

Three numbers to memorise

For estimating only — never for design. Design belongs to the designer. Sanity-checking belongs to you.

1.44 × kVA
Current at 400 V, 3φ
A 500 kVA load draws about 720 A. Because 1000 ÷ (√3 × 400) = 1.44.
2 A per kW
400 V motor full load
An 11 kW motor ≈ 21 A. A 75 kW motor ≈ 140 A. Close enough to catch an error.
1440 A
1000 kVA Tx at 400 V
The single most useful number in LV distribution. Scale it: 500 kVA ≈ 720 A, 1500 kVA ≈ 2165 A.
// the two formulas behind all three I (A) = kVA × 1000 ÷ (√3 × V) three-phase, apparent power I (A) = kW × 1000 ÷ (√3 × V × PF) three-phase, real power worked: 250 kW at 400 V, PF 0.85 250 000 ÷ (1.732 × 400 × 0.85) 250 000 ÷ 588.9 = 425 A
At your desk

A supplier quotes an MCC fed by a 630 A breaker, supplying 520 kW of motors. Two seconds of thinking: 520 kW × 2 = ~1040 A of connected motor current. That's well above 630 A — so the design must be leaning on diversity (not all motors run at once). Your question isn't "it's wrong". It's "what diversity has been assumed, and does the process actually work that way?"

2.2 / kW vs kVA

Why equipment is rated in kVA

The rule

A client who says "we need 800 kW" at power factor 0.8 actually needs a 1000 kVA transformer. Get this wrong at concept stage and you order the wrong transformer — a six-month lead item.

Ask this on any VSD-heavy project

Bad power factor costs money (network tariffs penalise it) — that's why capacitor banks appear on drawings. But modern loads (VSDs, LED drivers, UPS, IT) shift the problem from power factor to harmonics, which capacitors alone can make worse. Ask early: "has a harmonics assessment been done, and does the capacitor bank need to be detuned?" This is a classic late-stage surprise.

03

Three Currents

5 min
60-second version

Every electrical design decision answers one of three questions. Load current is about normal life. Short-circuit current is about the worst half-second of the equipment's life. Earth fault current is about whether someone dies.

3.1 / The framing

One circuit, three different currents

CurrentWhat it decidesWhere the number comes from
Load
Ib		 Cable size, breaker rating, busbar rating, volt drop Load schedule → maximum demand → diversity
Short circuit
Ik		 Breaker breaking capacity, cable withstand, busbar bracing Source impedance: transformer %Z + network + cable impedance

The mistake that causes real defects

A cable can be perfectly sized for load current and still be wrong, because it cannot survive the fault current for as long as the upstream breaker takes to clear it. Load current says nothing about fault withstand. This is exactly the trap in the classic "250 A breaker, undersized-looking cable" scenario — and we take it apart properly in Level 4.

04

Fault Level

6 min
60-second version

Fault level is not one number for a site. It is a different number at every board, and it always falls as you move downstream. When someone tells you "the fault level is 31 kA", the correct response is: "at which board?"

4.1 / The estimate

Fault level in your head, in ten seconds

Fault current is limited by the impedance between the source and the fault. Less impedance means more fault current. A transformer's impedance is printed right on its nameplate as %Z — so you can estimate its fault contribution without any software.

// the only fault formula you need at PE level Fault current ≈ Full-load current × 100 ÷ %Z // worked: 1000 kVA transformer, 11 kV / 400 V, %Z = 5.0 FLC = 1000 × 1000 ÷ (√3 × 400) = 1443 A I fault ≈ 1443 × 100 ÷ 5.0 ≈ 28 900 A = 28.9 kA // so the switchboard bolted to that transformer needs to survive ~29 kA, // plus the network's own contribution → you buy a 50 kA board.
Read it backwards too

Lower %Z means a bigger fault. A "better" transformer with 4% impedance instead of 6% delivers roughly 50% more fault current — which can push your switchboard from a 36 kA rating to a 50 kA rating and cost real money. If a supplier offers an alternative transformer, %Z is the first thing you check, not the price.

4.2 / Decay

Why the ratings get smaller as you go downstream

Red flag — cascading

Sometimes a designer uses a downstream device with a rating lower than the prospective fault, on the basis that the upstream device helps clear it. This is called cascading or back-up protection, and it is only valid for manufacturer-tested combinations — specific breaker A in front of specific breaker B. If a supplier proposes it, ask for the tested-combination tables. Never accept "it'll be fine", and never accept it across two different manufacturers.

05

Chain of Ratings

8 min
60-second version

This is the module. Read every part of an installation as a chain: source → protective device → cable → load. Five checks link them. When something is wrong on a real project, nine times out of ten it is one of these five that nobody closed out.

5.1 / The master model

Four links, five checks

5.2 / Check 1 in detail

The derating trap

Check 1 says Ib ≤ In ≤ Iz — design current, then device rating, then cable capacity, in that order. The part that catches everyone is Iz.

Where people get caught

A 95 mm² cable's tabulated current capacity assumes specific installation conditions. Bunch six of them together on a ladder in a 45 °C plant room and the real capacity can drop 30–40%. The tabulated number on the manufacturer's website is not the number that applies to your project.

Derating factors depend on ambient temperature, grouping, installation method, direct burial, soil thermal resistivity and depth. They live in AS/NZS 3008.1.1; the installation rules live in AS/NZS 3000. Verify values against the current edition — never from memory, and never from an old project.

Ask this on any cable schedule you review

"What derating factors were applied, and do they match how the cable is actually being installed on site?" Cables are routinely designed loose-on-ladder and installed bunched in a tray with fifteen others. That is a real, common, and completely invisible defect.

Protection

6 min
60-second version

One circuit breaker does three different jobs, and confusing them is the classic beginner error. Then there's a fourth thing — discrimination — which is the deliverable everyone forgets until commissioning, when it is far too late.

What discrimination actually means

For a fault anywhere, only the nearest upstream device operates. If a fault on one small final circuit trips the main incomer and blacks out the building, you have a discrimination failure — and on a hospital, data centre or process plant, that is a very serious one.

Ask this in week one, not at commissioning

"Has a protection discrimination study been done, who owns it, and when is it issued?" On many projects this deliverable falls between the consultant, the switchboard builder and the contractor — so nobody does it. It surfaces at commissioning, when changing breaker settings or trip units means re-testing, re-witnessing and a variation.

07

Earthing & MEN

6 min
60-second version

Australia uses MEN — Multiple Earthed Neutral. Neutral and earth are joined at exactly one point: the MEN link in the main switchboard. Downstream of it, neutral and earth stay separate. Forever. Most of the confusing faults you will ever chase on site come from someone breaking that rule.

7.1 / The arrangement

One link, and only one

The four site problems you will personally meet

1. A second N–E bond accidentally created in a distribution board or inside a piece of packaged plant (a chiller, a lift controller, a skid). Symptom: circulating currents, nuisance RCD tripping, and days of very confusing fault-finding.

2. Borrowed neutrals — a circuit using another circuit's neutral. A genuine safety defect, because isolating one circuit leaves the other's neutral live.

3. Missing equipotential bonding to structural steel, pipework and cable ladder.

4. Earth continuity quietly broken by a painted flange, a missing star washer, or an earth left unterminated in a gland plate. It tests fine until it doesn't.

Three things you should be able to point to on the drawings

The MEN link position. The earthing arrangement of any alternative supply — whether the generator or UPS neutral is earthed, and what happens to earthing at changeover. And the bonding schedule. If any of the three isn't shown, that's an RFI, and it's a good one to send early.

08

Volt Drop & Demand

5 min
60-second version

Two numbers that look technical but are actually commercial. Volt drop decides how much copper you buy. Maximum demand decides the size of your transformer, your supply and your main switchboard — your three longest-lead, most expensive items.

8.1 / Volt drop

Two cases, and beginners check only one

Current flowing through cable impedance loses voltage. The motor at the end of a long run sees less than 400 V — and motor torque falls with the square of voltage, so a 10% voltage dip costs about 19% of starting torque.

Case 1
Steady state
Full load current, running continuously. This is the one everybody checks.
Case 2
Motor starting
A direct-on-line motor pulls 6–8 × full load current for several seconds. This is the one people forget.
What a missed Case 2 looks like on site

The motor stalls or takes too long to run up. Or worse, it starts fine but the transient dip disturbs everything else — lights visibly dip, contactors drop out and reset plant, the UPS transfers to battery every time the big pump starts. That's a commissioning-week emergency with no cheap fix.

The limit

The commonly applied limit in Australian installations is 5% from the point of supply to the point of use (AS/NZS 3000). Confirm the current wording — and check your project specification, which very often imposes tighter sub-limits such as 2% on submains and 3% on final subcircuits. The spec wins when it is more onerous.

At your desk

A cable schedule shows a 240 mm² cable feeding a modest pump 400 m away. Your instinct says "that's oversized". It probably isn't. On long runs — pump stations, water and wastewater sites, mine sites, carparks, distributed campuses — volt drop, not thermal capacity, is what sets the cable size. Understand why before you challenge it, or you'll burn credibility with the designer.

8.2 / Maximum demand

Nothing runs at once

CONNECTED LOAD add up every nameplate — always the biggest number ↓ × DIVERSITY the allowance that not everything coincides ↓ MAXIMUM DEMAND what you actually buy: supply, transformer, MSB, submains

AS/NZS 3000 provides assessment methods and tables commonly used for commercial and residential work. Industrial and process sites are more often assessed from the load list and process knowledge, because it's the process — not a table — that decides which motors run together.

Three ways PEs get burned by maximum demand

1. MD was calculated at concept stage against an assumed tenancy or process. The process then changed six times and nobody revisited it.

2. The client brief required spare capacity (often 20–25%) and it was quietly consumed during design. The client asks about it at handover, when the transformer is already energised.

3. EV charging, gas-to-electric conversions, batteries and data loads have all moved the goalposts. An MD done from a five-year-old template is now routinely wrong.

Two questions worth asking in your first fortnight

"When was the maximum demand last updated, and against which revision of the load schedule?" — if the answer is a shrug, you have found a genuine project risk.

"Does the MD include EV charging provision, and what spare capacity does the client brief require?"

Worked Example

7 min
60-second version

Here is a real industrial main switchboard. With three rules of thumb and no software, you are about to form a credible technical opinion about it. That is the Level 1 skill.

10.1 / Read it like a PE

1000 kVA substation feeding an MSB

10.2 / The 90-second interrogation

What the five markers mean

1 · Does the incomer match the transformer?

1000 kVA at 400 V → full load current 1443 A. A 2000 A incomer sits comfortably above that with headroom. A 1250 A incomer would be an immediate query — you could never load the transformer to its rating.

2 · Is the fault rating credible?

1443 A × 100/5.0 ≈ 28.9 kA from the transformer, plus the network's contribution. So a 50 kA board is the right call. A 25 kA board here would be a serious red flag — the board could be destroyed by a fault it is supposed to contain.

3 · Is the breaker the right type?

An ACB (air circuit breaker) at 2000 A with an adjustable electronic trip unit is normal for a main incomer, because you need adjustable settings to achieve discrimination with everything downstream. Withdrawable construction matters for maintenance access — and that's a question for the client's operations team, not just the designer.

4 · Does the busbar match the incomer?

2000 A busbar behind a 2000 A incomer: consistent. A 1600 A busbar behind a 2000 A incomer is a genuine defect — the board could be loaded beyond what the bars can carry, and nothing would trip to stop it. Also check the 1 second withstand duration against the project spec; some clients require 3 seconds.

5 · Do the outgoings need to add up? (No.)

400 + 250 + 160 = 810 A of device rating. That is not meant to equal the incomer — outgoing ratings never add up, because of diversity. So don't raise that as a query. Do ask the real question: "what is the calculated maximum demand, and does it fit under 1443 A including the spare capacity the client's brief requires?"

And the one thing the drawing doesn't tell you

Fault level at MCC-01 will be materially lower than 28.9 kA once the feeder cable impedance is included — which is why the MCC's devices may legitimately be rated 36 kA. The SLD rarely shows fault level at each board. Ask for the fault study, and ask which revision of the SLD it was run against.

11

Quiz, Answered

10 min
How to use this

Read the question, have a guess in your head, then open it. The answer is always there — with the reasoning, not just the letter. Getting one wrong costs you nothing. This is the last thing you do in Level 1.

Q1A 250 kW three-phase load at 400 V, power factor 0.85. Roughly what current? (a) 360 A (b) 425 A (c) 510 A (d) 620 AShow
(b) 425 A
250 000 ÷ (√3 × 400 × 0.85) 250 000 ÷ 588.9 = 425 A

The trap is answer (a). If you forget the power factor and divide by √3 × 400 only, you get 361 A — and you'd undersize the cable by a full size or two. Power factor makes the current bigger, not smaller, because the cable has to carry the kVA, not the kW.

Sanity check with the rule of thumb: 250 kW at PF 0.85 is 294 kVA; 294 × 1.44 = 423 A. Same answer, no calculator.

Q2Which quantity sizes a transformer, and why? (a) kW (b) kVA (c) kVAr (d) power factorShow
(b) kVA

Equipment is limited by the current flowing through it — and current follows the hypotenuse of the power triangle, not the base. Windings and conductors heat up according to total current, and they neither know nor care how much of it is doing useful work.

Why this matters commercially: a client who says "we need 800 kW" at PF 0.8 actually needs a 1000 kVA transformer. Get that wrong at concept and you have ordered the wrong long-lead item, on a six-month delivery, against a fixed programme.

Q3A switchboard label reads 400 V · 3200 A · 50 kA 1 s. What does "50 kA 1 s" describe?Show
Short-circuit withstand

It means the board's busbars, supports and structure can survive 50 000 A of fault current flowing for one second without failing — the bars won't be ripped off their supports by the magnetic forces, and the board won't come apart.

Note carefully what it is not: it is not the breaker's breaking capacity (that's Icu, a separate rating on the device), and it is not a current the board is meant to carry in service. 3200 A is the service rating; 50 kA for 1 s is the survival rating.

The PE question hiding in here

Check the duration against your project specification. 1 second is common, but some clients and network operators require 3 seconds — and a 3 s board is a different, more expensive product. Finding this out after the board is built is a variation and a programme hit.

Q4Prospective fault level at a distribution board 120 m downstream of the MSB will be: (a) higher (b) the same (c) lower (d) depends only on the DB sizeShow
(c) Lower

Every metre of cable adds impedance, and impedance throttles fault current. 120 m of cable can drop the prospective fault from tens of kA at the MSB to single-digit kA at the DB.

That is the entire reason a $12 MCB is allowed to exist in the same installation as a $9,000 ACB. It is also why you must always ask "at which board?" when someone quotes you a fault level.

(d) is the tempting wrong answer — the DB's own size is irrelevant. Fault level is set by what's upstream of a point, never by what's at it.

Q5Where is the neutral connected to earth in a standard Australian LV installation?Show
At the MEN link, in the main switchboard

One connection. In one place. The MEN link bonds the neutral bar to the earth bar at the main switchboard, and downstream of that point neutral and earth conductors stay completely separate — through every submain, every DB and every final circuit.

Why: it gives an earth fault a deliberate low-impedance path back to the source, so the fault draws enough current to trip protection quickly, and exposed metalwork never sits at a dangerous voltage while it waits.

What breaks it in the real world

A second N–E bond created accidentally inside a DB or a piece of packaged plant — a chiller, a lift controller, a skid-mounted package that arrived with its own internal bond. Now neutral current has two paths home, and some of it flows through earth conductors and structural steel. Symptoms: circulating currents, nuisance RCD tripping, and days of genuinely baffling fault-finding.

Q6A long cable run to a remote pump station is far bigger than its load current suggests. Which of the five checks is usually the real reason?Show
Check 4 — volt drop

On long runs, volt drop — not thermal capacity — is what sets the cable size. The load might only need 95 mm² to carry its current safely, but 400 m of it would drop too much voltage, so the designer goes to 240 mm² to reduce the impedance of the run.

The PE behaviour this should produce: when a cable looks oversized on a long run, your first thought is "that's volt drop", not "that's a mistake". Check the volt drop calculation before you query it. Challenging a designer on something they got right is an expensive way to spend credibility you'll need later.

And remember there are two volt drop cases — steady state and motor starting. On a pump station, the starting case usually governs.

Q7Judgement. An SLD shows a 1000 kVA transformer feeding an MSB with a 1600 A incoming ACB and a 1600 A busbar. Is this wrong?Show
Not wrong — but it's a query

Run the numbers first: 1000 kVA at 400 V = 1443 A full load. A 1600 A incomer is only about 11% above the transformer's full load current, and the busbar matches the incomer, so the board is internally consistent. Nothing here is a defect.

What it is is a design with no headroom, and that's a question, not an accusation. Three things you'd ask:

Your three questions

1. "What is the calculated maximum demand, and against which revision of the load schedule?" If MD is 1350 A, this board is effectively full on day one.

2. "What spare capacity does the client brief require?" If the brief says 20% spare and MD is already 1350 A, the design does not meet the brief — and that is a finding worth raising formally.

3. "Is 1600 A a deliberate limit?" Sometimes it is: the client may be capping board size for space, cost, or an existing switchroom. If it's deliberate and documented, you note it and move on.

This is the real skill being tested. A weak PE says "it's wrong" and is embarrassed. A weaker one says nothing. A good PE identifies that the design is tight, works out which document would prove it, and asks a precise question.

Q8At your desk. The consultant emails: "Please confirm the switchboard fault rating is adequate. The fault level is 31 kA." What do you send back before answering?Show
Two questions — and do not confirm anything yet
Question 1 — at which board?

Fault level is a different number at every point in the installation. "31 kA" is meaningless until you know whether it's at the MSB terminals, at the MCC, or at some other point. This is the single most common piece of sloppy communication in electrical projects.

Question 2 — where does 31 kA come from?

Which fault study, which revision, and which revision of the SLD was it run against? Does it include the network's contribution as well as the transformer's? Does it include motor contribution (large running motors feed current into a fault for the first few cycles)? And what duration is required — 1 second or 3?

Why you don't just answer. The consultant is asking you to confirm something. The moment you write "confirmed", you have taken ownership of a number you didn't calculate, from a study you haven't seen, for a board you may not have specified. If it turns out to be wrong, the email trail says you confirmed it.

How to reply without being obstructive

"Happy to confirm once I can tie it back to the study — can you confirm which board the 31 kA applies to, and point me to the fault study revision and the SLD revision it was run against? On our current information the MSB is rated 50 kA for 1 s; I'll confirm adequacy at each board once I have that." Cooperative, fast, and you have taken on no risk you can't see.

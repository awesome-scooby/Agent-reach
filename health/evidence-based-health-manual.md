# The Evidence-Graded Operating Manual for Everyday Health

*A skeptical, interdisciplinary review of everyday behaviours, exposures and "biohacks". Compiled September 2026.*

> **Not medical advice.** This is a synthesis of published evidence for healthy adults. If you have a heart condition, diabetes, a neuropathy, epilepsy, are pregnant, take prescription drugs, or have a mental-health condition, several items below have specific contraindications (flagged in each entry). Talk to a clinician before cold immersion, sauna, fasting or supplement use in those situations.

---

## 0. How to read this document

### 0.1 Evidence grades

| Grade | Meaning | What it takes |
|---|---|---|
| **A — Strong** | We are confident this causally helps | Multiple consistent RCTs / meta-analyses on meaningful outcomes, or very large consistent cohort data plus a plausible mechanism plus supporting trials on intermediate outcomes |
| **B — Moderate / promising** | Probably helps, with real limitations | Some RCTs on intermediate outcomes (BP, glucose, mood scales), or strong-but-confounded observational data |
| **C — Preliminary** | Interesting, not established | Small, short, unblinded or inconsistent trials; mostly mechanistic data |
| **D — Weak / unproven** | Popular claim, little convincing human data | Anecdote, animal data, a handful of tiny studies |
| **E — Probably misleading** | Claimed mechanism or benefit exceeds what evidence shows, or contradicts it | Claims rest on misinterpreted studies, conflicted research, or implausible physics/biology |

**Confidence %** = my approximate probability that the *specific stated benefit* is real and meaningful for an ordinary adult. It is a judgement, not a statistic.

Many items get **split grades**. A behaviour can be worth doing (grade A or B) while the reason people give for it is wrong (grade E). Barefoot walking is the canonical example (see §13).

### 0.2 Five rules I applied

1. **Correlation ≠ causation.** "People who sauna / own dogs / go to church / eat nuts live longer" are mostly observational. Healthier, wealthier, more social people do more of almost everything good. I downgrade anything supported only by cohorts unless trials on intermediate outcomes back it up.
2. **Relative risks sound bigger than they are.** "40% lower risk" of something with a 2% baseline is 0.8 percentage points. I try to say which it is.
3. **Surrogate ≠ outcome.** Raising NAD⁺, plasma dopamine, HRV or brown fat activity in a lab is not the same as living longer or feeling better.
4. **Who was studied matters.** Much sauna data is middle-aged Finnish men; much light-therapy data is depressed patients; much fasting data is people with obesity. I flag population limits.
5. **Commercial conflicts.** Grounding, red-light, supplement and cold-plunge research is disproportionately funded or authored by people who sell the product.

### 0.3 The single most important finding in this document

The evidence hierarchy is brutally unglamorous. Five "boring" factors — **not smoking, regular physical activity, a mostly-whole-food diet, moderate-or-no alcohol, and a healthy body weight** — were associated in two large US cohorts with **~12–14 extra years of life expectancy at age 50** (Li et al., *Circulation* 2018). Add adequate, regular **sleep** and **social connection**, and you have covered the large majority of modifiable lifestyle risk that lifestyle can touch.

Nothing in the "biohack" category — grounding mats, cold plunges, red-light panels, NAD⁺ precursors, glucose monitors for non-diabetics, mouth tape — has evidence remotely comparable. Most of their plausible benefit, where it exists, comes from the fact that they bundle in something boring: getting outside, moving, a routine, attention to sleep.

**If you do nothing else:** walk ~7,000+ steps a day, lift something heavy twice a week, keep a regular sleep schedule with bright days and dim nights, eat mostly plants/fibre/protein with little ultra-processed food, don't smoke, drink little or no alcohol, spend time with people you like, and know your blood pressure.

## 1. Sleep and circadian biology

Sleep is one of the few areas where both trial and observational evidence are strong. Experimental sleep restriction (e.g., 4–6 h for 1–2 weeks) reliably worsens glucose tolerance, appetite regulation, mood, attention and vaccine antibody response. Consensus recommendation for adults: **7–9 h** (AASM/SRS, Watson et al., *Sleep* 2015).

#### 1.1 Sleep regularity (consistent sleep/wake times) — **A−** · confidence 80%
- **What / mechanism:** Going to bed and waking at similar times daily, including weekends. Stabilises the circadian clock so melatonin, cortisol, core temperature and sleep pressure line up.
- **What's known:** In UK Biobank (~60,000 people with accelerometers), sleep *regularity* predicted mortality more strongly than sleep *duration* (Windred et al., *Sleep* 2024). "Social jetlag" (weekday–weekend shift) is associated with worse metabolic markers. Causation isn't proven by cohorts, but experimental circadian misalignment (e.g., simulated shift work) causes measurable glucose and blood-pressure harm, so the mechanism is solid.
- **Magnitude:** Probably moderate. Irregular sleepers had roughly 20–50% higher mortality hazard vs the most regular — heavily confounded, so the true causal effect is smaller.
- **Best for:** Everyone; especially shift workers, students, people with insomnia or low mood.
- **Dose:** Keep wake time within ~30–60 min every day. Fix the *wake* time first; bedtime follows.
- **Cheapest way:** A fixed alarm, 7 days/week. Free.
- **Risks:** None meaningful.
- **Worth it?** Yes — top-5 intervention. **Product needed?** No.
- **Overhyped:** Nothing major; if anything, underhyped.

#### 1.2 Getting enough sleep (7–9 h opportunity) — **A** · 90%
- **Mechanism:** Glymphatic clearance, synaptic homeostasis, memory consolidation, hormonal regulation (leptin/ghrelin, cortisol, growth hormone, testosterone).
- **Evidence:** Short sleep associated with ~12% higher mortality in meta-analysis (Cappuccio et al., *Sleep* 2010) — note long sleep is *also* associated, largely because illness causes long sleep. Experimental data: people sleeping ≤6 h were ~4× more likely to catch a cold after rhinovirus exposure (Prather/Cohen, *Sleep* 2015); one week at 5 h/night cut testosterone 10–15% in young men (Leproult & Van Cauter, *JAMA* 2011); restricted sleep increases calorie intake ~250–400 kcal/day in several trials.
- **Magnitude:** Large for anyone chronically short; small for people already sleeping 7+ h.
- **Dose:** 7–9 h *in bed asleep* for most; teenagers 8–10 h.
- **Risks / caveats:** Forcing extra time in bed if you have insomnia makes it worse (see CBT-I).
- **Worth it?** Yes. **Product?** No.

#### 1.3 CBT-I (cognitive behavioural therapy for insomnia) — **A** · 90%
- **What:** Structured program: sleep restriction/compression, stimulus control (bed only for sleep/sex; get up if awake >~20 min), fixed wake time, cognitive work on sleep worry.
- **Evidence:** First-line treatment for chronic insomnia per the American College of Physicians (Qaseem et al., *Ann Intern Med* 2016); meta-analyses show ~20 min faster sleep onset, ~30 min less wake after sleep onset, durable effects that outlast sleeping pills (Trauer et al., *Ann Intern Med* 2015). Digital CBT-I works too (Espie et al., *JAMA Psychiatry* 2019, n≈1,700).
- **Magnitude:** Large for people with insomnia. Irrelevant if you sleep fine.
- **Cheapest way:** Free apps (e.g., US VA "CBT-i Coach"), books (e.g., *Quiet Your Mind and Get to Sleep*; *Say Good Night to Insomnia*), NHS-provided digital programmes in some regions.
- **Risks:** Sleep restriction causes short-term sleepiness — don't drive drowsy; bipolar disorder and epilepsy need clinician supervision.
- **Worth it?** Strongly, if insomnia lasts >3 months. **Overhyped:** Nothing — underused. Most people with insomnia get pills or supplements instead.

#### 1.4 Screening for sleep apnoea — **A** · 90%
- **Why here:** Obstructive sleep apnoea is common (roughly 1 in 10 adults to moderate-severe degree, far higher in overweight men), mostly undiagnosed, and causes daytime sleepiness, hypertension and crash risk. Treatment (CPAP, oral appliances, weight loss — including GLP-1 drugs, tirzepatide now FDA-approved for OSA) clearly improves sleepiness and BP.
- **Cheapest way:** Take the free STOP-BANG questionnaire; ask your doctor if you snore loudly, gasp at night or are sleepy despite enough time in bed.
- **Note:** Mouth taping is **not** a treatment (see §24).

#### 1.5 Bedroom temperature & pre-bed warming — **B** · 65%
- **Mechanism:** Sleep onset coincides with a drop in core temperature, achieved by shedding heat through hands/feet. A cool room helps; a warm bath 1–2 h before bed dilates skin vessels and accelerates the subsequent core-temperature drop.
- **Evidence:** Meta-analysis: a warm bath/shower (≈40–42.5 °C) 1–2 h before bed shortened sleep-onset latency by ~10 min on average (Haghayegh et al., *Sleep Med Rev* 2019) — small studies. Room temperature recommendations (~16–20 °C / 60–68 °F) are based on physiology and lab data; heat clearly disrupts sleep (heat-wave studies), exact optimum varies.
- **Magnitude:** Small–moderate. Big if your room is currently hot.
- **Cheapest way:** Open window, fan, lighter duvet, warm shower 60–90 min before bed, socks if your feet are cold.
- **Product needed?** Usually not. Cooling mattress covers (US$2,000+) have mostly company-funded data; a fan and breathable bedding get most of the benefit.

#### 1.6 Caffeine timing — **A** for harm to sleep · 85%
- **Mechanism:** Adenosine-receptor antagonist; half-life ~3–7 h (much longer in pregnancy, with oral contraceptives, liver disease; shorter in smokers).
- **Evidence:** 400 mg taken even 6 h before bed cut sleep by >1 h (Drake et al., *J Clin Sleep Med* 2013). A 2023 meta-analysis estimated a ~100 mg coffee should be consumed ≥~9 h before bed and a ~200 mg pre-workout ≥~13 h before bed to avoid reducing total sleep (Gardiner et al., *Sleep Med Rev* 2023). People often don't *feel* the effect, but deep sleep drops.
- **Separately:** Coffee itself (not caffeine timing) is associated with lower mortality in umbrella reviews (Poole et al., *BMJ* 2017) — observational; grade B that moderate coffee is safe-to-beneficial for most, C that it causes longevity.
- **Cheapest way:** Caffeine curfew ~8–10 h before bed (so ~noon–2 pm for an 11 pm sleeper).
- **Overhyped:** "Delay caffeine 90–120 min after waking to avoid the afternoon crash" — based on adenosine/cortisol speculation; **no good trial supports it (D)**. Harmless if you like it.

#### 1.7 Alcohol and sleep — **A** · 90%
- Alcohol shortens sleep latency but fragments the second half of the night, suppresses REM and worsens snoring/apnoea. Dose-dependent. Even 1–2 drinks measurably reduce HRV and sleep quality in wearables data and lab studies.
- **Action:** Avoid alcohol within ~3–4 h of bed; fewer drinks overall (see §5).

#### 1.8 Evening light dimming — **B+** · 70%
- **Mechanism:** Light — especially short-wavelength (blue) light via melanopsin cells — suppresses melatonin and delays the clock. Ordinary room light (~100–200 lux) suppresses melatonin substantially and shortens its duration (Gooley et al., *JCEM* 2011). Sensitivity varies ~50-fold between individuals (Phillips et al., *PNAS* 2019).
- **Evidence:** UK Biobank light-sensor data (~87,000 people): brighter nights associated with higher risk of psychiatric disorders and mortality; brighter days associated with lower risk (Burns et al., *Nat Ment Health* 2023; Windred et al., *PNAS* 2024). Observational but dose-dependent and consistent with lab physiology.
- **Cheapest way:** After sunset use lamps rather than ceiling lights; warm, low, dim. Keep the bedroom dark (blackout curtains or an eye mask ~US$10).
- **Overhyped:** "Screens destroy sleep" — see §16; brightness and engagement matter more than colour.

#### 1.9 Short naps — **B** · 70%
- **Evidence:** 10–20 min naps improve alertness and performance for 1–3 h with little grogginess (Brooks & Lack, *Sleep* 2006; many lab studies). Long daytime napping is *associated* with worse health — mostly reverse causation (illness, poor night sleep).
- **Dose:** ≤20 min, before ~3 pm. "Coffee nap" (caffeine then 15–20 min nap) has small supporting studies (C).
- **Caveat:** If you have insomnia, avoid naps.

#### 1.10 Melatonin — split grades
- **For jet lag and delayed sleep phase:** **B+** (Cochrane review, Herxheimer 2002; later RCTs). Low doses (0.5–1 mg) timed correctly (a few hours before desired bedtime) shift the clock.
- **As a general sleeping pill:** **C**. Meta-analysis: ~7 min faster sleep onset, ~8 min more sleep (Ferracioli-Oda et al., *PLoS One* 2013). Small.
- **Risks:** Supplement quality is poor — actual content ranged from −83% to +478% of label in one analysis (Erland & Saxena, *J Clin Sleep Med* 2017); US gummies often contain far more than labelled. A 2025 conference abstract linked long-term melatonin prescriptions to heart failure — observational, unpublished at the time, confounded; not proof of harm but a reason not to use it nightly for years without reason.
- **Worth it?** For jet lag/shift schedules, yes. As nightly habit, mostly no.

#### 1.11 Magnesium, glycine, ashwagandha, "sleepy girl mocktail" — **C/D**
- Magnesium for sleep: a few small trials, mostly older adults, low quality; effect likely small unless deficient. Glycine: tiny trials. Ashwagandha: several small RCTs show modest sleep/stress benefits, but liver-injury case reports and thyroid effects exist; some European regulators (e.g., Denmark) have moved to restrict it. Tart cherry: tiny trials. **Try CBT-I and regularity before any supplement.**

#### 1.12 Weighted blankets — **C+** · 45%
- One reasonable RCT in psychiatric patients with insomnia showed improved insomnia severity (Ekholm et al., *J Clin Sleep Med* 2020); other data weak. Low risk (not for small children or people who can't remove them). Try only if you like the feeling.

#### 1.13 White/pink noise — **C** · 40%
- Masking noise can help in noisy environments (city, partner snoring). Systematic review: low-quality evidence overall (Riedy et al., *Sleep Med Rev* 2021). "Pink noise boosts deep sleep and memory" comes from *closed-loop* stimulation timed to slow waves in labs (Ngo et al., *Neuron* 2013) — a continuous pink-noise app is not the same thing. Earplugs are the cheaper, more direct fix for noise.

#### 1.14 Sleep trackers — **C** as health tool
- Consumer wearables estimate total sleep reasonably but sleep *stages* poorly. Useful for noticing patterns (alcohol, late meals). Obsessing over scores can itself worsen sleep ("orthosomnia", Baron et al., *J Clin Sleep Med* 2017). Not necessary.

## 2. Movement and exercise

Physical activity has the broadest, deepest evidence base of any lifestyle behaviour: RCTs on blood pressure, glucose, lipids, fitness, depression, falls, cognition, cancer-treatment outcomes; massive consistent cohorts on mortality. WHO guidelines (Bull et al., *BJSM* 2020): **150–300 min/week moderate or 75–150 min vigorous aerobic activity + muscle-strengthening on ≥2 days**, and "some is better than none".

#### 2.1 Walking / daily steps — **A** · 90%
- **Evidence:** Meta-analysis of 15 cohorts: mortality risk falls progressively up to ~6,000–8,000 steps/day in adults ≥60 and ~8,000–10,000 in younger adults, then plateaus (Paluch et al., *Lancet Public Health* 2022). A 2025 meta-analysis found ~7,000 steps/day associated with ~47% lower all-cause mortality than 2,000 steps, with benefits for CVD, dementia, depression and falls (Ding et al., *Lancet Public Health* 2025). "10,000 steps" is a 1960s Japanese pedometer marketing number, not a physiological threshold — though more is not harmful.
- **Magnitude:** Large, especially going from very low (~2–4k) to moderate (~7k). Diminishing returns above ~8–10k for mortality.
- **Pace:** Brisk walking associated with better outcomes than slow at the same volume — partly because fitter people walk faster.
- **Cost:** Free. **Product:** None (a phone counts steps).
- **Risks:** Minimal.

#### 2.2 Resistance (strength) training — **A** · 90%
- **Mechanism:** Maintains muscle mass and strength (which decline ~1%/yr and ~2–3%/yr respectively after ~50 without training), improves insulin sensitivity (muscle is the main glucose sink), bone density, joint stability, fall resilience.
- **Evidence:** Meta-analysis: 30–60 min/week of muscle-strengthening associated with ~10–20% lower all-cause, CVD and cancer mortality; combined with aerobic activity ~40% lower (Momma et al., *BJSM* 2022). RCTs: improves strength and function at every age including 90+, reduces depressive symptoms (Gordon et al., *JAMA Psychiatry* 2018 meta-analysis), improves glycaemic control in type 2 diabetes, increases bone density.
- **Dose:** 2 sessions/week, major muscle groups, sets taken reasonably close to failure (~1–3 reps in reserve). As little as 1 set per exercise produces meaningful gains in beginners. Progressive overload is what matters, not the equipment.
- **Cheapest way:** Bodyweight (squats, push-ups, lunges, hinges, rows under a table), a backpack filled with books, resistance bands (~US$20). Gym optional.
- **Risks:** Low injury rate with sensible progression. Observational data hint at a J-curve beyond ~2 h/week of pure strength work — likely confounded; not a reason for concern at normal volumes.
- **Overhyped:** "Anabolic window" (see §5.6); "toning" specific areas; spot fat reduction (doesn't happen).

#### 2.3 Aerobic fitness (VO₂max / cardiorespiratory fitness) — **A** · 85%
- **Evidence:** Cardiorespiratory fitness is among the strongest predictors of mortality in cohort data (Kodama et al., *JAMA* 2009; Mandsager et al., *JAMA Netw Open* 2018), with no upper limit observed. Causality partly genetic, but trials show aerobic training improves VO₂max, BP, insulin sensitivity, lipids and mood.
- **Dose:** 150+ min moderate / 75+ vigorous weekly; mixing moderate and some vigorous is sensible.

#### 2.4 "Zone 2" cardio specifically — **A** for moderate aerobic exercise; **C** for the idea that Zone 2 is uniquely special · 55% for the special claims
- **What:** Steady exercise at an intensity just below the first lactate threshold — roughly "can talk in full sentences but wouldn't want to sing"; often ~60–70% max HR.
- **Claimed mechanism:** Maximally trains mitochondria and fat oxidation.
- **What's actually known:** Moderate continuous training is well-supported. But the claim that Zone 2 is *uniquely* superior to other intensities for mitochondrial or metabolic health is mostly extrapolated from elite endurance-athlete training and small mechanistic studies. Higher intensities also increase mitochondrial content, often more per minute. HIIT and moderate training produce similar or slightly larger VO₂max gains for HIIT in meta-analyses (Milanović et al., *Sports Med* 2015).
- **Worth doing?** Yes — because it's sustainable, low-injury aerobic exercise, not because of a magic zone. **Product needed?** No lactate meter or chest strap; the "talk test" works.

#### 2.5 High-intensity interval training (HIIT) — **A−** · 75%
- Time-efficient improvements in VO₂max, insulin sensitivity and BP in RCTs. The largest long-term trial in older adults (Generation 100, Stensvold et al., *BMJ* 2020, n≈1,500, 5 yrs) found no statistically significant mortality difference between HIIT, moderate training and guideline-following controls, with a trend favouring HIIT — the control group was also very active.
- **Dose:** e.g., 4 × 4 min hard with 3 min easy, 1–2×/week, or shorter sprints. **Caveat:** Build up gradually; people with heart disease should get clearance.

#### 2.6 Exercise snacks / VILPA — **B** · 65%
- **What:** 1–2 min bursts of vigorous activity (stairs, fast uphill walk, carrying shopping hard) spread through the day.
- **Evidence:** UK Biobank accelerometry: people doing ~3–4 one-minute bouts of vigorous incidental activity daily had ~40% lower all-cause and cancer mortality and ~50% lower CVD mortality than those doing none (Stamatakis et al., *Nat Med* 2022). Observational and early. Small RCTs show stair-climbing snacks (3 × 20 s, 3×/day) improve fitness in sedentary adults (Jenkins et al., *Appl Physiol Nutr Metab* 2019).
- **Worth it?** Yes, especially for people who "don't have time". Free. **Caveat:** Effect sizes from the cohort are almost certainly inflated by confounding.

#### 2.7 Walking after meals — **A−** for glucose effect · 80%; **C** for long-term outcomes
- **Mechanism:** Contracting muscle takes up glucose independent of insulin.
- **Evidence:** Meta-analysis: light walking after meals lowered post-meal glucose and insulin compared with sitting; even 2–5 min helped (Buffey et al., *Sports Med* 2022). Stronger effect in people with type 2 diabetes. Starting within ~15–30 min of eating is most effective.
- **Magnitude:** Post-meal glucose peaks reduced by roughly 10–30% in many studies. Long-term outcome trials in non-diabetics lacking.
- **Worth it?** Yes — free, pleasant, adds steps.

#### 2.8 Breaking up sitting — **B** · 65%
- **Evidence:** Interrupting sitting every 20–30 min with 2–5 min light walking lowers post-meal glucose and insulin (Dunstan et al., *Diabetes Care* 2012; many follow-ups). Harmonised meta-analysis of >1 million people: high sitting time's mortality association was largely eliminated by ~60–75 min/day of moderate activity (Ekelund et al., *Lancet* 2016). So "sitting is the new smoking" is exaggerated — **inactivity** is the problem.
- **Dose:** Stand up/walk ~every 30–60 min.

#### 2.9 Sitting vs standing desks — **C** for health benefit · 35%
- Standing desks reduce workplace sitting by ~1–2 h/day short-term (Cochrane, Shrestha et al., 2018, low-quality evidence), but health benefits are unproven. **Standing is not exercise** — energy cost is only slightly above sitting. Prolonged occupational standing is associated with varicose veins, back/foot pain and in one cohort heart disease (Smith et al., *Am J Epidemiol* 2018). Alternating and moving is the goal.
- **Product needed?** No. Walking breaks, walking meetings or phone calls on foot deliver more.

#### 2.10 Balance training — **A** for fall prevention in older adults · 85%
- Exercise programmes (especially balance + functional + strength) reduce the rate of falls by ~23%; tai chi by ~19% (Sherrington et al., Cochrane 2019). Falls are a leading cause of injury death over 65.
- **Dose:** ≥3 h/week of mixed balance/strength work in older adults; for younger adults, single-leg standing while brushing teeth is a cheap habit (C for benefit in young people). **Free.**

#### 2.11 Stretching and mobility — **B** for range of motion · 70%; **D/E** for injury prevention and soreness
- **Evidence:** Stretching increases flexibility/range of motion. It does **not** meaningfully reduce delayed-onset muscle soreness (Herbert et al., Cochrane 2011) and pre-exercise static stretching does not reliably prevent injury in general populations. Full-range resistance training improves range of motion about as well as stretching (Afonso et al., *Sports Med* 2021).
- **Worth it?** If you need more range (e.g., for a sport, stiffness, ageing), yes; 5–10 min several times/week. Dynamic warm-ups before sport are sensible. **Product?** No (foam rollers: modest short-term ROM/soreness effects, C).

#### 2.12 Yoga, tai chi, pilates — **B** · 65%
- Comparable to other exercise for chronic low back pain (Cochrane, Wieland et al., 2022), modest benefits for anxiety/depression, balance (tai chi strong for falls). Good choice if you'll actually do it. Claims about "detoxing" via twists: E.

#### 2.13 Exercise for depression and anxiety — **A−** · 80%
- Umbrella review: physical activity produced medium reductions in depressive symptoms (SMD ≈ −0.43) and anxiety (Singh et al., *BJSM* 2023). Network meta-analysis of 218 RCTs: walking/jogging, yoga and strength training were among the most effective, with effects comparable to psychotherapy — though trial quality was mostly low (Noetel et al., *BMJ* 2024).
- **Caveat:** An adjunct, not a substitute, for treatment of moderate–severe depression.

#### 2.14 Active commuting — **B** · 60%
- Cycling commuters had ~41% lower all-cause mortality in UK Biobank (Celis-Morales et al., *BMJ* 2017) — observational. Plausible because it builds activity into the day without willpower. Traffic injury risk and air pollution exposure are real but, in most modelling, outweighed by the activity benefit.

#### 2.15 Isometric exercise for blood pressure (wall sits, handgrip, planks) — **A−** · 75%
- **Underused.** Network meta-analysis of 270 RCTs: isometric exercise produced the largest BP reductions of any exercise mode (~8/4 mmHg), with wall squats particularly effective (Edwards et al., *BJSM* 2023). Typical protocol: 4 × 2-min wall sit with 2 min rest, 3×/week.
- **Caveat:** Trials are short (weeks) and many small; don't hold your breath during efforts. Uncontrolled severe hypertension: check with doctor first.

## 3. Sunlight and light exposure

#### 3.1 Bright daytime / morning outdoor light — **B+** · 70%
- **Mechanism:** Light is the dominant time cue for the master clock (suprachiasmatic nucleus). Morning light advances the clock (earlier sleepiness and waking), strengthens circadian amplitude, raises alertness and supports mood. Outdoors is vastly brighter than indoors: ~10,000–100,000 lux in daylight (≥1,000 even when overcast) vs ~100–500 lux indoors.
- **Evidence:** A week of camping with only natural light shifted melatonin timing ~2 h earlier (Wright et al., *Curr Biol* 2013). UK Biobank light-sensor data: brighter days associated with lower depression risk and mortality (Burns 2023; Windred 2024) — observational. Trials of morning light in healthy people show modest improvements in sleep timing and alertness. Bright-light therapy works clinically for seasonal depression and as an add-on for non-seasonal depression (meta-analysis of 11 RCTs, n≈858: remission 41% vs 23%; Menegaz de Almeida et al., *JAMA Psychiatry* 2025).
- **Magnitude:** Small–moderate for healthy people; large for night owls struggling with early schedules and for seasonal depression.
- **Dose:** ~10–30 min outdoors in the first hours after waking (longer on dark overcast days). Through a window loses much of the intensity; sunglasses reduce it but it's still far brighter than indoors.
- **Cheapest way:** Walk outside, coffee on the balcony, walk part of your commute.
- **Risks:** Never look at the sun. Light therapy can trigger mania in bipolar disorder.
- **Overhyped:** "You must get sunlight in your eyes within 30 minutes of waking or your cortisol/dopamine rhythm is ruined" — **E** as stated. The timing is soft; consistency over days matters more than any one morning.
- **Product needed?** For most, no. A 10,000-lux light box (~US$50–100) is justified for seasonal depression, very dark winters, or early shift schedules.

#### 3.2 Moderate sun exposure (skin) — **B** for vitamin D and mood; **A** for harm from burning
- **Mechanism:** UVB → vitamin D synthesis; UVA releases nitric oxide from skin stores (small BP drops in lab studies, Liu et al., *J Invest Dermatol* 2014 — C).
- **Evidence:** Swedish cohort: women who avoided sun had higher mortality than sun-seekers (Lindqvist et al., *J Intern Med* 2014/2016) — heavily confounded by activity and health. Sunburn and cumulative UV are established causes of skin cancer, including melanoma.
- **Practical:** Short, non-burning exposure is reasonable; for longer exposure use shade, clothing, sunscreen. At high latitudes in winter, skin can't make vitamin D — diet/supplement matters then.
- **Confidence that "more sun = longer life" causally:** ~30%.

#### 3.3 Daily sunscreen — **A** for skin cancer & photoaging · 85%
- Nambour RCT (Australia): daily sunscreen roughly halved invasive melanoma incidence over follow-up (Green et al., *J Clin Oncol* 2011) and reduced skin aging (Hughes et al., *Ann Intern Med* 2013). Applies most to fair-skinned people in high-UV settings.
- **Cheapest:** Any broad-spectrum SPF 30+ you'll actually use daily on face/hands. Luxury brands don't add protection.
- **Overhyped fears:** "Sunscreen causes vitamin D deficiency" — real-world use has minimal effect on vitamin D status. Some ingredients (oxybenzone) have absorption questions; mineral sunscreens avoid this.

#### 3.4 Outdoor time for children's eyes (myopia prevention) — **A** · 85%
- **Underused.** Cluster RCT in Guangzhou: adding 40 min outdoor activity at school reduced 3-yr myopia incidence from 39.5% to 30.4% (He et al., *JAMA* 2015); Taiwanese trials confirm. Mechanism likely bright light → retinal dopamine → slows eye elongation.
- **Dose:** ~2 h/day outdoors for children. Free.

#### 3.5 Blue light at night — see §16.

## 4. Nature and outdoor exposure

The honest summary: time in green/blue space is consistently associated with better mental health and some physical outcomes, and short-term experiments show reduced stress and rumination. But it is very hard to separate nature itself from the exercise, social contact, sunlight, quiet, cleaner air and wealth that come with it. **Practically this doesn't matter — you get the bundle.**

#### 4.1 Time in nature / green space — **B** · 65%
- **Evidence:** Umbrella/meta-analysis: greenspace exposure associated with lower cortisol, heart rate, diastolic BP, type 2 diabetes, all-cause mortality and better self-reported health (Twohig-Bennett & Jones, *Environ Res* 2018) — mostly observational. English survey (~20,000): people spending ≥120 min/week in nature reported better health/wellbeing than those spending none, plateauing around 200–300 min (White et al., *Sci Rep* 2019). Small experiment: a 90-min nature walk reduced rumination and subgenual prefrontal activity vs an urban walk (Bratman et al., *PNAS* 2015, n=38).
- **Magnitude:** Small–moderate for mood and stress; unknown for hard outcomes.
- **Dose:** ~2 h/week, in whatever chunks. Parks count.
- **Worth it?** Yes. Free.

#### 4.2 Forest bathing (shinrin-yoku) — **B−** for stress/mood · 55%; **D** for immune/NK-cell claims
- **What:** Slow, mindful time in a forest.
- **Evidence:** Meta-analyses show reductions in cortisol, BP, anxiety scores after sessions, but studies are small, often unblinded, many Japanese/Korean with non-random site assignment (e.g., Antonelli et al., *Int J Biometeorol* 2019 on cortisol). The famous claims that phytoncides (tree volatiles) raise natural killer cell activity come from a handful of small studies by one group (Li et al., ~2007–2010).
- **Worth it?** Yes as a pleasant walk. **Product?** Guided "forest therapy" sessions are fine but not necessary; essential-oil diffusers do not replicate it.

#### 4.3 Blue space (ocean, lakes, rivers) — **C+** · 45%
- Associations with better mental health, especially coastal living in lower-income groups (Gascon et al., review 2017; White et al.). Confounding and selection are big. Swimming outdoors adds exercise and cold exposure. **Drowning risk is real.**

#### 4.4 Gardening — **B** · 60%
- Meta-analysis of mostly small studies: gardening associated with reduced depression/anxiety, improved BMI and life satisfaction (Soga et al., *Prev Med Rep* 2017). First RCT of community gardening (n≈290): increased fibre intake and physical activity and reduced stress/anxiety vs control (Litt et al., *Lancet Planet Health* 2023).
- **Risks:** Soil exposure: tetanus (keep vaccinated), back strain; old urban soils may contain lead — test before growing food.

#### 4.5 "Awe" walks, nature sounds, window views — **C** · 40%
- A small trial of weekly "awe walks" in older adults increased positive emotion (Sturm et al., *Emotion* 2022). Hospital-window-view study (Ulrich, *Science* 1984) is a classic but small and old. Nature-sound playback shows small stress-recovery effects in labs. Cheap, low-stakes, low-certainty.

## 5. Nutrition and meal timing

Nutrition science is noisier than exercise or sleep science: most data are observational, food-frequency questionnaires are imprecise, and long-term diet RCTs are rare. The *dietary patterns* below nonetheless have converging trial + cohort support. **What you eat matters far more than when you eat it.**

#### 5.1 Mediterranean-style / whole-food, plant-forward diet — **A** · 80%
- **Evidence:** PREDIMED (Spain, high-risk adults): Mediterranean diet supplemented with extra-virgin olive oil or nuts reduced major cardiovascular events ~30% vs a low-fat control (Estruch et al., *NEJM* 2018, republished after randomisation irregularities were corrected — conclusions held with caveats). CORDIOPREV (secondary prevention) also favoured Mediterranean over low-fat (Delgado-Lista et al., *Lancet* 2022). DASH diet lowers BP ~5–11 mmHg in trials (Appel 1997; Sacks 2001, *NEJM*).
- **What it means in practice:** vegetables, fruit, legumes, whole grains, nuts, olive oil, fish; less processed meat, refined starch and sugary drinks.
- **Absolute effect:** In PREDIMED, roughly 3 fewer major CV events per 100 high-risk people over ~5 years.
- **Product needed?** No. Expensive "superfoods" add nothing essential.

#### 5.2 Fibre (≥25–30 g/day) — **A−** · 80%
- Meta-analysis of cohorts and trials: 25–29 g/day associated with 15–30% lower all-cause and CV mortality, CHD, stroke, type 2 diabetes and colorectal cancer, with dose-response; RCTs show improvements in weight, BP and cholesterol (Reynolds et al., *Lancet* 2019). Most Western adults eat ~15–20 g.
- **Cheapest:** Beans/lentils (the cheapest high-fibre, high-protein food), oats, whole-grain bread, frozen veg, fruit, nuts. Increase gradually to avoid bloating.

#### 5.3 Minimise ultra-processed foods (UPF) — **B+** · 70%
- Inpatient RCT (n=20): ad-libitum UPF diet led to ~500 kcal/day higher intake and weight gain vs matched unprocessed diet (Hall et al., *Cell Metab* 2019). Umbrella review: UPF associated with 32 adverse outcomes, most convincingly CV mortality, type 2 diabetes and common mental disorders (Lane et al., *BMJ* 2024) — observational. "UPF" is a heterogeneous category (wholemeal bread and soft drinks both qualify), so the useful heuristic is: less sugary drinks, processed meat, packaged snacks, fast food.

#### 5.4 Sugar-sweetened beverages — cut them: **A** · 85%
- Consistent association with weight gain, type 2 diabetes and CVD; RCTs in children show replacing them reduces weight gain. Easiest dietary win for heavy consumers. Water, sparkling water, tea, coffee.

#### 5.5 Protein amount — **A** for adequacy/muscle with training · 85%
- **Evidence:** In resistance-trained adults, gains in lean mass plateau around ~1.6 g/kg/day (95% CI up to ~2.2) (Morton et al., *BJSM* 2018). RDA is 0.8 g/kg (a minimum). Older adults likely need ~1.0–1.2 g/kg, more if ill (PROT-AGE, Bauer et al., *JAMDA* 2013) because of "anabolic resistance".
- **Who benefits most:** Older adults, people losing weight (especially on GLP-1 drugs), people lifting.
- **Cheapest:** Eggs, dairy/Greek yoghurt, legumes, canned fish, chicken, tofu. Protein powder is just convenient food.
- **Caveats:** Advanced kidney disease → follow medical advice. Very high intakes aren't dangerous for healthy kidneys but are unnecessary.

#### 5.6 Protein timing / "anabolic window" — **C** · 30% for meaningful timing effects
- Meta-analysis: apparent benefits of protein timing around workouts disappeared once total daily protein was controlled (Schoenfeld, Aragon & Krieger, *JISSN* 2013). The "max 20–30 g per meal can be used" claim was challenged by a trial showing a 100 g dose produced a larger and longer anabolic response (Trommelen et al., *Cell Rep Med* 2023). Spreading protein across 3–4 meals (~0.3–0.4 g/kg each) is a reasonable, mildly supported heuristic, especially in older adults.
- **Bottom line:** Hit your daily total. Don't stress the clock.

#### 5.7 Intermittent fasting / time-restricted eating (TRE) — **B** as a weight-loss tool equal to (not better than) calorie restriction · 70%; **C** for special metabolic benefits beyond weight loss
- **Evidence:** 16:8 TRE produced no significant weight loss vs 3 meals/day in the TREAT RCT, with some lean-mass loss (Lowe et al., *JAMA Intern Med* 2020). TRE + calorie restriction vs calorie restriction alone: no difference at 12 months (Liu et al., *NEJM* 2022). Alternate-day fasting ≈ daily calorie restriction (Trepanowski et al., *JAMA Intern Med* 2017). Early TRE (eating window ending mid-afternoon) improved insulin sensitivity and BP without weight loss in a tiny crossover (Sutton et al., *Cell Metab* 2018, n=8) — intriguing, unreplicated at scale.
- **Scary headline to ignore:** A 2024 conference abstract linking <8-h eating windows to 91% higher CV death came from 1–2 days of dietary recall — **not credible evidence of harm** either.
- **Who might benefit:** People who find it an easier way to eat less. **Caveats:** Not for people with eating-disorder history, pregnancy, type 1 diabetes or on insulin/sulfonylureas without supervision, or underweight/frail older adults.
- **Overhyped:** "Autophagy", "cellular cleanup" and "longevity switches" from 16-h fasts — human evidence for meaningful autophagy change from short fasts is essentially absent (**D/E**).

#### 5.8 Meal timing: earlier, larger breakfast/lunch; lighter late meals — **C+** · 45%
- Late eating increased hunger and reduced energy expenditure in a tightly controlled study (Vujović et al., *Cell Metab* 2022, n=16). Morning- vs evening-loaded calories produced similar weight loss, but less hunger with morning-loading (Ruddick-Collins et al., *Cell Metab* 2022). Eating a large meal right before bed can worsen reflux and sleep.
- **Practical:** Finish main meals ~2–3 h before bed if convenient.

#### 5.9 Hydration — **B** · 65% (for "drink to thirst" adequacy)
- **Evidence:** Water needs vary hugely with body size, climate and activity (doubly-labelled water study, Yamada et al., *Science* 2022). The "8 glasses a day" rule has no scientific origin; food provides ~20% of water, and tea/coffee count. Dehydration of ≥~2% body mass modestly impairs endurance and cognition. For people with a history of kidney stones, drinking enough to pass >2 L urine/day halves recurrence (Borghi et al., *J Urol* 1996) — **A** for that group.
- **Who needs to think about it:** Older adults (blunted thirst), heat, heavy exercise, stone-formers, some medications.
- **Cheapest check:** Pale-yellow urine.
- **Overhyped:** Electrolyte powders for sedentary people; "most people are chronically dehydrated" (**E**); alkaline or hydrogen water (**E**).
- **Risk:** Over-drinking during long endurance events → hyponatraemia (potentially fatal).

#### 5.10 Alcohol: less is better — **A** · 85%
- Large reanalyses find no mortality benefit from "moderate drinking" once biased abstainer groups are handled (Zhao et al., *JAMA Netw Open* 2023). Alcohol is a Group 1 carcinogen (breast, bowel, oesophagus, liver, mouth); WHO states no safe level for cancer. Canada's 2023 guidance: ≤2 drinks/week is low risk; 3–6 moderate. Absolute risks at 1 drink/day are modest but real; at 2+ they climb.
- **Worth it?** Reducing is one of the highest-value changes for drinkers. Improves sleep within days.

#### 5.11 Salt substitution (potassium-enriched salt) — **A** for people with high BP or older adults · 80%
- **Underused.** SSaSS RCT (~21,000 people in rural China with prior stroke or aged ≥60 with hypertension): replacing regular salt with 75% NaCl / 25% KCl reduced stroke by 14%, major CV events 13% and death 12% over ~5 years (Neal et al., *NEJM* 2021).
- **Contraindications:** Kidney disease, potassium-sparing diuretics, ACE inhibitors/ARBs at high doses, hyperkalaemia risk — check with doctor.
- **Cheap:** Salt substitutes cost a few dollars.

#### 5.12 Nuts, legumes, olive oil, oily fish (1–2×/week) — **B+** · 70%
- Nuts: cohort meta-analyses show ~20% lower CVD and mortality at ~a handful daily (Aune et al., *BMC Med* 2016); PREDIMED trial arm. Fish: dietary guidelines support 1–2 portions/week. **Fish-oil capsules** are different — see §24.

#### 5.13 Coffee and tea — **B** safe-to-beneficial · 65%
- Coffee 2–4 cups/day associated with lower mortality, T2D, liver disease; RCTs don't exist for longevity. Unfiltered coffee (French press, Turkish) raises LDL via cafestol — paper filters remove it. Green tea: small benefits in some trials for BP/LDL.

#### 5.14 Fermented foods — **C+** · 45%
- A 10-week trial (n=36) found a high-fermented-food diet increased gut microbiome diversity and lowered several inflammatory markers, whereas high-fibre did not in that short window (Wastyk et al., *Cell* 2021). Promising, small. Yoghurt, kefir, kimchi, sauerkraut are cheap.

#### 5.15 Red and processed meat — **B** for limiting processed meat · 65%
- IARC classifies processed meat as a Group 1 carcinogen (colorectal cancer; ~18% relative risk increase per 50 g/day — absolute lifetime risk rising from ~5% to ~6%). Evidence for unprocessed red meat is weaker and contested (Johnston et al., *Ann Intern Med* 2019 rated certainty low). Reasonable: processed meat occasional; red meat a few times a week max.

#### 5.16 Cooking at home / eating slowly — **C+** · 45%
- Cooking at home associated with better diet quality; eating slowly associated with lower intake in small lab studies. Low-cost, low-risk, plausible.

## 6. Metabolic health

The priority list: **weight trajectory, waist circumference, activity, muscle mass, fibre/UPF, sleep, alcohol.** Know your fasting glucose/HbA1c, lipids and BP.

#### 6.1 Waist and weight management — **A** · 85%
- Excess visceral fat is causally linked (including genetic Mendelian randomisation evidence) to type 2 diabetes, hypertension, fatty liver, CVD and several cancers. Modest loss (5–10%) produces large improvements in glucose and BP; the Diabetes Prevention Program lifestyle arm cut diabetes incidence 58% over ~3 years (Knowler et al., *NEJM* 2002).
- **Cheapest measure:** Tape measure: waist < half your height is a useful target (NICE).
- **Note:** GLP-1/GIP drugs (semaglutide, tirzepatide) are medical therapies with strong RCT evidence (e.g., SELECT: 20% fewer CV events in people with obesity and CVD, Lincoff et al., *NEJM* 2023). Not "lifestyle", but for eligible people they outperform every lifestyle hack.

#### 6.2 Continuous glucose monitors (CGMs) for non-diabetics — **C/D** · 25%
- Valuable in diabetes. In healthy people, glucose spikes after carbs are normal; the clinical meaning of "spikes" in non-diabetics is unknown; CGM readings show day-to-day variability that makes single-meal "experiments" unreliable. Personalised glycaemic responses exist (Zeevi et al., *Cell* 2015) but acting on them hasn't been shown to improve hard outcomes. Risk of disordered eating and unnecessary food fear. **Not worth ~US$100–200/month for most.** A cheap HbA1c test at your GP answers the real question.

#### 6.3 Vinegar before meals, "glucose hacks" (veg first, etc.) — **C** · 40%
- Small trials show vinegar (~1–2 tbsp diluted) and eating vegetables/protein before carbohydrates modestly blunt post-meal glucose. Effects on long-term outcomes unknown. Harmless if diluted (undiluted vinegar erodes enamel). A post-meal walk is more robust (§2.7).

#### 6.4 Sleep, stress and metabolism — see §1 and §7. One week of sleep restriction measurably reduces insulin sensitivity in healthy young people.

## 7. Stress regulation

Chronic stress affects sleep, BP, eating and mood. The strongest evidence is for **exercise, sleep, social support, and structured psychological skills (CBT, mindfulness-based programmes)**. Quick physiological techniques help in the moment but their long-term effects are less established.

#### 7.1 Mindfulness meditation (structured programmes: MBSR/MBCT) — **A−** for anxiety/depression/pain · 75%
- **Evidence:** Meta-analysis of 47 RCTs: moderate evidence for small-to-moderate improvements in anxiety, depression and pain (effect sizes ~0.3), no better than other active treatments like exercise or CBT (Goyal et al., *JAMA Intern Med* 2014). MBCT reduces depressive relapse risk (~31% lower hazard) in recurrent depression (Kuyken et al., *JAMA Psychiatry* 2016, IPD meta-analysis). MBSR was non-inferior to escitalopram for anxiety disorders in an 8-week RCT (Hoge et al., *JAMA Psychiatry* 2023).
- **Dose:** 8-week programmes typically use ~30–45 min/day; many people sustain 10–20 min/day. Benefit at 5 min/day is less certain.
- **Cheapest:** Free MBSR courses online (e.g., Palouse Mindfulness), free app tiers, library books.
- **Risks:** A minority experience worsening anxiety, dissociation or re-experiencing trauma, especially on intensive retreats. People with PTSD/psychosis: trauma-informed guidance.
- **Overhyped:** "Rewires your brain / grows grey matter" — early MRI findings (e.g., Hölzel 2011) did not replicate well in larger controlled studies (e.g., Kral et al., *Sci Adv* 2022).

#### 7.2 NSDR / Yoga Nidra / body scan — **C+** · 45%
- **What:** Guided lying-down relaxation (body scan, breath awareness) — "non-sleep deep rest" is a rebranding of yoga nidra and progressive relaxation.
- **Evidence:** Small trials show reduced perceived stress and improved sleep quality (e.g., Moszeik et al., *Curr Psychol* 2022). The widely-quoted "65% increase in dopamine" comes from a PET study of 8 experienced practitioners (Kjaer et al., *Cogn Brain Res* 2002) — cannot be generalised. Progressive muscle relaxation (older, better-studied) has moderate evidence for anxiety.
- **Worth it?** Yes as a 10–20-min recovery tool, especially for people who can't nap. Free (YouTube). Don't expect neurochemical miracles.

#### 7.3 Physiological sigh / cyclic sighing — **B−** · 50%
- **What:** Two inhales through the nose (second one topping up) followed by a long slow exhale.
- **Evidence:** Remote RCT (n=108): 5 min/day of cyclic sighing for a month improved positive mood and reduced respiratory rate slightly more than mindfulness meditation (Balban et al., *Cell Rep Med* 2023). Small effect, one trial, no inactive control group.
- **Worth it?** Free, 1–5 min, low risk. Fine as an in-the-moment tool.

#### 7.4 Slow breathing (~6 breaths/min, resonance breathing) — **B** · 60%
- Increases heart-rate variability acutely; modest BP reductions in trials. AHA scientific statement gave device-guided slow breathing a IIa (reasonable) rating for BP lowering (Brook et al., *Hypertension* 2013). Meta-analysis of breathwork: small reductions in self-reported stress, anxiety and depression, with high risk of bias (Fincham et al., *Sci Rep* 2023). No device needed (4–5 s in, 5–6 s out).

#### 7.5 Expressive writing / journaling — **B−** · 50%
- Pennebaker-style writing (15–20 min on 3–4 days about stressful experiences) produces small average benefits in meta-analysis (Frattaroli, *Psychol Bull* 2006, r≈0.08). Effects vary widely; some feel worse short-term. Planning/to-do "brain dump" before bed reduced sleep-onset latency in one small study (Scullin et al., *J Exp Psychol Gen* 2018). Free.

#### 7.6 Psychological therapy (CBT etc.) — **A** for depression/anxiety
- Not a lifestyle "hack" but worth stating: CBT and related therapies have decades of RCT support. Self-guided CBT (books/apps) has moderate evidence for mild–moderate symptoms. "Feeling Good" by David Burns is among the better-studied bibliotherapy books.

## 8. Mental health and cognition

#### 8.1 Behavioural activation (scheduling valued/pleasant activities) — **A−** for depression · 75%
- A core component of CBT; meta-analyses show effects similar to full CBT (Ekers et al., *PLoS One* 2014). Practically: plan small, specific enjoyable or meaningful activities and do them regardless of motivation. Free. Underused as a self-help skill.

#### 8.2 Gratitude practices — **B−** for small mood benefits · 50%
- Meta-analyses: gratitude interventions produce small improvements in wellbeing and small reductions in depression/anxiety symptoms, often no better than other active positive-activity controls (Davis et al., *J Couns Psychol* 2016; Cregg & Cheavens, *J Happiness Stud* 2021). "Three good things" and gratitude letters are free and harmless. Don't expect life-changing effects; don't force it during depression if it feels invalidating.

#### 8.3 Kindness, volunteering, purpose — **B−** · 50%
- Acts of kindness: small positive effects on the giver's wellbeing (Curry et al., *J Exp Soc Psychol* 2018). Volunteering and purpose in life are associated with lower mortality in cohorts (confounded). Worth doing for their own sake.

#### 8.4 Learning new, demanding skills — **B−** for cognition · 50%
- Learning cognitively demanding new skills (digital photography, quilting) improved episodic memory vs social or passive activities in a small RCT (Park et al., *Psychol Sci* 2014). Education is a robust dementia protective factor at population level (Livingston et al., Lancet Commission 2024). Plausible "cognitive reserve" benefit; modest.

#### 8.5 Commercial brain-training games — **C/D** for broad benefits · 25%
- People get better at the trained tasks; transfer to everyday cognition is weak (Simons et al., *Psychol Sci Public Interest* 2016). Lumosity paid US$2 million to settle FTC deceptive-advertising charges in 2016. **Exception worth watching:** in the ACTIVE trial (n=2,802 older adults), ~10 sessions of speed-of-processing training plus boosters was associated with ~25% lower diagnosed dementia over 20 years (Coe et al., *Alzheimer's Dement TRCI* 2026) — a secondary analysis, but from a randomised trial. Memory and reasoning training did not show this. **Grade C+ for that specific training.**

#### 8.6 Music (listening, playing, singing) — **B** for mood/anxiety/pain · 60%
- Music reduced postoperative pain, anxiety and analgesic use in a meta-analysis of 73 RCTs (Hole et al., *Lancet* 2015). Music therapy shows short-term benefits for depression (Cochrane, Aalbers et al., 2017). Playing an instrument: weak far-transfer to IQ/academics in children (Sala & Gobet meta-analyses). "Mozart effect": **E**.

#### 8.7 Reading, puzzles, crosswords, games — **C** · 35%
- Associated with lower dementia risk in cohorts; reverse causation likely (early decline reduces engagement). Enjoyable, harmless.

#### 8.8 Hearing protection and hearing aids — **A−** for dementia risk factor · 70%
- **Underused.** Hearing loss is the largest single modifiable dementia risk factor at population level in the Lancet Commission model (~7% of cases; Livingston et al., *Lancet* 2024). ACHIEVE RCT: hearing intervention did not slow cognitive decline overall over 3 years, but slowed it ~48% in the higher-risk subgroup (Lin et al., *Lancet* 2023). Hearing aids clearly improve communication and social engagement. OTC hearing aids (US, since 2022) cut cost.

#### 8.9 Vision correction (glasses, cataract surgery) — **B** · 60%
- Untreated vision loss is a Lancet Commission dementia risk factor (added 2024); cataract surgery associated with lower dementia risk in cohorts; also reduces falls and car crashes. Get eyes checked.

## 9. Social connection

#### 9.1 Social relationships and integration — **A−** (strong observational, moderate causal) · 75%
- **Evidence:** Meta-analysis of 148 studies (~300,000 people): stronger social relationships associated with ~50% higher odds of survival (Holt-Lunstad et al., *PLoS Med* 2010), comparable to smoking and larger than physical inactivity in those data. Loneliness and isolation associated with ~26–32% higher mortality (Holt-Lunstad et al., *Perspect Psychol Sci* 2015). WHO's Commission on Social Connection (2025) estimated loneliness is linked to ~871,000 deaths/year. Mechanisms: stress physiology, health behaviours, help-seeking, sleep, inflammation.
- **Causal caution:** Sick or depressed people withdraw; confounding by health/SES is large. Intervention trials for loneliness show small-moderate effects, with cognitive approaches (addressing maladaptive social thinking) best (Masi et al., *Pers Soc Psychol Rev* 2011).
- **Worth it?** Yes. Probably top-5 for wellbeing and plausibly for longevity.
- **Cheapest:** Recurring, low-friction commitments beat spontaneous plans: a weekly walk with a friend, a club, a class, a team sport, volunteering, family dinner. Quality > quantity.

#### 9.2 Touch, hugs, physical affection — **B** · 55%
- Meta-analysis of 137 studies: touch interventions reduced pain, depression and anxiety in adults and benefited newborns (Packheiser et al., *Nat Hum Behav* 2024). People who received more hugs were less likely to get sick after virus exposure when stressed (Cohen et al., *Psychol Sci* 2015). Massage therapy: short-term benefits for pain and anxiety. Consent matters; touch from trusted people works best.

#### 9.3 Pets — **C+** · 40%
- AHA scientific statement: dog ownership "may be reasonable" for CV risk reduction but not proven causal (Levine et al., *Circulation* 2013). Meta-analysis: dog owners had ~24% lower all-cause mortality (Kramer et al., *Circ Cardiovasc Qual Outcomes* 2019) — healthy people are likelier to own dogs. Dogs increase walking. Pets also bring cost, allergies, fall risk for older adults and grief.
- **Worth it?** Only if you want a pet for its own sake. Don't adopt one as a health intervention.

#### 9.4 Religious/community participation — **B−** · 45%
- Service attendance associated with lower mortality in large cohorts (e.g., Li et al., *JAMA Intern Med* 2016) — likely mediated by social support and confounded. Secular equivalents (choirs, clubs, volunteering) plausibly deliver the social part.

## 10. Breathing

#### 10.1 Slow/resonance breathing — see §7.4 (**B**).

#### 10.2 Nasal breathing (at rest and light exercise) — **C** for performance · 35%
- Nose filters, humidifies and warms air, and nasal nitric oxide has plausible benefits. For people who breathe through their mouth because of nasal blockage, treating the cause (allergies, septum, polyps) is the real intervention. Claims that nasal-only breathing during exercise improves performance/"CO₂ tolerance" rest on small studies.

#### 10.3 Wim Hof / cyclic hyperventilation + breath holds — **C** for stress/immune claims · 35%; dangerous around water
- Small experiment (n=24): trained participants voluntarily activated the sympathetic nervous system and had attenuated inflammatory responses to injected endotoxin (Kox et al., *PNAS* 2014). Interesting physiology, not proof of better health or disease resistance. **Hyperventilation + breath-holding in water has caused deaths from shallow-water blackout.** Never do it in water or while driving.

#### 10.4 Breathing retraining for asthma (Buteyko, etc.) — **B−** · 50%
- Cochrane: breathing exercises may improve asthma quality of life and symptoms (low-to-moderate certainty) (Santino et al., 2020). Adjunct to, never replacement for, inhalers.

#### 10.5 Inspiratory muscle strength training (IMST) — **B−** · 50%
- **Underused/emerging.** 30 breaths/day through a high-resistance handheld trainer for 6 weeks reduced systolic BP ~9 mmHg in midlife/older adults in a small RCT (Craighead et al., *J Am Heart Assoc* 2021). Devices ~US$30–60. Awaiting larger trials.

#### 10.6 Mouth taping — **D/E** · 15% (see §24.6)

## 11. Heat and cold exposure

#### 11.1 Sauna (traditional Finnish, ~80–100 °C) — **B** · 60% for CV benefit
- **Evidence:** Finnish KIHD cohort (~2,300 middle-aged men): 4–7 sessions/week associated with ~40% lower all-cause mortality and ~50% lower fatal CVD than 1/week (Laukkanen et al., *JAMA Intern Med* 2015); also lower dementia. **Observational, one population, where sauna is cultural and near-universal (so less confounded than elsewhere, but still confounded by health).** Short-term trials show acute BP reduction, improved arterial compliance, and heart-rate responses similar to moderate exercise. Passive heating (hot-water immersion) improved vascular function in sedentary adults over 8 weeks (Brunt et al., *J Physiol* 2016).
- **Depression:** A single session of whole-body hyperthermia reduced depressive symptoms vs sham for ~6 weeks in a small RCT (Janssen et al., *JAMA Psychiatry* 2016, n≈30) — **C+**.
- **Dose:** 15–20 min, 2–4×/week in the cohort data.
- **Cheapest:** Gym/municipal pool saunas; hot baths (~40 °C) are a reasonable cheap proxy for some vascular effects.
- **Risks/contraindications:** Alcohol + sauna (deaths); unstable angina, recent MI, severe aortic stenosis, uncontrolled hypertension; pregnancy (first trimester hyperthermia); dehydration/fainting. Heat transiently reduces sperm count (reversible; Garolla et al., *Hum Reprod* 2013) — relevant if trying to conceive.
- **Infrared saunas:** Lower air temperature, less data; don't assume the Finnish findings transfer.
- **Product needed?** A home sauna (US$3,000–15,000) is a lifestyle purchase, not a health necessity.

#### 11.2 Cold showers — **B−** for sickness absence · 50%; **C** for mood/energy
- Dutch RCT (n≈3,000): ending showers with 30–90 s cold for 30 days → 29% fewer self-reported sickness-absence days, but **no reduction in days of illness** (Buijze et al., *PLoS One* 2016) — people felt ill equally often but perhaps functioned better (or reported differently; unblinded).
- Subjective energy/mood boost is real for many people acutely.
- **Risks:** Low for healthy people. Caution with heart disease, Raynaud's.

#### 11.3 Cold-water immersion / ice baths — **C** · 35% for general-health claims
- **Evidence:** Systematic review/meta-analysis: CWI reduced stress at ~12 h post-exposure and may improve sleep and quality of life, with a transient rise in inflammation; studies small and heterogeneous (Almahayni & Hammond, *PLoS One* 2025). Brown fat activation and energy expenditure increase modestly; no demonstrated meaningful weight loss.
- **Big misleading claim:** "Cold plunge raises dopamine 250%." That figure is *plasma* dopamine after **1 hour** in 14 °C water in 10 young men (Šrámek et al., *Eur J Appl Physiol* 2000). Plasma dopamine doesn't cross the blood–brain barrier and says nothing about brain dopamine or mood.
- **Harms to gains:** Cold immersion soon after strength training blunts muscle growth and strength gains (Roberts et al., *J Physiol* 2015; subsequent meta-analyses). If hypertrophy matters, wait several hours or do cold on non-lifting days.
- **Risks:** Cold shock response (gasp reflex, drowning), arrhythmias especially when face immersion + cold shock conflict ("autonomic conflict", Shattock & Tipton, *J Physiol* 2012), hypothermia. Never alone in open water.
- **Product needed?** No. A US$5,000+ chiller plunge offers no demonstrated benefit over a cold shower, cold bath with ice, or a lake with a friend.

#### 11.4 Hot baths — **B−** · 50%
- See §1.5 for sleep; Japanese cohort associated frequent hot-tub bathing with lower CVD (observational). Cheap alternative to sauna.

## 12. Posture and ergonomics

#### 12.1 "Correct posture" to prevent back/neck pain — **D** · 20%
- Systematic reviews find weak or no association between sitting/standing posture (including slouching and "text neck") and back or neck pain (e.g., Swain et al., *J Biomech* 2020; Slater et al., *JOSPT* 2019). Variety of posture and movement matters more than a single "correct" one. Fear-driven posture vigilance may worsen pain.

#### 12.2 Exercise to prevent low back pain — **A−** · 75%
- Meta-analysis of RCTs: exercise alone reduced the risk of a low back pain episode by ~35% and with education by ~45%; **back belts, shoe insoles and education alone did not work** (Steffens et al., *JAMA Intern Med* 2016).

#### 12.3 Ergonomic chairs, posture correctors, standing mats — **D** for pain prevention · 20%
- Comfort is legitimate; prevention claims are not supported. Basic setup: screen top near eye level, feet supported, keyboard near elbow height, **change position often.**

#### 12.4 20-20-20 rule / eye breaks for digital eye strain — **C** · 40%
- Blinking decreases during screen work; breaks and blinking reduce dryness/fatigue symptoms short-term in small studies. Blue-light glasses: **no benefit** for eye strain (Cochrane, Singh et al., 2023).

## 13. Foot health and barefoot activity

This section splits one popular idea into three separate claims, as requested.

#### 13.1 Barefoot / minimalist walking **for foot mechanics, strength, balance and sensory input** — **B−** · 55%
- **Mechanism:** Cushioned, rigid shoes reduce the work of intrinsic foot muscles and dampen sensory feedback from the sole. Barefoot/minimalist footwear increases muscle use and plantar sensory input.
- **Evidence:** Walking in minimalist shoes for 6 months increased intrinsic foot muscle size and foot strength substantially (~57% strength increase in one RCT; Ridge et al., *Med Sci Sports Exerc* 2019). Habitually barefoot children show better balance and jumping in cross-sectional studies (Zech et al., *Front Pediatr* 2018). Calluses protect without reducing tactile sensitivity (Holowka et al., *Nature* 2019). Foot-strengthening exercises ("short foot", toe curls) help some people with flat feet/plantar heel pain in small trials. **Unknown:** whether any of this reduces injury, falls or pain in the general population.
- **Who benefits most:** People with weak feet, those who want to strengthen feet gradually, children (safe surfaces).
- **Dose:** Start with 10–15 min/day on safe, varied surfaces (grass, sand, home floors) and increase over weeks. For running, transition over months.
- **Risks/contraindications:** **Diabetes or peripheral neuropathy — do not walk barefoot** (undetected wounds → ulcers → amputation). Rapid transition to minimalist running → metatarsal stress injuries and bone-marrow oedema (Ryan et al., *BJSM* 2014). Puncture wounds (tetanus), glass, hot surfaces. Hookworm/strongyloides in parts of the tropics. **Older adults:** being barefoot or in socks/slippers indoors is associated with markedly higher fall risk (Koepsell et al., *J Am Geriatr Soc* 2004) — firm, well-fitting shoes are safer.
- **Worth it?** Moderately, for most healthy adults. **Product needed?** No; minimalist shoes (~US$100–200) are optional.

#### 13.2 Walking barefoot **outdoors on grass/sand as "time outside"** — **B** (via the nature/activity/light bundle) · 60%
- Every benefit in §3–4 (light, activity, nature, relaxation) applies. This is the most likely explanation for why people feel better after barefoot beach or grass walks. Sand walking also costs ~1.6–2.5× more energy than firm ground.

#### 13.3 "Grounding"/"earthing" — barefoot contact **to transfer Earth's electrons and reduce inflammation** — **E** · 10%
- **The claim:** The Earth's surface carries a negative charge; skin contact lets "free electrons" flow into the body, neutralising free radicals (acting as antioxidants), reducing inflammation, thinning blood ("zeta potential"), normalising cortisol and improving sleep.
- **What the evidence looks like:** A handful of small pilot studies (often n=8–60), frequently unblinded or poorly blinded, often uncontrolled, published largely in alternative-medicine journals, and **disproportionately authored or funded by people linked to companies selling earthing products** (e.g., Chevalier et al., *J Environ Public Health* 2012 review; Ghaly & Teplitz 2004, n=12, uncontrolled cortisol study; Chevalier 2013 blood viscosity study, n=10). No large, independent, well-blinded RCT has shown clinical benefit.
- **Physics/biology problems:** Being grounded equalises your body's electrical potential with the ground; it does not deliver a meaningful stream of "antioxidant electrons" into tissues. Biological antioxidant chemistry involves specific molecular reactions (glutathione, enzymes), not free electrons from the soil. Grounding does reduce body voltage induced by nearby AC wiring — true and measurable — but there's no evidence that that voltage is harmful at household levels.
- **Why people still feel better:** Being outdoors, barefoot sensory input, relaxation, expectation/placebo, and time away from screens.
- **Worth it?** Walk barefoot for the §13.1–13.2 reasons if you enjoy it. Don't do it *for electrons*.

#### 13.4 Commercial grounding mats/sheets/patches plugged into an electrical outlet's ground — **E** · 5%
- Inherits the weak evidence of §13.3 plus removes the actual benefits (outdoors, sensory input, activity).
- **Safety concerns:** They connect you to the building's earth conductor. In a correctly wired building with a current-limiting resistor in the cord, risk is low; but if outlets are mis-wired or you touch a faulty appliance while grounded, you complete a path to earth, which is exactly what electrical safety tries to prevent. Many mats include a resistor for this reason; not all cheap ones do. Outlet "checker" products exist precisely because mis-wiring is common in older homes.
- **Advertising:** Claims to treat inflammation, autoimmune disease, insomnia or "EMF" exposure are unsupported.
- **Worth it?** No. **Cheaper alternative with more actual benefit:** a 15-minute walk outside.

#### 13.5 Footwear choices
- **Running shoes matched to pronation type do not prevent injury** (927 novice runners, Nielsen et al., *BJSM* 2014); choosing by comfort is a reasonable filter (Nigg et al., *BJSM* 2015). **C** for any specific shoe technology.
- **Custom orthotics** are generally not better than prefabricated ones for common conditions like plantar heel pain (**B**). Toe spacers: **D**.

## 14. Environmental health and air quality

Air pollution is a top-10 global risk factor for death (Global Burden of Disease). Individuals can't fix outdoor air, but indoor exposure — where people spend ~90% of their time — is partly controllable.

#### 14.1 Don't smoke / avoid second-hand smoke & vaping around others — **A** · 99%
- Stating the obvious because it dwarfs everything else here: smoking costs ~10 years of life expectancy; quitting before 40 recovers ~90% of that (Jha et al., *NEJM* 2013). If you smoke, quitting (with varenicline, NRT or e-cigarettes as a *transition* tool — Cochrane finds e-cigs help quitting) beats every other item in this document combined.

#### 14.2 Test for radon — **A** · 90%
- **Underused.** Radon is the leading cause of lung cancer in non-smokers and second overall (WHO). It's invisible, varies house-to-house, and is fixable (sub-slab ventilation). Test kits cost ~US$15–30. Especially relevant for basements/ground floors in granite/uranium-bearing regions.

#### 14.3 Carbon monoxide + smoke alarms — **A** · 95%
- Cheap, prevent deaths. Test monthly.

#### 14.4 Kitchen ventilation / gas stoves — **B** · 65%
- Gas stoves emit NO₂, and sometimes benzene, at levels that can exceed outdoor standards in poorly ventilated kitchens. Meta-analysis: gas cooking associated with ~42% higher odds of current asthma in children (Lin et al., *Int J Epidemiol* 2013). **Cheapest fix:** use the range hood (vented outside) every time, open a window, prefer back burners. An induction hot plate (~US$60) is a cheap partial switch.

#### 14.5 Portable HEPA air purifiers — **B** · 60%
- Randomised trials show lower indoor PM2.5 and small systolic BP reductions of roughly 3–4 mmHg (systematic review: Walzer et al., *Hypertension* 2020). Most useful near busy roads, in wildfire-smoke regions, for asthma/allergies, or with indoor sources. **Cheap DIY option:** Corsi-Rosenthal box (box fan + 4 MERV-13 filters, ~US$60–100), which performs comparably to commercial units in tests.
- **Don't buy:** ionisers/ozone generators (ozone is a lung irritant), "photocatalytic" gimmicks.

#### 14.6 Ventilation and CO₂ — **B** for infection transmission · 65%; **C** for cognition
- Better ventilation reduces airborne infection risk (strong mechanistic + COVID-era evidence). Cognitive effects: a well-known study found lower decision-making scores at ~1,400 ppm CO₂ (Allen et al., *Environ Health Perspect* 2016), but other controlled studies of pure CO₂ up to 5,000 ppm found little effect (e.g., Zhang et al., *Indoor Air* 2017) — likely CO₂ is a marker for other bioeffluents. Stuffy rooms do feel worse.
- **Cheapest:** Open windows briefly several times a day; bedroom window cracked. A CO₂ monitor (~US$100–200; NDIR sensor) is optional and useful for learning your space.

#### 14.7 Damp and mould — **A−** · 80%
- Dampness/mould associated with asthma, respiratory infections and symptoms (WHO 2009 guidelines). Fix leaks, ventilate bathrooms, dry laundry with ventilation.

#### 14.8 Wildfire smoke and pollution days — **A−** · 80%
- Stay indoors with filtered air, use N95/FFP2 masks outdoors during heavy smoke. Check AQI apps. Move vigorous outdoor exercise indoors on bad days.

#### 14.9 Houseplants to clean air — **E** · 5% (for air purification)
- The famous 1989 NASA study used sealed chambers. Real homes would need ~10–1,000 plants per m² to match ordinary ventilation (Cummings & Waring, *J Expo Sci Environ Epidemiol* 2020). Keep plants for mood/aesthetics (C), not air.

#### 14.10 Candles, incense, air fresheners, wood stoves — **B** for reducing · 60%
- Substantial indoor PM and VOC sources. Wood stoves are a major PM2.5 source. Use sparingly with ventilation.

#### 14.11 Plastics, microplastics, endocrine disruptors — **C** · 35% for meaningful individual benefit
- Microplastics found in human tissues; carotid plaques containing microplastics were associated with higher rates of MI, stroke or death over ~3 years (Marfella et al., *NEJM* 2024) — observational, contamination concerns raised in follow-up commentary. BPA/phthalates associated with metabolic and reproductive outcomes in cohorts. **Low-cost reasonable steps:** don't microwave food in plastic, don't put plastic in the dishwasher's hot cycle, prefer glass/steel for hot foods, filter tap water if local quality is poor. Don't panic-buy expensive "detox" products.

#### 14.12 Lead — **A** · 90%
- No safe level; homes built before ~1978 (US) may have lead paint; old pipes. Test water if in doubt; keep children away from peeling paint and contaminated soil.

## 15. Noise and sound

#### 15.1 Protect hearing (volume limits, earplugs at loud events) — **A** · 85%
- Noise-induced hearing loss is permanent and preventable; hearing loss is linked to social isolation and the largest modifiable dementia risk factor (§8.8). WHO safe-listening guidance: roughly 80 dB for up to 40 h/week (adults). Loud concerts (~100+ dB) can damage within minutes.
- **Cheapest:** Enable phone headphone-volume limits; "60/60" rule (≤60% volume, ≤60 min at a time) is a simple heuristic; foam earplugs (~US$0.20/pair) or musician's plugs (~US$20) at concerts, power tools, motorcycles.

#### 15.2 Night-time noise → sleep & cardiovascular health — **B** · 60%
- WHO Environmental Noise Guidelines (2018) recommend road traffic noise below 53 dB Lden and 45 dB at night; chronic traffic/aircraft noise is associated with hypertension and CVD, with plausible mechanisms (sleep fragmentation, stress hormones). **Cheapest:** earplugs, bedroom on the quiet side, heavy curtains, a fan for masking.

#### 15.3 Silence — **C/D** · 20% for specific claims
- The popular claim "2 hours of silence grows new brain cells" is from a mouse study (Kirste et al., *Brain Struct Funct* 2013). A small study found 2-min silent pauses lowered HR/BP more than relaxing music (Bernardi et al., *Heart* 2006, n=24). Quiet time is pleasant and may aid recovery; the neurogenesis claim is unsupported in humans.

## 16. Technology and screen use

#### 16.1 Remove phones from the bedroom / screen curfew — **B** · 60%
- Mechanisms: engagement and arousal, displacement of sleep time, notifications, light. Bedtime smartphone use associated with shorter, worse sleep, especially in adolescents; trials of evening screen restriction show modest sleep improvements. **Cheapest:** charge the phone outside the bedroom; use a US$10 alarm clock.

#### 16.2 "Digital detox" / reducing smartphone and social media use — **B−** · 50%
- **Evidence:** RCT: 4 weeks of Facebook deactivation increased subjective wellbeing by ~0.09 SD (small) and reduced political polarisation (Allcott et al., *Am Econ Rev* 2020). RCT (n≈470): blocking mobile internet on smartphones for 2 weeks improved sustained attention, mental health and wellbeing, with 91% improving on at least one measure (Castelo et al., *PNAS Nexus* 2025) — participants knew what they were doing (no blinding), and the dramatic press claims ("better than antidepressants", "reverses 10 years of attention decline") come from comparisons across different studies and should be discounted. Systematic reviews of digital detox overall: mixed, small effects (Radtke et al., *Mob Media Commun* 2022). The adolescent social-media/mental-health debate remains genuinely contested (associations are small on average; causal evidence emerging but disputed).
- **Practical:** Turn off non-essential notifications (small RCT: reduced inattention and stress, Kushlev et al., CHI 2016); greyscale mode; app time limits; phone out of sight during focused work.
- **Overhyped:** "Dopamine detox" — the behaviour (reducing high-stimulation activities) can help attention; the neurochemical framing (**E**) is wrong — you don't deplete or reset dopamine by avoiding phones.

#### 16.3 Blue light — split
- **Blue-blocking glasses for eye strain:** **E** · 5%. Cochrane: no short-term benefit for eye strain; screens don't damage retinas at normal use (Singh et al., 2023).
- **Blue-blocking glasses for sleep:** **C** · 30%. Mixed small trials; some benefit in people with insomnia/delayed phase using strongly tinted (amber) lenses in evenings. Clear "blue light" lenses do little.
- **Night Shift / Night Mode:** **C/D**. One study found no improvement in sleep from Night Shift alone (Duraccio et al., *Sleep Health* 2021). Dimming the screen and ending use earlier matter more than hue.
- **Magnitude check:** An e-reader used for 4 h before bed delayed melatonin by ~1.5 h and delayed sleep onset ~10 min vs a printed book (Chang et al., *PNAS* 2015) — real but modest, under extreme exposure.

#### 16.4 Multitasking and task-switching — **B** · 60%
- Switching between tasks has measurable time/accuracy costs; heavy media multitaskers perform worse on some attention tasks (Ophir et al., *PNAS* 2009; mixed replications). Batch messages; single-task demanding work.

#### 16.5 "The mere presence of your phone reduces cognitive capacity" — **D** · 20%
- Original "brain drain" study (Ward et al., 2017) failed to replicate in larger preregistered attempts. Phone-away still makes sense to reduce interruptions.

## 17. Hormones and reproductive health

#### 17.1 Testosterone (men) — lifestyle factors **A−** · 75%
- **What moves it:** Sleep restriction lowers testosterone ~10–15% (Leproult & Van Cauter, *JAMA* 2011). Weight loss in men with obesity raises it meaningfully (Corona et al., *Eur J Endocrinol* 2013). Heavy alcohol lowers it. Exercise helps modestly via body composition.
- **"T-boosting" supplements:** **E** · 5%. A review of top-selling products found only about a quarter had any data supporting their claims, and some ingredients had data showing *lower* testosterone (Clemesha et al., *World J Mens Health* 2020). Tongkat ali/fenugreek: small trials, C at best.
- **Acute post-workout hormone spikes do not drive muscle growth** (West et al., *J Appl Physiol* 2010).

#### 17.2 "Adrenal fatigue" — **E** · 2%
- Not a recognised diagnosis; systematic review found no substantiation (Cadegiani & Kater, *BMC Endocr Disord* 2016). Real conditions (adrenal insufficiency, depression, sleep apnoea, thyroid disease, anaemia) should be tested for instead. "Cortisol face", "cortisol belly" (social media trends): **E**.

#### 17.3 Cycle syncing (training/eating by menstrual phase) — **D** · 15%
- Meta-analysis: menstrual-cycle phase has at most trivial average effects on exercise performance, with large individual variation (McNulty et al., *Sports Med* 2020). Adjust to how you feel; a rigid phase-based plan isn't evidence-based. "Seed cycling": **E**.

#### 17.4 Pre-conception and pregnancy — **A**
- Folic acid (400 µg/day) before conception and early pregnancy prevents neural-tube defects. Stopping smoking/alcohol; exercise during pregnancy is safe and beneficial for most (ACOG). Heat exposure/hot tubs in early pregnancy: avoid prolonged hyperthermia.

#### 17.5 Male fertility — **B**
- Avoid smoking, heavy alcohol, anabolic steroids and *exogenous testosterone* (it suppresses sperm production), prolonged scrotal heat (saunas, laptops on lap, hot tubs — reversible effects). Mediterranean-style diet associated with better semen quality.

#### 17.6 Menopause
- Resistance training and weight-bearing exercise protect bone and muscle (**A**). Hormone therapy is the most effective treatment for vasomotor symptoms and a medical decision (**A**). CBT has RCT evidence for hot-flush bother and sleep (**B**). Most menopause supplements: **D**.

## 18. Immune health

"Boosting" the immune system is a marketing phrase; you want a *well-regulated* one. The effective levers are vaccination, sleep, activity, not smoking, adequate nutrition and hygiene.

#### 18.1 Vaccinations (flu, COVID per guidance, tetanus, pneumococcal, shingles, HPV, etc.) — **A** · 95%
- Among the best-evidenced health interventions in existence.
- **Emerging bonus:** A natural experiment in Wales (eligibility cut-off by exact birth date, ~280,000 adults) found the live shingles vaccine reduced new dementia diagnoses by ~20% over 7 years (Eyting, Xie et al., *Nature* 2025), with follow-up work in *Cell* (2025). Strong quasi-experimental design; mechanism not settled; newer recombinant vaccine (Shingrix) appears similar in observational data. **B** for the dementia effect — but shingles vaccination is already recommended for other reasons.

#### 18.2 Sleep and immunity — **A−** · 80%
- ≤6 h sleep → ~4× higher cold incidence after viral challenge (Prather et al., *Sleep* 2015); sleep deprivation around vaccination reduces antibody response.

#### 18.3 Handwashing — **A** · 85%
- Meta-analysis: hand hygiene reduced GI illness ~31% and respiratory illness ~21% in community settings (Aiello et al., *Am J Public Health* 2008). Soap and water; hand sanitiser when unavailable.

#### 18.4 Regular moderate exercise — **B** · 60%
- Associated with fewer upper respiratory infections and better vaccine responses; the "open window" theory of post-exercise immunosuppression is now contested.

#### 18.5 Supplements for colds
- **Vitamin C:** does not prevent colds in the general population; ~8% shorter duration if taken regularly; ~50% fewer colds in people under extreme physical stress (marathon runners, soldiers) (Hemilä & Chalker, Cochrane 2013). **B−** for those niches, **D** for "boosting".
- **Zinc lozenges started within 24 h:** possibly shorten colds; certainty low and inconsistent (Cochrane 2024). **C**. Side effects: nausea, taste disturbance; zinc nasal sprays caused permanent smell loss — avoid.
- **Vitamin D:** modest (~8–12%) reduction in acute respiratory infections, mainly in deficient people taking daily doses (Martineau et al., *BMJ* 2017; Jolliffe et al., *Lancet Diabetes Endocrinol* 2021). **B−**.
- **Echinacea, elderberry, colloidal silver, "immune shots":** **D/E**. Colloidal silver can permanently turn skin blue-grey (argyria).
- **Probiotics:** **B** for antibiotic-associated diarrhoea; **D** for general immunity.

## 19. Cardiovascular health

CVD remains the leading cause of death globally. The AHA's **Life's Essential 8** (diet, activity, nicotine, sleep, weight, lipids, glucose, BP) is a good checklist (Lloyd-Jones et al., *Circulation* 2022).

#### 19.1 Know and control blood pressure — **A** · 95%
- Every 10 mmHg systolic reduction lowers major CV events ~20% (Ettehad et al., *Lancet* 2016). Hypertension is often silent. **Underused tool:** a validated home BP monitor (~US$30–60; check validatebp.org) — measure seated, arm supported, after 5 min rest, average several readings.
- **Lifestyle BP levers (approximate systolic reductions in hypertensive people):** weight loss ~1 mmHg per kg; DASH diet ~11; sodium reduction ~5–6; potassium-rich food/salt substitute ~4–5; aerobic exercise ~5–8; isometric training ~8; alcohol reduction ~4. These add up.

#### 19.2 Know your lipids (including a one-off Lp(a)) — **A** for LDL · 90%
- LDL cholesterol is causal for atherosclerosis (genetic, trial and epidemiological evidence). Statins have large RCT evidence for people at elevated risk. Lipoprotein(a) is ~90% genetic, affects ~20% of people, and many guidelines (e.g., ESC/EAS 2019; National Lipid Association) now recommend measuring it **once in a lifetime** — cheap, informative about family risk.

#### 19.3 Oral health (brushing, flossing/interdental cleaning, dental visits) — **B** for gum health · 65%; **C** that it prevents heart disease
- Cochrane: flossing plus brushing reduces gingivitis (low-certainty evidence) (Worthington et al., 2019). Periodontitis is associated with CVD and diabetes — causality uncertain. Brushing twice daily with fluoride toothpaste is **A** for caries.

#### 19.4 Everything in §2 (exercise), §5 (diet), §1 (sleep), §9 (social), §14 (smoking/air) applies.

## 20. Brain health

The 2024 *Lancet* Commission estimated ~45% of dementia cases are potentially attributable to 14 modifiable risk factors: less education, hearing loss, high LDL, depression, traumatic brain injury, physical inactivity, diabetes, smoking, hypertension, obesity, excess alcohol, social isolation, air pollution and untreated vision loss (Livingston et al., *Lancet* 2024). These are population-attributable estimates (not guarantees for individuals), and they assume causality.

#### 20.1 Multidomain lifestyle interventions — **B** · 60%
- FINGER trial (Finland, at-risk older adults): 2 years of diet + exercise + cognitive training + vascular risk management improved cognitive test scores modestly vs control (Ngandu et al., *Lancet* 2015). The US POINTER trial (2025) reported that a structured multidomain programme produced modestly better cognitive outcomes than a self-guided one over 2 years. Benefits are real but modest; they matter at population scale.

#### 20.2 Blood pressure control — **A−** for cognitive impairment · 75%
- SPRINT MIND: intensive BP control (<120 systolic) reduced mild cognitive impairment (Williamson et al., *JAMA* 2019).

#### 20.3 Head protection — **A** · 90%
- Helmets for cycling/skiing/motorbikes; seatbelts; fall-proofing homes. TBI is a dementia risk factor and a direct cause of disability.

#### 20.4 Exercise and the brain — **B** · 60%
- Aerobic exercise increased hippocampal volume ~2% in older adults over a year (Erickson et al., *PNAS* 2011); meta-analyses show small cognitive improvements, largest for executive function. Acute exercise produces small short-term cognitive boosts.

#### 20.5 Creatine for cognition — **C+** · 40%
- Meta-analyses suggest small memory improvements, larger in older adults and vegetarians, and under sleep deprivation (e.g., Prokopidis et al., *Nutr Rev* 2023). Studies small and heterogeneous. Cheap and safe (see §24).

#### 20.6 Omega-3, ginkgo, "nootropic stacks" for cognition — **D** · 15%
- Large trials (e.g., VITAL, GEM for ginkgo) show no meaningful cognitive benefit in healthy older adults. Multivitamins: COSMOS reported a small cognitive benefit (Baker et al., *Am J Clin Nutr* 2023) — **C**, awaiting confirmation.

## 21. Healthy ageing and longevity

#### 21.1 The boring core — **A**
- Li et al. (*Circulation* 2018): never smoking, BMI 18.5–24.9, ≥30 min/day moderate–vigorous activity, moderate alcohol (in that paper; newer data favour less), and a high-quality diet → ~14 extra life-years (women) and ~12 (men) at age 50 vs none of these. Observational, but each component has independent causal support.

#### 21.2 Maintain muscle and strength — **A** · 85%
- Grip strength and leg strength predict mortality and disability (Leong et al., *Lancet* 2015, PURE). Resistance training works at every age. Protein ~1.0–1.2 g/kg in older adults.

#### 21.3 Fall prevention — **A** · 85%
- Balance/strength training, tai chi (§2.10), home hazard removal (especially for those with previous falls — Cochrane evidence), vision checks, medication review (sedatives), proper footwear.

#### 21.4 Calorie restriction — **B** for cardiometabolic markers · 60%; **C** for human longevity
- CALERIE-2: ~12% calorie restriction for 2 years in healthy non-obese adults improved cardiometabolic risk factors (Kraus et al., *Lancet Diabetes Endocrinol* 2019) and modestly slowed the DunedinPACE epigenetic pace-of-aging measure (Waziry et al., *Nat Aging* 2023). Loss of bone/muscle is a concern; not advised for older/frail people. Lifespan extension in humans unproven.

#### 21.5 Epigenetic clocks, telomere tests, "biological age" tests — **C** as research tools; **D/E** as consumer products · 20%
- Clocks predict mortality at population level but individual consumer results have substantial technical noise (different results from the same sample are documented; Higgins-Chen et al., *Nat Aging* 2022 on reliability). Changing a clock score ≠ proven change in lifespan. Telomere length tests: poor individual predictive value. **Not worth paying for.**

#### 21.6 "Longevity drugs/supplements" (NMN, NR, resveratrol, rapamycin, metformin) — **D** for healthy people · 10–20%
- NAD⁺ precursors raise blood NAD⁺ but have shown little meaningful functional benefit in human trials. Resveratrol: negative/null human data. Metformin: TAME trial (longevity) has not reported; metformin *blunts* some benefits of exercise training in older adults (Konopka et al., *Aging Cell* 2019; Walton et al., *Aging Cell* 2019). Rapamycin: promising animal data; small human trials; immunosuppressive risks; prescription-only. **See §27 (emerging).**

#### 21.7 Blue Zones — **C** as evidence
- The "Blue Zones" lifestyle (plant-heavy diet, daily movement, social bonds) is consistent with good evidence, but some Blue Zone longevity claims rest on age records with documented errors (e.g., Newman, working paper 2019/2024 on record validity). Take the lifestyle, not the mythology.

#### 21.8 Purpose, optimism, conscientiousness — **B−** (observational) · 45%
- Associated with lower mortality (e.g., optimism: Rozanski et al., *JAMA Netw Open* 2019 meta-analysis). Largely via behaviour and health. Cultivating purpose (volunteering, meaningful roles) is reasonable.

#### 21.9 Injury prevention — **A**
- Seatbelts, no phone while driving, helmets, pool/water safety, safe medicine storage. Accidental injury is the leading cause of death in people under ~45 in many countries.

#### 21.10 Cancer screening (per national guidance) — **A** for colorectal, cervical, breast (age-appropriate), lung (heavy smokers)
- Colorectal screening (FIT or colonoscopy) is one of the best-evidenced preventive actions. Follow national guidelines; age thresholds are dropping (e.g., US colorectal from 45).

#### 21.11 Whole-body MRI scans for healthy people — **D** · 10%
- No evidence that they reduce mortality; high rate of incidental findings leading to anxiety, invasive follow-ups and costs. The American College of Radiology does not recommend them for asymptomatic people (2023 statement). US$1,000–2,500.

## 22. Work productivity and concentration

#### 22.1 Sleep — **A** (the dominant factor). 17–19 h awake impairs performance similarly to a blood alcohol concentration of ~0.05% (Williamson & Feyer, *Occup Environ Med* 2000).

#### 22.2 Caffeine (strategic) — **A** for acute alertness · 90%
- ~1–3 mg/kg improves reaction time, vigilance and endurance. Tolerance develops. Keep total ≤400 mg/day for most adults (≤200 mg in pregnancy); respect the sleep curfew (§1.6).
- **Caffeine + L-theanine:** small studies show improved attention vs caffeine alone (**C**). Green tea gives both.

#### 22.3 Microbreaks — **B−** · 50%
- Meta-analysis: microbreaks (<10 min) improved vigour and reduced fatigue; performance effects were small and limited to some task types (Albulescu et al., *PLoS One* 2022). Take a short walk or look out a window; a 40-s "green view" break study (Lee et al., 2015) is small and not well replicated.

#### 22.4 Single-tasking, notifications off, phone out of reach — **B−** · 55% (see §16)

#### 22.5 Implementation intentions ("If it's 7 am Monday, I will…") — **A−** for goal attainment · 75%
- Meta-analysis of 94 studies: medium-to-large effect on goal achievement (d≈0.65) (Gollwitzer & Sheeran, *Adv Exp Soc Psychol* 2006). This is the most useful tool for making *everything else in this document* actually happen.

#### 22.6 Habit formation — **B** · 65%
- Median ~66 days (range 18–254) for a behaviour to become automatic (Lally et al., *Eur J Soc Psychol* 2010); a 2024 review found median ~59–66 days (Singh et al., *Healthcare*). Anchor new habits to existing cues ("after I pour coffee, I'll walk outside for 10 min"). Missing a day doesn't reset progress.

#### 22.7 Exercise before cognitive work — **B−** · 50%
- Acute exercise produces small improvements in cognitive performance for ~1–2 h (Chang et al., *Brain Res* 2012 meta-analysis). A brisk 10-min walk before a demanding task is reasonable.

#### 22.8 Pomodoro technique, "deep work", productivity systems — **C** · 35%
- Plausible, little rigorous research. Use what works; the main active ingredients are breaking work into defined chunks and protecting attention.

#### 22.9 "Nootropics" (racetams, stacks), microdosing psychedelics — **D** · 10%
- Microdosing: placebo-controlled self-blinding study found benefits equivalent to placebo (Szigeti et al., *eLife* 2021). Legal and safety issues.

## 23. Recovery

#### 23.1 Sleep — **A**. Nothing else in recovery comes close.

#### 23.2 Adequate protein and energy — **A** (§5.5). Chronic under-fuelling in athletes (RED-S) impairs recovery, bone and hormones.

#### 23.3 Periodisation / rest days / deloads — **B** · 65%. Progressive load with planned lighter periods reduces overtraining symptoms; evidence mostly from sports science.

#### 23.4 Active recovery (light movement) — **C+** · 45%. Modest effects on perceived soreness; good for mood.

#### 23.5 Cold-water immersion for recovery — **B** for short-term soreness · 60%; **harmful for hypertrophy** if done right after lifting (§11.3). Useful in tournaments/multi-day events.

#### 23.6 Massage — **B−** · 55% for perceived soreness/fatigue; minimal effect on performance measures. Foam rolling ~similar, cheaper.

#### 23.7 Compression garments — **C** · 35%. Small effects on perceived soreness.

#### 23.8 Percussion guns, cryotherapy chambers, "recovery" supplements (BCAAs) — **D** · 15%
- Whole-body cryotherapy (−110 °C chambers): little evidence beyond ice-bath equivalents; frostbite and at least one reported death. BCAAs are pointless if total protein is adequate.

#### 23.9 Heart-rate variability (HRV) monitoring for training decisions — **C** · 40%
- HRV-guided training shows small benefits in some athlete trials; for general people, day-to-day HRV noise is high. Subjective readiness ("how do I feel?") performs comparably in many studies.

## 24. Low-cost "biohacks" (the ones that are actually worth it — and the ones that aren't)

#### 24.1 Creatine monohydrate — **A** for strength/lean mass with training · 85%; **C+** for cognition
- **What:** 3–5 g/day, any time. No loading needed.
- **Evidence:** Adds strength and lean mass gains when combined with resistance training, including in older adults (e.g., Chilibeck et al., *Open Access J Sports Med* 2017 meta-analysis). Among the most researched and safest supplements. Raises blood creatinine (a lab marker, not kidney damage) — tell your doctor you take it. Some water weight (~1 kg).
- **Worth it?** Yes if you lift, are older, or vegetarian. Plain monohydrate ~US$0.10–0.20/day; premium forms (HCl, "buffered") add nothing.

#### 24.2 Vitamin D (if deficient or low sun) — **A** to correct deficiency · 80%; **D** for disease prevention in replete people
- VITAL (25,871 adults, 2,000 IU/day): no reduction in cancer or CVD (Manson et al., *NEJM* 2019) or fractures (LeBoff et al., *NEJM* 2022); a ~22% reduction in autoimmune disease was reported (Hahn et al., *BMJ* 2022 — **B−**). Useful in winter at high latitudes, dark skin + low sun, housebound elderly. Typical 800–2,000 IU/day; don't megadose.

#### 24.3 Protein powder, oats, beans, frozen vegetables — "food biohacks" — **A** via §5.

#### 24.4 A home BP monitor — **A** (§19.1). Best-value health device most people don't own.

#### 24.5 Earplugs + eye mask — **B** · 60%. ~US$10 total. Improves sleep in noisy/light environments; hospital ICU studies show improved sleep (Cochrane, low certainty).

#### 24.6 Mouth taping — **D/E** · 10%
- Systematic review of 10 studies (213 patients), all rated poor quality: evidence too limited to recommend; potential asphyxiation risk if the nose is blocked; may mask untreated sleep apnoea (Rhee et al., *PLoS One* 2025). If you mouth-breathe at night, investigate nasal obstruction and sleep apnoea.

#### 24.7 Stair climbing, loaded carries, farmer's walks — **B** (§2.6). Free.

#### 24.8 Salt substitute — **A** for those eligible (§5.11). Few dollars.

#### 24.9 Light box in winter — **A** for seasonal depression, **B** as adjunct in non-seasonal depression (§3.1). ~US$50–100.

#### 24.10 Blue-light glasses, grounding mats, hydrogen water, ionised bracelets, EMF shields, salt lamps — **E**. See §26.

#### 24.11 Apple cider vinegar, cinnamon, berberine — **C**
- Vinegar: small glucose blunting (§6.3). Cinnamon: inconsistent. Berberine ("nature's Ozempic"): modest glucose/lipid lowering in trials (mostly Chinese, variable quality), GI side effects and drug interactions (CYP enzymes); **nothing like** GLP-1 drug effect sizes.

#### 24.12 Omega-3 supplements — **B−** for specific groups, **D** for general health
- VITAL: no reduction in major CV events or cancer in the general population. REDUCE-IT (high-dose purified EPA in high-risk people on statins) showed 25% event reduction, debated because the mineral-oil placebo may have raised risk in controls. High-dose omega-3 increases atrial fibrillation risk (Gencer et al., *Circulation* 2021). Eat fish instead unless your doctor prescribes it for triglycerides.

#### 24.13 Multivitamins — **D** for healthy people · 15%
- USPSTF (2022): insufficient evidence for benefit in CVD/cancer prevention; recommends *against* beta-carotene (lung cancer risk in smokers) and vitamin E. Exceptions: pregnancy (folate), restricted diets, older adults with poor intake, B12 for vegans.

## 29. Top 10 almost everyone should consider

1. **Don't smoke** (or quit) — the largest single modifiable risk.
2. **Walk ~7,000+ steps/day** — biggest bang for effort, especially from a low base.
3. **Strength train twice a week** — muscle is a longevity and metabolic organ.
4. **Sleep 7–9 h at consistent times** — fix wake time first.
5. **Drink less alcohol** — sleep, cancer, BP, mood improve.
6. **Eat mostly whole foods: plants, legumes, fibre, adequate protein; few sugary drinks/UPF.**
7. **Know your blood pressure (home cuff) and lipids** — treat if high.
8. **Invest in relationships** — recurring social commitments.
9. **Daylight in the day, dim light at night.**
10. **Stay up to date with vaccinations and cancer screening.**

## 30. Top 10 underused discoveries

1. **Isometric wall sits for blood pressure** — the largest BP drop of any exercise type in a 270-trial network meta-analysis.
2. **Salt substitutes (potassium-enriched)** — cut stroke and death in a 21,000-person RCT (check kidney/medications first).
3. **Radon testing** — a US$20 kit for the #2 cause of lung cancer.
4. **Outdoor time for children's myopia** — ~2 h/day, backed by RCTs.
5. **CBT-I instead of sleeping pills** — first-line, durable, often free as an app.
6. **Sleep regularity over sleep duration** — consistency is underrated.
7. **Walking for 2–15 min after meals** — measurable glucose reduction.
8. **Hearing protection and treating hearing loss** — top modifiable dementia factor.
9. **Implementation intentions** — "When X, I will Y" roughly doubles follow-through on goals.
10. **Shingles vaccination** — recommended anyway; natural-experiment data suggest ~20% fewer dementia diagnoses.

*Honourable mentions:* one-time Lp(a) test, exercise snacks, creatine for older adults, balance training, Corsi-Rosenthal box during smoke season, behavioural activation.

2. **Stair/hill sprint "exercise snack"** (1–2 min, a few times daily).
3. **Wall sit** (2 min × 2 on most days — full protocol is 4 × 2 min).
4. **Step outside for morning light** (5 min is a start; 10–30 is better).
5. **Cyclic sighing / slow breathing** (5 min).
6. **Measure your blood pressure** (5 min, once a week).
7. **Set up phone controls**: notifications off, bedtime mode, app limits (5 min once).
8. **Send a message or make a call to someone you care about.**
9. **Write an implementation intention** for one habit ("After I brush my teeth at night, I will…").
10. **Single-leg balance while brushing teeth** / 5-min mobility routine.

## 33. Top 10 questionable wellness trends — what the evidence actually says

1. **Grounding/earthing mats** — Mechanism implausible; research small and conflicted. The outdoor walk is the active ingredient.
2. **Cold plunges for dopamine/fat loss** — Short-term mood lift is real; "250% dopamine" misrepresents a plasma measure; fat loss negligible; blunts muscle gains after lifting.
3. **Mouth taping** — Poor-quality studies; can mask sleep apnoea; asphyxiation risk with blocked nose.
4. **Blue-light glasses** — No effect on eye strain; only amber lenses in the evening may help some delayed sleepers.
5. **Red-light panels for everything** — Real niche medical uses; consumer claims (fat loss, testosterone, "mitochondrial boosting") far exceed evidence.
6. **CGMs for healthy people** — Normal glucose excursions pathologised; no outcome benefit.
7. **NAD⁺/NMN and longevity supplements** — Raise biomarkers, not proven to improve health or lifespan.
8. **Biological-age tests** — Noisy, not validated as individual targets.
9. **Detoxes, greens powders, alkaline/hydrogen water** — Your liver and kidneys do the detoxing; greens powders are expensive multivitamins.
10. **"Dopamine detox", "adrenal fatigue", "cortisol face"** — Useful behaviour wrapped in wrong neurochemistry, or conditions that don't exist.
